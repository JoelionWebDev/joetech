Imagine opening your website one morning and finding it gone. The homepage shows an error, the pages are blank, or the whole site has been replaced with something you did not put there. Your customers are trying to reach you, your orders are stuck, and you have no copy of the site to restore.

This happens more often than most business owners expect. Websites break during updates, hosting accounts run into problems, and attackers target small business sites because they expect nobody to be watching. The businesses that recover quickly are the ones that had a backup. A backup plan is not complicated, and it does not need expensive tools.

## What a backup really is

A backup is a complete copy of your website stored somewhere separate from the website itself. That last part matters most. A copy stored on the same server as your website is not a real backup, because the same problem that takes down your site can take down the copy too.

A full backup has two parts. The first is your files: the design, the images, the pages, and the code that makes the site work. The second is your database, which holds your content, your customer information, your orders, and your settings. Backing up only one part leaves you with half a website, so always check that a backup tool covers both before you rely on it.

## Decide what to back up and how often

Not every part of your website changes at the same speed. Your content changes most: blog posts, prices, orders, and enquiries change daily or weekly for an active business. Back up your database at least once a week, and daily if your site takes orders or bookings every day.

Your files change less often: your design, images, and pages change when you update the site or change your branding. A weekly or fortnightly backup is enough, plus a fresh one after any major change or new product photos.

Write your schedule down: what is backed up, how often, and where it is stored. Share it with anyone who works on your website.

## Choose a backup method that suits your site

There are three common ways to back up a website. The simplest is your hosting company's backup service. Many hosts take automatic backups, sometimes daily. Before you rely on it, check how long backups are kept and whether you can download a copy yourself. If the only copy lives on your host's server, you do not truly control it.

The second method is a backup plugin built for your website platform. WordPress sites have several well-known backup plugins that copy your files and database on a schedule and send them to cloud storage. If your site runs on WordPress, a backup plugin is usually the best option for a small business.

The third method is a manual backup, where you or your developer downloads the files and exports the database by hand. Even if you use an automatic tool, take a manual backup before any major change, such as a redesign or a move to a new host.

## Store your backups in more than one place

A backup is only as safe as where it is stored. The golden rule is simple: keep at least one copy somewhere completely separate from your website's server. Cloud storage is the most practical option for most small businesses. Services such as Google Drive or Dropbox can hold your backup files safely, and you can reach them from anywhere. If your website host has a serious problem, your backups are unaffected, because they live with a different provider.

An external hard drive is a useful extra copy, but not your only one: a drive in the same office is vulnerable to the same theft, fire, or flood. Whatever storage you choose, organise it clearly. Name each backup file with the date it was taken, such as website-backup-2026-11-05, so you can find the right one without guessing. Keep a few weeks of backups, not just the latest one: if a problem went unnoticed for a week, the latest backup may contain it too.

Protect your backups too: store them in an account with a strong password and two-factor authentication, and do not share the login casually.

## Test your backup before you need it

This is the step almost everyone skips, and it is the step that matters most. A backup you have never tested is a hope, not a plan. Testing does not mean breaking your live website. It means checking, at least once every few months, that a backup file can actually be used to restore a site. The simplest test is to restore the backup to a test location, such as a staging site or a local copy on a computer, and check that the pages load, the images appear, and the content is complete.

If you work with a developer, ask them to do a test restore and show you the result. It should take less than an hour, and it will reveal problems you would never find otherwise: a backup that stopped halfway, an empty database file, or a tool backing up the wrong folder. If a test fails, fix the problem immediately, while you still have time.

## What to do when something goes wrong

Even with a good plan, a broken website is stressful. Having a recovery routine written down in advance makes it far less so. First, do not panic or change things at random. Note the error message, which pages are affected, and when you first noticed it.

Second, contact your hosting company or your developer and describe the problem clearly. They can often tell quickly whether it is a hosting issue, a broken update, or something worse. Third, if the site cannot be fixed in place, restore from your most recent backup that was taken before the problem appeared. This is why keeping several weeks of backups matters.

Fourth, find out what caused the problem and fix the cause, not just the symptom. Finally, take a fresh backup as soon as the site is healthy.

## A backup checklist for your business

Use this list to set up or review your backup plan:

- Do you back up both your files and your database?
- Is there a written schedule for how often each is backed up?
- Is at least one copy stored away from your website's server?
- Are backup files named with dates, and do you keep several weeks of them?
- Have you tested a restore in the last few months?
- Does someone other than you know where the backups are and how to use them?
- Is there a fresh backup from before your last major website change?

If you answered no to any of these, that item is your next job. Each item you fix makes your business safer.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How often should I back up my website?**

It depends on how often your site changes. A site that takes daily orders should back up its database daily; a simple brochure site can back up weekly. As a minimum, back up fully at least once a week, plus before any major change.

**My hosting company says it backs up my site. Is that enough?**

It is a good start, but check how often backups run, how long they are kept, and whether you can download a copy yourself. Many businesses discover too late that their host kept only a few days of backups.

**What is the difference between a backup and website security?**

Security tries to stop problems from happening. A backup is what saves you when security fails or something breaks by accident. You need both: security without a backup leaves you nothing to restore, and a backup without security means the same problem keeps coming back.

**How much storage do I need for backups?**

It depends on your images and videos. Most small business sites need far less storage than their owners expect: check the size of one full backup, then multiply by the copies you want to keep.

## Backups are how businesses survive bad days

Every website will have a bad day eventually. The businesses that treat it as a small inconvenience are the ones that prepared in advance. A backup plan takes a little time to set up and almost no time to maintain, but it is the difference between a few hours of restoration and weeks of rebuilding. Set up your schedule, store a copy away from your server, and test that it works.
