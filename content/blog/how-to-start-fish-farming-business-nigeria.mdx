Fish is one of the most eaten proteins in Nigeria. From pepper soup and catfish barbecue joints to home cooking and party catering, demand for fresh fish — especially catfish — is steady all year round in Lagos, Abuja, Port Harcourt, Ibadan, Enugu, Kano and almost every town in between. That steady demand is why fish farming remains one of the most popular agribusinesses in the country.

But fish farming is not a get-rich-quick scheme. Fish eat every day, water must be managed, and a single mistake in stocking or feeding can wipe out a whole pond. The farmers who succeed learn the basics, start at a size they can manage, keep proper records and sell deliberately. This guide covers the full journey — choosing your system, setting up, stocking, feeding, managing water, costing and selling — so you can start small and grow on real profits rather than guesswork.

## Step 1: Learn the Basics and Study Your Local Market

Before you dig a pond or buy a fingerling, learn how fish farming works. Visit two or three working farms in your area and ask the owners honest questions: what system do they use, what does feed cost each month, how long does a batch take to reach market size, and what problems do they face? An afternoon on a real farm teaches more than a week of reading.

Learn the species first. Catfish is the most common choice for beginners because it grows relatively fast, tolerates the warm climate well, and has a ready market almost everywhere. Tilapia is another option, but it needs different management and is more sensitive to cold and overcrowding. For your first batch, catfish is usually the safer choice — start with what your local market already buys.

Study your local buyers before you stock. Barbecue and pepper soup spots buy daily in small quantities, market women buy in bulk on market days, households buy for the weekend, and hotels, restaurants and caterers want a steady supply. Walk through your nearest fish market, ask sellers what sizes move fastest, and note the prices per kilogram — a kilogram of table-size catfish commonly sells for roughly ₦2,500 to ₦4,500 depending on your city and the season, often higher in Lagos and Abuja. Knowing your local price before you start is essential, because it decides whether your numbers work at all.

Also decide your goal for the first cycle. A realistic first target is a small pond or tank producing a few hundred fish, sold over several weeks. That scale lets you learn feeding, water management and selling without risking a large sum. You can expand after one successful cycle, using what you learned and what you earned.

## Step 2: Choose Your Farming System

There are three main systems for small-scale fish farming in Nigeria, and your choice depends on your land, your budget and your water supply.

Earthen ponds are dug into the ground and are the traditional choice. They are cheap to build if you have land, the soil helps to keep the water stable, and fish often grow well in them. But they need enough land, a reliable water source, and good soil that holds water — sandy soil leaks. They are also harder to harvest completely and harder to protect from flooding during heavy rains.

Concrete ponds are built above or partly below ground with blocks and cement. They are durable, easy to clean, easy to harvest, and work well in a small compound or backyard. They cost more to build than an earthen pond, and the water in them can heat up quickly in direct sun, so shade or a cover helps. For many urban and semi-urban beginners, a concrete pond is the most practical option.

Tarpaulin or plastic tanks are the fastest way to start. A collapsible tarpaulin pond can be set up in a backyard, a rented space or even a large veranda, and it needs no construction skills. The cost is lower than concrete, and you can start with one or two tanks. The downside is that tarpaulin tanks hold fewer fish, the material can tear or wear out, and water temperature changes faster than in a bigger pond.

Whatever system you choose, plan drainage and water supply from the start: a way to bring in fresh water regularly and a way to drain the pond for harvest or cleaning. A borehole is the most reliable source, but many farmers use a good well or treated tap water left to stand so the chlorine clears. Never stock fish in water straight from a chlorinated tap — let it sit for a day or two first.

## Step 3: Set Up Your Pond and Prepare the Water

Location matters. Choose a spot that is accessible for feeding, harvesting and selling, close to your water source, and not prone to flooding. If you are digging an earthen pond, pick ground that is slightly sloped so water can drain by gravity, and avoid areas where chemicals, sewage or farm run-off can flow in. For concrete and tarpaulin ponds, a flat, shaded spot in your compound is usually best.

Size your first pond to your budget and your ability to feed the fish. A common beginner size is a concrete pond of about 3 metres by 2 metres, or a tarpaulin tank of about 2,000 to 3,000 litres. Stocking density matters more than pond size: overcrowded fish grow slowly, fall sick easily and eat more feed per kilogram of growth. A widely used guide for catfish is roughly 50 to 100 fish per cubic metre for a beginner. When in doubt, stock fewer fish — a pond with fewer healthy fish that reach market size earns more than a crowded pond full of stunted fish.

Prepare the pond before stocking. For a new concrete pond, fill it with water, leave it for a few days, then drain and refill to remove the chemicals that leach from fresh cement. For an earthen pond, clear weeds, level the bottom, and fill it gradually, watching for leaks. For a tarpaulin tank, check for sharp objects underneath, fill it, and let the water stand for a day or two. Some farmers add a small amount of salt or use recommended pond treatments, but keep it simple: clean water, left to stand, is the right start for a beginner.

Buy your basic equipment before the fish arrive: a scoop net, buckets, a weighing scale, feed containers, a thermometer, and a simple water test kit if you can afford one. You will also need a cover or net over the pond to keep out birds and stop fish jumping out during rain. A generator helps if you use an aerator or pump, but many small ponds do fine with regular water changes and no electricity — plan your system around the power you actually have, not the power you wish you had.

## Step 4: Buy Good Fingerlings or Juveniles

Your harvest begins with the quality of the fish you stock. Buy fingerlings or juveniles from a reputable hatchery, not from a random seller at the roadside. A good hatchery will tell you the age and size of the fish, how they were bred, and what they have been fed. Ask other farmers in your area which hatcheries they trust — a hatchery with a good name is worth travelling for.

Juveniles, which are larger than fingerlings, cost more per fish but survive better and reach market size faster, so many beginners prefer them for a first batch. Fingerlings are cheaper and give you more fish for your money, but they are more fragile and need more careful handling. Whichever you buy, inspect them before you pay: healthy young fish swim actively, have no visible wounds or white patches, and respond quickly when disturbed. Avoid fish that float listlessly, have damaged fins, or look thin.

Transport the fish carefully in clean water in a covered container or oxygenated bag, kept shaded, and move them as quickly as you can. On arrival, do not pour them straight into the pond: float the bag or container in the pond water for 15 to 30 minutes so the temperatures equalise, then gently release the fish. This step, called acclimatisation, prevents shock that can kill many fish on the first day.

Count your fish as you stock them and write the number down — you will need it to calculate survival rate, feed quantity and expected harvest. Expect some losses, but if you lose many fish in the first week, something is wrong with the water, the handling or the quality of the stock, and you should find out what before it gets worse.

## Step 5: Feed Properly — Feed Is Your Biggest Cost

Feed is the largest expense in fish farming, often taking up well over half of your total production cost, so getting feeding right is the difference between profit and loss. Buy feed from a reputable manufacturer or dealer, and choose the right size for the age of your fish: smaller pellets for fingerlings, larger ones as they grow. The bag should state the protein content — catfish feed commonly ranges from about 35 to 45 percent crude protein depending on the growth stage, with higher protein for younger fish.

Feed twice a day, morning and evening, and feed only what the fish will finish in a few minutes. Overfeeding wastes money and pollutes the water, which then harms the fish. A practical guide is to feed roughly 3 to 5 percent of the total body weight of the fish per day when they are young, reducing the percentage as they grow. You will need to estimate their weight by sampling a few fish regularly with your scale — weigh a small sample, work out the average, and adjust the feed quantity. Keep a feeding record: date, feed type, quantity and any observations. That record is how you know whether your fish are growing as they should.

For your first cycle, use a good commercial feed and learn the business first. Store feed in a cool, dry place, off the floor, and use it within its shelf life — damp or mouldy feed can poison your fish.

## Step 6: Manage Water Quality and Fish Health

Fish live in their water, so water quality is fish health. The most common beginner mistake is feeding well and ignoring the water until fish start dying. Check your pond every day: look at the colour and smell of the water, watch how the fish behave, and notice whether they are eating normally. Fish gasping at the surface, refusing feed, or swimming sluggishly are warning signs — act on them the same day, not next week.

Change part of the water regularly. For a small concrete or tarpaulin pond, replacing about 20 to 30 percent of the water every few days, or more often when the fish are large and the water looks dirty, keeps waste levels down. Do not change all the water at once unless you have to, because a sudden change can shock the fish. If you use tap water for changes, let it stand for a day so the chlorine clears. Keep the pond covered or shaded where possible, because very hot water holds less oxygen and stresses the fish.

Watch for disease: white patches, swollen bellies, damaged fins, unusual spots, or fish dying one after another. Remove dead fish immediately and dispose of them far from the pond. Do not pour antibiotics or chemicals into the pond at random — get advice from an experienced farmer or a fisheries officer first, because the wrong treatment can kill the whole pond. Prevention is cheaper than cure: good water, correct stocking density, proper feeding and clean handling prevent most problems. Keep nets and buckets clean and never move equipment between ponds without cleaning it.

## Step 7: Work Out Your Costs and Your Selling Price

Before your fish reach market size, know your numbers. Add up everything you spent on the cycle: pond construction or tank, fingerlings or juveniles, feed, water, medication, labour, transport and any equipment. Divide that total by the number of fish you expect to harvest and their expected weight, and you have your cost per kilogram. Compare it with the selling price per kilogram in your area. If your cost is close to or above the selling price, something needs to change — cheaper feed, better survival, or a bigger average size.

As a rough guide, a small beginner cycle of a few hundred catfish can cost anywhere from roughly ₦300,000 to ₦800,000 depending on pond type, fish numbers and feed prices, and a cycle typically takes about four to six months to reach table size of around one kilogram per fish. These are wide ranges, not promises — your own figures depend on your local prices and how well you manage the pond. The point is to calculate before you commit, not after you have spent the money.

Sell by weight, not by guesswork. Weigh your fish with a scale and charge per kilogram, so both you and the buyer know the deal is fair. Agree your price before you harvest, and remember that prices vary by season — fish often sell better and at higher prices during festive periods, weekends and dry season months when supply is tighter. Plan your stocking so that your harvest falls when demand is strong, if you can.

## Step 8: Sell Your Fish — Start Before Harvest Day

Do not wait until your fish are ready before looking for buyers. Start selling while the fish are still growing. Tell the barbecue spots, pepper soup joints, market women, hotels and caterers in your area that you have fish coming, show them your pond, and agree on quantities and prices in advance. A buyer who has seen your farm and trusts your fish will pay a fair price and come back for the next cycle.

Harvest in stages if you can: sell in batches over a few weeks instead of everything on one day. This spreads your income, avoids flooding the local market, and lets the remaining fish keep growing. Keep harvested fish alive in clean water until the buyer arrives and handle them gently, because bruised or stressed fish lose quality and value.

Use WhatsApp to announce your harvest: clear photos, the price per kilogram, your location and your phone number. Ask happy buyers to recommend you to others, and keep a list of your customers with their phone numbers so you can tell them first when the next batch is ready. A fish farmer with a list of regular buyers never struggles to sell, because the customers are already waiting.

## Step 9: Keep Records, Review the Cycle and Grow

Record everything: the date you stocked, the number and cost of fingerlings, every bag of feed, every water change, every death, and every sale with the weight and price. At the end of the cycle, add it all up: total cost, total sales, profit or loss, survival rate, and average weight at harvest. This review is the most valuable part of the cycle, because it tells you exactly what to change next time — whether you need better fingerlings, less feed waste, or a different selling price.

Grow only after a profitable cycle: add a second pond or tank, or increase your numbers modestly — one step at a time. Consider keeping your own broodstock or learning to hatch fingerlings only after you have mastered growing fish to market size, since hatcheries are a separate business with their own skills. You can also add value later with smoked fish, packaged fillets or a regular restaurant contract, each step funded by the profits of the last one, not by fresh borrowing. Start small, learn the cycle, sell well, and let the next pond be paid for by the first one.
