Every day in Lagos, Abuja, Port Harcourt and Onitsha, thousands of hungry people open WhatsApp before they open any food app. They are in estate groups, office groups, church groups and family groups, asking the same question: "Who is selling food today?" If you can cook well, that question is your market. You do not need a restaurant, a shop, or a big budget to start. You need a phone, a working kitchen, good food, and a WhatsApp setup that turns chats into paid orders.

This guide walks you through the whole thing, step by step, from choosing what to sell to collecting your money and getting food to your customers while it is still hot. Everything here is written for Nigerian realities: data costs, power cuts, traffic, dispatch riders, and customers who will pay by transfer and then ask, "Have you seen it?"

## Why WhatsApp Works So Well for Food in Nigeria

WhatsApp is already on almost every phone in Nigeria. Your customers do not need to download anything new, learn a new app, or create an account. They already chat there every day. That is the biggest advantage you have over a food app or a website: there is no friction between seeing your food and ordering it.

The costs are low too. WhatsApp Business is free. You can start with the phone you already have, the pots you already own, and ingredients bought from the market that morning. Many successful food vendors in Lekki, Surulere, Wuse, GRA and Diobu started exactly like this: one pot, one phone, and a broadcast list of 50 people.

## Step 1: Decide Exactly What You Will Sell

Do not try to sell everything. A vendor who sells jollof rice, ofada, amala, pepper soup, small chops, cakes, shawarma and zobo on the same day is a vendor who will run out of gas, run out of time, and disappoint somebody. Start narrow and do it brilliantly.

Pick one lane first. Here are lanes that work well on WhatsApp in Nigeria:

- **Daily lunch packs:** Jollof rice, fried rice, white rice and stew, ofada rice, amala and ewedu, pounded yam and egusi. Sold in packs from ₦2,500 to ₦5,000 depending on your area and portion.
- **Soups in litres:** Egusi, ogbono, vegetable, bitter leaf, afang, edikang ikong and nsala, sold in 1-litre, 2-litre and 5-litre bowls. A 2-litre bowl of egusi with stockfish and meat can sell for ₦12,000 to ₦18,000 in Lagos.
- **Small chops and finger food:** Spring rolls, samosa, puff puff, meat pie, chicken wings and grilled turkey for birthdays, office parties and owambes.
- **Breakfast packs:** Bread and beans, yam and egg sauce, akara and pap, sandwiches and fruit packs for offices that order before 8 a.m.
- **Weekend specials:** Full trays of party jollof, asun, peppered chicken, grilled fish and moi moi for Saturdays, when demand is highest.

Choose based on three things: what you cook best, what ingredients you can buy reliably near you, and what people around you already buy. If you live near offices in Ikeja or Victoria Island, lunch packs are gold. If you live in a residential estate in Ajah or Lugbe, soups in litres and weekend trays will sell better because families stock up.

## Step 2: Price Your Food So You Actually Make Money

This is where many food vendors fail. They price by copying a neighbour, or by guessing what customers will accept, and then discover at the end of the month that they worked every day and made nothing. Price from your costs, not from feelings.

### Work out your cost per pack

Buy your ingredients for one batch and write down every naira. Include the rice, oil, pepper, meat, seasoning, gas, packaging, foil, nylon, and the dispatch rider if you deliver. Then divide by the number of packs you produced.

Here is a simple example for jollof rice in Lagos. A bag of rice, chicken, tomatoes, pepper, oil, seasoning and gas for 20 packs might cost you ₦48,000 in total. That is ₦2,400 per pack. If you sell at ₦3,500, you make ₦1,100 per pack before delivery costs. If you sell at ₦2,500, you make only ₦100 — and one spoilt batch wipes out a whole week.

Use this formula and write it on paper where you can see it:

**Selling price = cost per pack + your profit + delivery allowance**

Aim for at least 30 to 40 percent gross margin on food. If your cost is ₦2,400, your price should be ₦3,500 to ₦4,000. Customers will pay for food that tastes good, arrives hot, and arrives on time. They will not stay loyal to cheap food that disappoints them.

### Set clear prices and stick to them

Publish a short price list and pin it in your WhatsApp Business catalogue and in your profile. For example:

- Jollof rice + chicken — ₦3,500
- Fried rice + turkey — ₦4,000
- Ofada rice + ayamase + meat — ₦4,500
- Egusi soup, 2 litres — ₦15,000
- Small chops, pack of 10 — ₦5,000

Do not negotiate every order down. If a customer says "it is too expensive," you can offer a smaller pack, not a lower price for the same pack. Discounts should be planned, for example a 10 percent discount on orders of 10 packs or more for an office, not given away because somebody asked nicely in a chat.

## Step 3: Set Up WhatsApp Business Properly

Download WhatsApp Business from the Play Store or App Store. It is free and it is different from regular WhatsApp: it gives you a business profile, a catalogue, labels, quick replies, away messages and greeting messages. Use a dedicated number for the business if you can, even if it is a second SIM in the same phone. Mixing business chats with family chats is how orders get lost.

### Your business profile

Fill in every field. Business name, for example "Mama T's Kitchen — Lekki." Category: Food and Drink. Address or area, for example "Chevron, Lekki, Lagos." Opening hours, for example "Monday to Saturday, 7 a.m. to 7 p.m." Email and website if you have them. Add a clear profile photo: your food, your logo, or your smiling face in an apron. People order from people they can see.

### Your catalogue

The catalogue is your menu. Add every dish as a product with a good photo, the exact name, the price, and a short description. "Party jollof rice with peppered chicken, served hot in a foil pack — ₦3,500." Customers can browse the catalogue and tap to order without you typing the menu 40 times a day.

Take your photos in daylight, near a window, on a clean plate or in clean packaging. You do not need a professional camera. A clean phone photo in natural light will outsell a dark, blurry photo every time. Show the real food, the real portion, and the real packaging. If the photo shows more chicken than the pack contains, customers will feel cheated when it arrives.

### Quick replies, labels and messages

Set up quick replies for the questions you will answer 20 times a day. For example, type "/menu" and it sends your full menu. Type "/delivery" and it sends your delivery areas and fees. Type "/payment" and it sends your account details. This saves you hours and it means customers get answers in seconds, not hours.

Use labels to organise your chats: "New order," "Paid," "Cooking," "Out for delivery," "Delivered," "Repeat customer." At a glance you can see which orders still need attention. On a busy Saturday with 30 orders, labels are the difference between a smooth day and chaos.

Set a greeting message for new chats — "Hello, welcome to Mama T's Kitchen! Our menu is in our catalogue. What would you like to order today?" — and an away message for when you are sleeping or cooking: "Thank you for your message. We are currently cooking. We will reply within 30 minutes."

## Step 4: Take Orders Without Losing Any

Orders will come from everywhere: direct chats, group mentions, status replies, broadcast replies, referrals, and voice notes. You need one simple order process that works every time, no matter where the customer came from.

Use an order format and ask every customer for the same details:

1. Name
2. What they want and how many packs
3. Delivery address with a landmark
4. Phone number
5. Time they want it delivered
6. Payment method

Copy that list into a quick reply so you can send it in one tap. When the customer replies, confirm the order back to them in one message: "Confirmed: 2 packs jollof rice + chicken (₦7,000) + delivery to Ikate (₦1,500) = ₦8,500. Delivery by 1 p.m. Please send payment to confirm." That single confirmation message prevents almost every misunderstanding.

### Payment before cooking

For new customers, collect payment before you cook. This is not rude; it is standard for food vendors in Nigeria. Food is perishable. If you cook 10 packs for a customer who never shows up, you lose the food and the money. Your rule can be simple: "Payment confirms your order." Regular customers who have paid reliably 10 times can pay on delivery if you choose, but new customers pay first.

Accept transfers to your bank account, and consider a Moniepoint or Opay account for fast confirmation alerts. If you use Paystack or Flutterwave payment links, even better: the customer pays with a card or transfer and you get a notification. Always confirm when you have seen the money. Send a short message: "Payment received, thank you! Your food will be ready by 1 p.m." That message builds trust instantly.

## Step 5: Cook, Pack and Deliver Like a Professional

Good food that arrives cold, spilled or late is a bad experience. Packaging and delivery are part of the product.

Buy proper packaging: foil packs, food-grade plastic containers with tight lids, nylon bags, and paper bags for soups in litres. Pack hot food in containers that will not melt or leak. Seal soups well. Put a serviette and a spoon in each pack if you can; small touches make customers remember you.

Cook in batches and cook to a timetable. If you promise delivery by 1 p.m., the food must leave your kitchen by 12:30 p.m. at the latest. Lagos traffic is unpredictable, so build in 30 minutes of buffer. It is better to arrive 15 minutes early than 15 minutes late with cold food.

### Delivery options

- **Self-delivery for nearby orders:** If the customer is in your estate or street, deliver it yourself or send a family member. It is free and personal.
- **Dispatch riders:** For orders across Lekki, Ikeja, Surulere or Wuse, use a dispatch rider. Agree the fee with the customer before you cook, and add it to the total. A typical short delivery within Lekki might cost ₦1,500 to ₦2,500. Longer trips cost more. Use riders you trust, and give them the customer's phone number and a clear address with a landmark.
- **Pickup:** Offer pickup for customers who drive. Give a clear pickup point and a pickup time window. Many office workers prefer to pick up on their way home.

Tell the customer the delivery fee upfront, before they pay. Nothing annoys a customer more than a surprise delivery charge after payment. If delivery to a far area is too expensive, say so honestly and suggest pickup instead.

## Step 6: Get Customers Every Day

You do not need adverts to start. You need consistency and visibility in the places your customers already are.

### WhatsApp Status, every day

Post on your WhatsApp Status at least twice a day: once in the morning, around 7 a.m. to 8 a.m., when people are deciding what to eat, and once around 11 a.m., when hunger sets in. Show the food cooking, the finished packs, a customer testimonial, or a short video of steam rising from a fresh pot. Add the price and the order link. Status is free advertising to everyone who has saved your number.

Ask happy customers to save your number so they see your status. Many vendors lose sales simply because customers never see their daily menu.

### Broadcast lists, not spam groups

Create broadcast lists of customers who have ordered before or asked to be added. A broadcast sends your daily menu to each person privately. Send one menu message per day, in the morning, with the dishes, prices and the cut-off time for orders. Do not send five messages a day. One good message beats five annoying ones, and customers will block you if you spam them.

## Step 7: Keep Customers Coming Back

Getting an order is hard. Keeping a customer is cheaper and more profitable. A customer who orders lunch every weekday is worth more than 20 one-time customers.

After delivery, send a short follow-up message: "Hello Mrs Ade, hope you enjoyed the jollof. Thank you for ordering!" That single message makes people feel valued. If there was a problem, they will tell you privately instead of telling the whole estate group.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Cooking without payment from strangers.** You will be left with food and no money.
- **No cut-off time.** Set an order cut-off, for example 10 a.m. for lunch and 4 p.m. for dinner. Orders after that go to the next day. Without a cut-off, you will be cooking at midnight.
- **Underpricing to win customers.** You will attract customers who leave the moment you raise prices, and you will burn out cooking for almost no profit.
- **Poor photos.** Blurry, dark photos of food in a dirty pot will kill your sales. Take 10 minutes to plate the food well and photograph it in daylight.
- **Saying yes to every area.** If you cannot deliver to Sangotedo, Badagry or Kubwa reliably, say so. A refused order is better than a failed delivery.
- **No records.** If you do not write down what you sold and what you spent, you will not know whether you are making money or losing it.
- **Ignoring hygiene.** Wash your hands, cover your hair while cooking, keep your kitchen clean, and use clean water. Customers notice, and food safety is not negotiable.

## How Much Can You Realistically Make?

It depends on your area, your menu and your consistency, but here is a realistic example. If you sell 20 lunch packs a day at ₦3,500, that is ₦70,000 in daily sales. If your cost per pack is ₦2,400, your gross profit is ₦22,000 a day before delivery and data costs. Over 22 working days, that is ₦484,000 in gross profit. Even after gas, transport, packaging and data, a well-run small food business can earn a solid income — and it can grow.

Growth looks like this: more packs per day, then soups in litres on weekends, then office contracts where a company orders 30 packs every weekday, then a small shop or a cloud kitchen. Many vendors start with 10 packs a day and grow to 100 packs a day within a year. The key is consistency: same quality, same time, same price, every day.

## Your First Week Action Plan

Do not wait for everything to be perfect. Start small and start this week.

1. **Day 1:** Choose your menu. Cost it properly. Set your prices.
2. **Day 2:** Set up WhatsApp Business. Add your profile, catalogue and quick replies.
3. **Day 3:** Cook your menu for family and friends. Take good photos in daylight.
4. **Day 4:** Post your menu on your status. Send it to 20 contacts personally. Ask them to share it.
5. **Day 5:** Take your first paid orders. Collect payment before cooking. Deliver on time.
6. **Day 6:** Follow up with every customer. Ask for feedback. Fix what needs fixing.
7. **Day 7:** Count your money. Write down what sold, what did not, and what you will do differently next week.

If you sell only 10 packs in your first week, that is a win. You have proof that people will pay for your food. Build from there, one happy customer at a time.

Your kitchen is already a business. WhatsApp is your shopfront, your menu, your cashier and your customer service desk, all in one app on your phone. Set it up properly, cook food you are proud of, price it fairly, deliver on time, and talk to your customers like people, not transactions. Do that every day, and the orders will come — from your kitchen to their tables, one chat at a time.
