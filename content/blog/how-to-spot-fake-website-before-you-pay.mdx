You found it. The phone you have been wanting, at half the price. Or the perfect dress, the cheap flight, the "limited offer" that ends tonight. The website looks good. The pictures are sharp. You are one click away from paying.

Stop for two minutes first. A beautiful website can still be a scam, and fake online stores are one of the most common ways people lose money in Nigeria. The good news is that most fake websites give themselves away — if you know what to look for.

## The First Check: The Address Bar

Before you look at anything else on the page, look at the address bar at the top of your browser. This is where many scams are exposed.

**Look for the padlock and https.** A real online store should use "https" at the start of its address, with a small padlock icon beside it. This means the connection between you and the site is encrypted. If a site asking for payment shows only "http" with no padlock, or your browser warns you that the site is not secure, do not pay there.

But be careful: a padlock alone does not prove a site is genuine. Scammers can get a padlock too. It only means your connection is private, not that the business is honest. So treat the padlock as a minimum requirement, not a guarantee.

**Read the domain name carefully.** Scammers love names that look almost right. A fake store might use "jumia-deals.com" instead of "jumia.com.ng", or "paystack-pay.com" instead of "paystack.com". They swap a letter, add a word like "deals" or "promo", or use a strange ending. If you clicked a link from a WhatsApp broadcast or an advert, type the real website address into your browser yourself instead of trusting the link.

## Look at the Website Itself

A genuine business invests in its website. A fake one usually cuts corners, and the corners show.

**Spelling and grammar.** Read the text on the homepage. If it is full of spelling mistakes and strange sentences, be suspicious. A real business that wants your money usually pays attention to how it presents itself.

**No real contact details.** A real store gives you a way to reach a human being: a working phone number, an email address on its own domain, and a physical address. If the only contact is a WhatsApp number, or there is no contact page at all, that is a red flag. Try calling the number — if it never picks up, walk away.

**Copied pictures.** Search for a product photo online. If the same photos appear on many different websites under different business names, the "store" probably stole them. Real sellers usually have their own photos.

**No about page, no story.** A real business tells you who it is, where it is based, and how long it has been operating. A fake site often has no about page at all, or a vague one-liner that could belong to anyone.

## Prices That Are Too Good to Be True

This is the oldest trick in the book, and it still works because it plays on hope.

If an iPhone that sells for ₦1,200,000 everywhere else is listed at ₦350,000, it is not a bargain. It is a trap. The same goes for a "flash sale" where every single item is 80% off, or a luxury item at a price that makes no business sense. A real business cannot sell below cost and survive. A scammer can, because they never intend to send you anything.

A genuine discount is usually modest — 10%, 20%, maybe 30% during a real sale. When the discount is so large that you would feel foolish not to buy, that is exactly the feeling the scammer wants. Slow down and compare the price with two or three well-known stores. If the gap is enormous, trust the gap, not the deal.

## Check Who Is Behind the Site

A real business leaves traces. A fake one tries not to.

**Search for the name.** Type the business name into Google, followed by "review", "scam", or "complaint". If other people have been cheated, someone has usually posted about it. No results at all can also be a warning — a real business that has traded for a while usually has some footprint: reviews, a Facebook page, or a Google Business Profile.

**Check their social media.** Click through to their Instagram or Facebook page. Are there genuine posts over time, real comments from customers, and photos that match the website? Or was the page created last week, with ten followers and no real engagement? A page created days ago to support a "big sale" is a classic sign of a scam.

**Look for a registered business.** In Nigeria, a real company is usually registered with the Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC), and some websites show their RC number. If one is shown, you can check it. If no registration is mentioned anywhere, ask why.

## Payment Red Flags

How a site asks you to pay tells you a lot about whether it is real.

**Only a personal bank account.** A genuine store usually accepts card payments, transfer to a business account, or a recognised payment gateway. If the only option is to transfer money to a personal account — "Send ₦45,000 to John Doe, GTBank" — be very careful. Many small Nigerian businesses do use personal accounts, so it is not proof of fraud on its own, but combined with other red flags it should stop you.

**Pressure to pay immediately.** "Only 2 left!" "Offer ends in 10 minutes!" Urgency is a sales tactic, but scammers use it to stop you thinking. A real store will still be there tomorrow. If you feel rushed, that is the moment to slow down.

**No pay on delivery, no escrow, no buyer protection.** A trustworthy seller is usually comfortable with payment methods that protect you, such as pay on delivery or a gateway that lets you dispute a charge. A seller who insists on full upfront payment to a personal account is asking you to take all the risk.

**Strange payment pages.** If you click "Pay" and land on a page that looks nothing like the store, or it asks for your card PIN or bank login details, close it immediately. A real payment page never asks for those.

## What to Do If You Already Paid a Fake Site

If you have already sent money and now suspect the site was fake, act fast:

1. **Contact your bank immediately.** Report the transfer as fraud. If the money has not yet been withdrawn, the bank may be able to place a hold or trace it. The sooner you report, the better your chances.
2. **Keep every record.** Save screenshots of the website, the payment receipt, the account number you paid into, and every message you exchanged with the seller.
3. **Report the site.** Report a fraudulent website to the police cybercrime unit, the Economic and Financial Crimes Commission (EFCC), and the platform where you found the link. Reporting helps protect the next person.
4. **Warn others.** If you found the site through a group or a friend's share, tell them. Scams spread because the first victims stay quiet out of embarrassment. There is no shame in being targeted.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a padlock in the address bar enough to trust a site?

No. The padlock only means your connection is encrypted, and scammers can get one too. Treat it as a minimum requirement, then check the domain name, contact details, prices, and the business's history before you pay.

### Can a fake website appear at the top of Google?

Yes. Scammers run adverts and use search tricks to appear high in results, and some pay for sponsored listings. A high ranking is not proof that a site is genuine. Always run the checks in this guide, even for sites you found through search.

### What if the site has many good reviews?

Read them carefully. Fake reviews are often short and vague — "Great seller, fast delivery, thanks!" repeated many times under slightly different names. Real reviews mention specific details: the product, the delivery time, the area, a small problem and how it was fixed. If every review sounds the same, be suspicious.

### Should I ever pay into a personal bank account?

Many honest small businesses in Nigeria do use personal accounts, so it is not automatic proof of fraud. But it is a reason to be extra careful. If the seller only accepts a personal account, has no verifiable business details, and is pressuring you to pay quickly, do not proceed. Safer options include pay on delivery or a recognised payment gateway.

Nobody plans to fall for a fake website. It happens in a moment of excitement, when a deal looks too good and the clock seems to be ticking. The defence is simple: pause, check the address bar, check the contact details, compare the price, and search the name. Two minutes of checking can save you money, stress, and a long fight to get it back.
