Pricing is the hardest part of running a salon, a beauty business or a tailoring shop in Nigeria. Charge too little and you work all day for almost nothing. Charge too much and clients walk to the shop next door. Many skilled hairdressers, makeup artists, nail technicians and tailors are excellent at their craft and terrible at pricing it — and that is why they stay broke while being busy.

This guide will help you price your services properly. You will learn how to calculate your true costs, how to set prices that leave a real profit, how to handle clients who say your price is too high, and when and how to raise your prices without losing the customers you worked hard to win.

## Why Most Service Businesses Undercharge

The most common reason is fear. You are afraid that if you raise your price, your clients will leave. So you keep your prices low, you attract clients who only care about cheap prices, and you end up exhausted and underpaid.

Another reason is that many service providers do not know their true costs. They count the obvious cost — the attachment, the fabric, the polish — but forget the rent, the electricity, the transport, the time, and the wear on their equipment. When you forget half your costs, any price feels like profit, until the month ends and there is nothing left.

A third reason is copying competitors without understanding their situation. The salon down the road may charge less because the owner does not pay rent, or because they use cheaper products, or because they are new and trying to attract clients. Their price is not your price.

The truth is simple: clients do not only buy the cheapest option. They buy the service they trust, the one that is convenient, and the one that makes them look and feel good. If your work is good and your service is reliable, there are clients willing to pay a fair price for it.

## Step 1: Know Your True Costs

Before you set any price, you must know what each service actually costs you. There are three types of cost to count.

**Direct costs** are the materials used for one client: the hair attachment, the relaxer, the dye, the makeup products, the polish, the fabric and thread for a tailor, the electricity used during the service. These change with every client.

**Indirect costs** are the costs of running your business whether you have one client or fifty: rent, generator fuel, electricity bills, water, cleaning supplies, and the cost of your tools and equipment wearing out.

**Your time** is a cost too. If a hairstyle takes four hours, those four hours have a value. Many service providers work for free without realising it, because they never put a price on their own time.

Add up your monthly indirect costs and divide by the number of clients you serve in a month. That gives you the share of rent and bills each client should carry. Then add the direct cost of the service and the value of your time. The result is your true cost. Any price below that is a loss, no matter how busy you are.

Keep a simple notebook or spreadsheet. Write down what you spend each week and what you earn. After a month, you will see clearly whether your prices are working or not.

## Step 2: The Simple Pricing Formula

A practical formula for pricing a service is: price equals direct cost plus your share of indirect costs plus your time plus profit.

Start with the direct cost. If a braiding service uses attachment worth eight thousand naira and other materials worth two thousand naira, the direct cost is ten thousand naira.

Add the share of your monthly running costs. If your rent, fuel and bills come to one hundred and fifty thousand naira a month and you serve seventy-five clients, each client carries two thousand naira of running costs.

Add the value of your time. If you want to earn at least three thousand naira per hour and the service takes three hours, that is nine thousand naira.

Add a profit margin on top — at least twenty to thirty percent of the total so far. That profit is what lets you grow, restock, handle emergencies and pay yourself properly.

Using this example, the price would be ten thousand plus two thousand plus nine thousand, which is twenty-one thousand naira, plus a profit margin of about five thousand, giving a price of around twenty-six thousand naira. If that looks high compared with what you charge now, it probably means you have been undercharging.

You do not need to show clients this calculation. But you need to do it yourself, so your prices are based on reality and not on guesswork.

## Step 3: Research What Others Charge

Knowing your costs is only half the job. You also need to know the market.

Visit or call three to five salons, beauty studios or tailoring shops in your area. Ask about their prices for the same services you offer. Check their Instagram pages and WhatsApp catalogues, where many now publish price lists.

Do not aim to be the cheapest. Aim to be fairly priced for the quality you offer. If your work is better, your shop is cleaner, and your service is more reliable, you can charge a little more than the average and still keep clients.

Notice the range. In Lagos, prices for the same hairstyle can differ widely between Lekki, Ikeja and a neighbourhood salon, because rent and clientele differ. Price for your area and your clients, not for a different part of town.

Also notice what premium providers charge and what they offer for that price: a comfortable chair, a clean environment, refreshments, a booking system, and excellent finishing. Those extras are part of why clients pay more, and you can add them gradually.

## Step 4: Pricing for Salons

Hair services have some of the widest price ranges in the beauty business, because the cost of materials varies so much.

For braids, your price should reflect the style, the length, the size of the parts, and the cost of the attachment. Knotless braids take longer and use more attachment than simple cornrows, so they should cost more. State clearly whether the price includes the attachment or the client brings their own, because that changes the price significantly.

For wigs and installations, price the wig, the installation and the styling separately, or as a clear package. Clients need to understand what they are paying for.

For relaxers, dyeing and treatments, factor in the cost of the chemicals and the risk involved. A treatment that goes wrong costs you far more than the price of the service, so do not rush and do not underprice.

For barbing salons, consider a simple price list: a regular cut, a cut with dye, a cut with hair treatment, and a home service price. Offer a small discount for regular weekly customers, but do not make your whole business dependent on discounts.

Set a price for home service that covers your transport and your time. Many hairdressers lose money on home service because they charge the shop price and then spend an hour in traffic for free.

## Step 5: Pricing for Beauty Services

Makeup pricing depends on the occasion and the products used. A simple face beat for a party is different from full bridal makeup with lashes, gele tying and a touch-up kit. Price bridal makeup as a package that includes the trial, the day-of service and any travel to the venue.

Nail services should be priced by the service: manicure, pedicure, gel polish, acrylic full set, refills and nail art. Each one uses different products and takes a different amount of time, so each needs its own price. Charge extra for stones, French tips and complex designs.

Lash and brow services are priced per session, with refills at a lower price. Be clear about how long the work lasts so clients understand what they are paying for.

Skin care services such as facials should be priced by the type of facial and the products used. A basic facial and a deep-cleansing facial with extra steps are not the same service and should not have the same price.

For all beauty services, publish a price list. Clients who can see your prices before they book are more likely to book, and less likely to argue about the price when they arrive.

## Step 6: Pricing for Tailors

Tailoring has a special pricing problem: clients bring their own fabric, so the tailor's price is mostly for labour, skill and time. That makes it easy to undercharge, because the tailor forgets to price their time.

Price by the garment and by the complexity. A simple gown is not the same as a gown with lining, beading, a corset or hand finishing. A senator wear is not the same as a three-piece suit. Each style should have a base price, with extra charges for complex details.

Factor in the cost of thread, zips, buttons, lining, interfacing and electricity. These small costs add up, and if you do not include them, you pay for them from your profit.

Charge for express work. If a client brings fabric on Friday and needs the outfit for Saturday, that is urgent work that disrupts your other orders. An express fee of twenty-five to fifty percent is fair, and it teaches clients to bring their work early.

Take a deposit before you cut the fabric. A deposit of fifty percent or more is standard. It commits the client, covers your materials, and protects you if the client disappears.

Be clear about alterations. Small adjustments after delivery are part of good service, but a full redesign because the client changed their mind is a new job and should be charged as one.

Agree on the delivery date in writing, even if it is just a WhatsApp message. Late delivery is the most common complaint against tailors, and a clear date protects both you and the client.

## Step 7: Packages, Discounts and Loyalty

Packages make it easier for clients to buy and easier for you to earn. A salon can offer a "full glam" package: hair, makeup and nails for a bride at a package price. A tailor can offer a discount for three outfits ordered together. Packages increase the value of each sale.

Discounts should be deliberate, not desperate. A discount for a first-time client, a birthday discount, or a festive-season offer is fine. A permanent discount is not a discount — it is just a low price.

Loyalty rewards work well for repeat services. A free manicure after five paid ones, or a small discount on a client's tenth visit, gives clients a reason to return. Keep it simple and track it honestly.

Avoid price wars. If a competitor drops their price, do not automatically follow them. Compete on quality, cleanliness, punctuality and customer care. Clients who leave you for a cheaper price will often return when the cheaper service disappoints them.

## Step 8: How to Raise Your Prices

Every service business needs to raise prices from time to time. Costs rise, your skill improves, and your time becomes more valuable. The key is to do it well.

Give your clients notice. Tell them two to four weeks in advance, in person or by message: "From the first of next month, the price of this service will change from X to Y." Clients accept a change they were warned about far better than a surprise at the till.

Explain briefly, without over-apologising. "The cost of materials and running the shop has gone up, and I want to keep giving you the same quality." Most clients understand.

Raise prices gradually rather than all at once. A small increase every few months is easier for clients to absorb than a sudden jump.

Improve something at the same time. A cleaner shop, a better product, a small extra service, or a more comfortable experience makes the new price feel justified.

Expect to lose a few clients. That is normal and healthy. The clients who leave because of a fair increase are usually the ones who paid the least and complained the most. The clients who stay are the ones who value your work, and they are the foundation of a profitable business.

## Step 9: Handling "Your Price Is Too High"

Every service provider hears this. How you respond matters.

Stay calm and confident. Do not apologise for your price and do not immediately offer a discount. Instead, explain what the price includes: the quality of the products, the time involved, the skill required, and the result the client will get.

Offer a simpler option if you have one. If a client cannot afford a full set of acrylics with nail art, offer a manicure with gel polish at a lower price. If a client cannot afford a fully lined gown, offer a simpler style. Giving options is better than cutting your price.

Know when to walk away. A client who insists on paying far below your cost is not a client; they are a loss. Politely decline and wish them well. Your time is better spent on clients who respect your work.

Never argue about price in front of other clients. Keep the conversation private and respectful. Your dignity is part of your brand.

## Step 10: Getting Paid Properly

Set clear payment terms and stick to them. For salons and beauty services, payment is usually made after the service, but a deposit is wise for long appointments, bridal work and home service.

For tailors, always take a deposit before you start, and collect the balance before you hand over the finished outfit. Tailors who release clothes without full payment spend too much time chasing money.

Accept transfers as well as cash. Many clients prefer to transfer, so have your account details ready. Confirm payment before the client leaves, and send a receipt by WhatsApp if they ask for one.

Keep records of your income. At the end of each week, write down what you earned and what you spent. This simple habit shows you whether your business is growing and whether your prices are working.

## Your Pricing Action Plan

This week, write down your prices for your five most popular services and calculate the true cost of each one using the formula in this guide. If any service costs you more than you charge, raise the price or stop offering it.

Next, check the prices of three competitors in your area. Compare honestly: is your work better, the same, or worse? Price accordingly.

Then publish a clear price list on your WhatsApp Business catalogue and your Instagram page. Clients who know your prices before they book are easier to serve.

Finally, set a date to review your prices every three months. Costs change, your skill grows, and your prices should grow with them.

You are not being wicked by charging a fair price. You are running a business, and a business that cannot pay its owner properly will not survive. Price your skill with confidence, deliver excellent work, and the right clients will stay with you.
