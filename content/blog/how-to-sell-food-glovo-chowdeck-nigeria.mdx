Every day in Lagos and Abuja, thousands of people open Glovo or Chowdeck, scroll through the food options, and order lunch, dinner or a late-night snack without ever leaving their homes or offices. Behind each of those orders is a restaurant, a cloud kitchen or a small food vendor who decided to put their food where the customers already are.

If you run a food business in Nigeria — a restaurant, a bukka, a shawarma spot, a cake business, a home kitchen selling soups in litres — these delivery apps can bring you customers you would never reach on your own. But they are not free money. Both platforms charge commission on every order, they have standards you must meet, and the vendors who succeed treat the app as a real sales channel, not a side experiment.

This guide walks you through selling on Glovo and Chowdeck in Nigeria: how each platform works, what you need to join, how to set up your menu and prices, and how to handle orders during a busy rush.

## Glovo vs Chowdeck: What Is the Difference?

Both platforms deliver food, but they work differently, and understanding the difference helps you choose where to start.

**Glovo** is an international delivery platform that operates in Lagos and Abuja. It delivers almost anything — food, groceries, pharmacy items, parcels — and its rider network is large. For a food vendor, Glovo offers wide reach and a big pool of customers who already use the app for many things. The trade-off is that your restaurant sits alongside hundreds of others, including big chains, so standing out takes effort.

**Chowdeck** is a Nigerian food delivery platform built specifically for food. It focuses on restaurants and food vendors, and it has grown quickly in Lagos and Abuja by working closely with local kitchens. Many small food businesses find Chowdeck easier to work with because its team understands the local market — Nigerian dishes, local pricing, and the way small kitchens actually operate.

You do not have to choose just one. Many successful vendors list on both platforms at the same time, and some also keep selling through their own WhatsApp and Instagram. Being on both apps doubles your visibility. The extra work is real — you must keep both menus updated and manage orders from two places — but for a kitchen that can handle the volume, the extra sales are usually worth it.

## What You Need Before You Apply

Before you start the application, gather the documents and information both platforms typically ask for. Having them ready will speed up your onboarding by days.

**Your business details.** Your business name, the address of your kitchen or restaurant, and your phone number and email address. If your business is registered with the Corporate Affairs Commission, have your registration details ready.

**A bank account in the business name.** Both platforms pay you through bank transfer, usually weekly. Set up a dedicated business account if you do not have one already — mixing app payouts with personal spending makes it hard to know whether the apps are actually making you money.

**A food hygiene and safety mindset.** Your kitchen must be clean, your food handling must be safe, and your packaging must keep food hot, fresh and presentable during a 30 to 45 minute ride on a bike. Vendors with repeated complaints about food quality can be suspended.

**Good photos of your food.** This is not optional. Customers order with their eyes. You need clear, appetising photos of every dish on your menu, taken in good light, showing the actual portion the customer will receive. A phone camera is fine — take photos near a window in daylight, use a clean plate, and do not use filters that change the colour of the food.

**A menu with prices.** Decide your menu before you apply. Start with your best sellers — the five to ten dishes you cook well, that travel well, and that you can prepare quickly. A long menu slows down your kitchen and increases waste. You can always add dishes later.

## How to Register on Glovo

Glovo onboards restaurants through its partner portal. The usual process is:

**1. Sign up as a partner.** Go to the Glovo website and find the section for becoming a partner or adding your restaurant. Fill in your business name, location, contact details and the type of food you sell.

**2. Wait for the onboarding team to contact you.** A Glovo representative will call or email you to discuss your business, your menu and your kitchen. Be ready to answer questions about your opening hours, your delivery area and how many orders you can handle per hour.

**3. Agree the commercial terms.** Glovo charges a commission on each order — the exact percentage depends on your agreement and can vary, so read the terms carefully and ask questions before you sign. Make sure you understand how often you will be paid, what the commission covers, and whether there are any additional fees.

**4. Set up your store.** You will upload your menu, prices, food photos and opening hours through the Glovo partner app or portal. Take your time with this step — a well-set-up store with good photos and clear descriptions sells far better than a rushed one.

**5. Go live and receive your first orders.** Once your store is approved, it appears on the Glovo app. Orders come in through the partner app on your phone or a tablet in your kitchen. Accept each order quickly, prepare it within the stated time, and hand it to the rider when they arrive.

## How to Register on Chowdeck

Chowdeck's onboarding is similar, with a strong focus on helping local vendors get set up properly:

**1. Sign up as a vendor.** Go to the Chowdeck website and look for the vendor or partner sign-up page. Fill in your business details and the kind of food you sell.

**2. Speak with the Chowdeck team.** A member of their team will contact you to learn about your kitchen and walk you through the process. Use this conversation to ask every question you have — about commission, payouts, packaging, and how busy periods work.

**3. Agree the terms and set up your store.** Like Glovo, Chowdeck charges a commission per order. Confirm the rate, the payout schedule and any other fees in writing before you go live. Then upload your menu, prices, photos and opening hours.

**4. Complete any training.** Chowdeck provides guidance on how to use the vendor app, how to manage orders and how to keep your ratings high. Take it seriously — the vendors who follow the guidance tend to get more orders because the platform recommends reliable kitchens.

**5. Go live.** Your restaurant appears on the Chowdeck app, and orders start coming in. Keep your store open during the hours you promised, and keep your menu accurate.

If you are a small home-based kitchen, do not assume you are too small for these platforms. Both Glovo and Chowdeck work with small vendors, including cloud kitchens that have no dine-in area at all. What matters is that you can cook consistently, package properly and deliver on time.

## Pricing Your Food for Delivery Apps

This is where many vendors make their biggest mistake. They list the same prices they charge walk-in customers, then discover at the end of the week that after commission, packaging and delivery costs, they made little or nothing on each order.

Before you set your app prices, work out the true cost of each dish. Add up the cost of ingredients, the packaging (food packs, nylon bags, cutlery), the platform commission, and a share of your gas, electricity and other running costs. That total is your cost per dish. Your price must be above it by enough to leave you a real profit.

Many vendors set their app prices slightly higher than their walk-in prices — for example, a plate of jollof rice that sells for ₦3,500 in the restaurant might be listed at ₦4,000 or ₦4,500 on the app. This is normal, because customers are paying for the convenience of delivery. But do not overdo it: if your price is far above similar dishes on the app, customers will scroll past you.

Watch your competitors on the app. Search for the dishes you sell and see what others charge. You do not need to be the cheapest — the cheapest vendor often makes the least money — but you need to be in a range customers find reasonable.

Review your prices every month or two. Food prices in Nigeria change frequently, and a price that worked in January may lose you money by March. When you raise a price, do it in small steps and watch whether orders drop.

## Packaging: The Part Customers Judge You On

On a delivery app, your packaging is your dining room. The customer never sees your kitchen, your tables or your staff. They see a bag, open it, and judge your business by what is inside.

Invest in packaging that keeps food hot, prevents soup from spilling, and survives a bike ride over Lagos roads. Use sturdy food packs with tight-fitting lids for soups and stews. Double-bag items that can leak. Put hot food and cold drinks in separate bags. Include cutlery and serviettes, because many customers order to eat at work or in traffic and have no spoon at hand.

Brand your packaging if you can afford it. A simple sticker with your business name, phone number and WhatsApp contact turns every delivery into an advert. Even a hand-written note saying "Thank you for ordering from us" makes a difference.

Do not cut corners on packaging to save money. A soup that spills in transit costs you the refund, the bad review, and the customer. Good packaging costs a few hundred naira per order. A lost customer costs far more.

## Managing Orders During the Rush

The lunch rush between 12 noon and 2pm, and the dinner rush between 6pm and 9pm, are when most of your orders will come. They are also when things go wrong — the kitchen is overwhelmed, orders pile up, riders wait too long, and food goes out late.

Prepare for the rush before it starts. Cook your base items in advance: rice, stews, soups and proteins that can be portioned quickly. Keep your most popular dishes ready to pack. Assign one person — even if that person is you — to watch the app and accept orders immediately. An order that sits unaccepted for ten minutes is an order the customer may cancel.

Set realistic preparation times on the app. If a dish takes 25 minutes to prepare, do not promise 10. The platforms track how long you take, and vendors who are consistently late get fewer orders. It is better to set a slightly longer preparation time and deliver early than to promise speed you cannot keep.

If you are overwhelmed, use the app's option to pause new orders temporarily. A short pause is far better than accepting orders you cannot fulfil. Customers would rather see your store as temporarily busy than wait an hour for cold food.

Finally, keep your phone or tablet charged and your data on. Nothing is worse than missing orders because your device died in the middle of the lunch rush. Keep a charger in the kitchen.

## Getting Good Ratings and More Orders

On both Glovo and Chowdeck, your rating and your reliability decide how often customers see you. The apps recommend vendors who deliver good food on time, and they quietly push down vendors with complaints and cancellations. Your rating is your shopfront.

**Keep your food consistent.** The jollof rice a customer orders on Monday should taste the same on Friday. Use the same recipes, the same portions and the same ingredients every time. Consistency is what turns a first-time order into a weekly habit.

**Never cancel an order after accepting it unless you truly have no choice.** Cancellations hurt your standing on the platform and frustrate customers who are already hungry. If an ingredient runs out, mark the dish as unavailable on the app immediately, so customers cannot order it.

**Respond to problems fast.** If a customer complains through the app, respond politely and quickly. Apologise and offer a fix — a replacement, a refund through the platform, or a discount on the next order.

**Use the quiet hours to your advantage.** Breakfast orders, late-night snacks and weekend family orders are less competitive than the lunch rush. If your kitchen can handle it, consider opening earlier or staying open later than your competitors. A vendor who is open at 8pm when others have closed will collect the evening orders by default.

## Tracking Your Money: Are the Apps Actually Profitable?

Many vendors are busy on the apps but have no idea whether they are making money. Do not be one of them. Every week, sit down with your numbers.

Write down your total sales on each app for the week. Subtract the commission the platform took. Subtract your packaging costs, your ingredient costs for those orders, and a share of your running costs. What is left is your real profit from the app. If that number is small or negative, something needs to change — your prices, your menu, your packaging costs, or the dishes you offer.

Keep a separate record for each platform, so you can see whether Glovo or Chowdeck earns you more. That information tells you where to focus your energy and your promotions.

For keeping your books straight, simple tools help: a notebook, a spreadsheet, or a free invoice generator for the receipts and records you send to customers and suppliers, such as the one at joetech360.ng/tools/invoice-generator.

Also watch your payout schedule. Platforms usually pay weekly, which means the money from this week's orders arrives next week. Plan your cash flow around that delay. Do not spend money you have earned but not yet received — keep enough cash on hand to buy ingredients for the coming week.

## Common Mistakes Vendors Make on Delivery Apps

**Listing a menu you cannot cook consistently.** A dish that is excellent on Monday and terrible on Wednesday will earn you bad reviews. Start with dishes you can cook well every single day, and add new dishes only when you are sure of them.

**Using the same photos for every dish.** Customers notice when the photo for the fried rice looks identical to the photo for the jollof. Use real photos of each dish, showing the actual portion.

**Ignoring the app during opening hours.** A store that is marked open but does not accept orders quickly will lose customers and damage its rating. If you cannot watch the app, assign someone who can, or shorten your opening hours on the app to the times you can actually manage.

**Forgetting your own direct customers.** The apps bring you new customers, but the commission means you earn less per order than from a direct sale. Include a small card or sticker in every delivery with your WhatsApp number, so happy customers can order from you directly next time. Just make sure you follow each platform's rules about what you can include in an order.

**Giving up too early.** The first two weeks on an app are usually slow. You have no reviews and no history yet. Give it at least a month of consistent quality before you judge whether it works for you.

## Your First-Month Checklist

- Register your business details and bank account, and gather your food photos.
- Choose your starting menu: five to ten dishes you cook well and that travel well.
- Price each dish after working out the true cost, including commission and packaging.
- Set up your store on one platform first, get comfortable, then add the second.
- Buy proper packaging and brand it with your business name and WhatsApp number.
- Prepare for the lunch and dinner rushes with pre-cooked bases and a clear system for accepting orders.
- Track your weekly sales, commission, costs and profit for each platform separately.
- Ask happy customers to leave a review, and respond quickly and politely to any complaint.
- After a month, review the numbers: which dishes sell, which platform earns more, and what to change.

Selling food on Glovo and Chowdeck will not make your business successful overnight. But for a kitchen that cooks good food consistently, packages it well and treats the app like a real shop, these platforms can add a steady stream of orders every single day. Start with one platform, get your menu and your system right, and then expand.
