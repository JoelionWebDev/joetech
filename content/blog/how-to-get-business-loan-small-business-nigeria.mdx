Every growing small business in Nigeria reaches a point where its own money is not enough. You need more stock before December, a second freezer for your food business in Surulere, a delivery bike for your Lekki online shop, or a bigger space for your tailoring shop in Abuja. Your sales are good, your customers are asking for more, but you do not have the money in hand to take the next step.

That is the moment many owners think about a loan. It can be the bridge between where your business is and where it could be, or a burden that drags a healthy business down if you borrow too much, at the wrong price, or for the wrong reason. The difference is preparation. Lenders in Nigeria do not lend on sympathy. They lend on evidence that your business exists, earns money, is managed honestly, and can repay.

## Before You Borrow: Is a Loan the Right Move?

Before you approach any lender, answer three honest questions. First, what exactly will the money be used for? A loan works best for a specific, income-generating purpose: buying stock you know will sell, buying equipment that lets you serve more customers, or paying for a shop in a better location. It works badly for vague purposes such as "growing the business" or covering everyday spending that your sales should already cover.

Second, how will the loan pay for itself? Work out the numbers on paper. If you borrow one million naira to buy stock, how much will that stock sell for, and after all your costs, how much will be left to repay the loan and still leave you with something? If the answer is unclear, the loan is risky.

Third, can you repay even in a bad month? Business in Nigeria is not steady. A month can be slow because of rain, fuel scarcity, a road closure, or a quiet season. Choose a repayment amount you can still manage in a slow month, not only in your best month. If the monthly repayment only works when everything goes perfectly, it is too high.

If you cannot answer these three questions clearly, do not borrow yet. Spend a few weeks tightening your records and testing your numbers first. A lender will ask the same questions, and your answers will decide whether you are approved.

## Know the Main Loan Options in Nigeria

Nigerian small businesses have more loan options than many owners realise. Each option suits a different situation, and each has its own cost and requirements.

Commercial banks offer business loans, overdrafts and term loans. Their interest rates are usually lower than other lenders, but their requirements are stricter. They often ask for a registered business, a corporate bank account with a history of transactions, collateral such as property, and a good credit record. A bank loan suits an established business that can provide documents and wait through a longer approval process.

Microfinance banks are often a better fit for smaller businesses. They lend smaller amounts, sometimes starting from tens of thousands of naira, and they are used to traders, artisans and small shop owners. Their interest rates are usually higher than commercial banks, and repayment is often weekly or monthly over a shorter period. If you trade in a market in Onitsha, run a salon in Port Harcourt, or sell foodstuffs in Mile 12, a microfinance bank may know your type of business well.

Cooperative societies are another strong option. Many market associations, trade groups and staff cooperatives lend to their members at fair rates, often without the strict paperwork a bank demands. The catch is that you usually need to be a member for some time and to have saved regularly with the cooperative before you can borrow. If you belong to a traders' association or a cooperative, ask about their loan terms before you look elsewhere.

Online lenders and fintech lenders offer quick loans through an app, often with a decision in hours. They are convenient, but read the terms with great care. Some charge high interest and short repayment periods that can trap a borrower. Only use an online lender licensed by the Central Bank of Nigeria, and always calculate the total amount you will repay, not just the monthly figure.

Government and development programmes also exist. The Bank of Industry, the Development Bank of Nigeria, and programmes run through the Small and Medium Enterprises Development Agency of Nigeria, SMEDAN, support small businesses at different times, often at lower interest rates but with specific eligibility rules and longer processes. Check their official websites for current programmes rather than relying on rumours.

## What Lenders Actually Check

Lenders are not mysterious. They check the same handful of things, and you can prepare for each one.

They check that your business is real. Is it registered with the Corporate Affairs Commission, CAC? Do you have a shop, a stall, a website, or a verifiable place of business? Can they visit you or confirm your address? A business with no registration and no fixed address looks risky to a lender.

They check your sales and income. Your bank statements are the main evidence. Lenders look at how much money comes in regularly, whether sales are growing or falling, and whether the money in your account matches what you claim to earn. This is why keeping your business money in your business account matters. If most of your sales go through your personal account, or in cash that never enters a bank, the lender cannot see your real income.

They check your character and history. Have you borrowed before and repaid on time? Do you have a credit report with the credit bureaus in Nigeria? Lenders check your credit history, and a record of unpaid loans or unpaid bills makes approval much harder. If you have never borrowed before, that is not a problem by itself, but it means the lender will look even more carefully at your records.

They check your capacity to repay. This is a simple calculation: after your normal business costs and your household expenses, how much is left each month to service a loan? A lender will not approve a loan whose monthly repayment eats up everything you earn. Keep your requested amount modest and your repayment period realistic.

They check collateral and guarantees. Collateral is something valuable you pledge, such as landed property with a proper title, a vehicle, or equipment. If you cannot repay, the lender can claim it. Not all loans need collateral. Microfinance and some government loans use guarantors instead: people who promise to repay if you do not. Choose guarantors who know your business and trust you, and never ask someone to guarantee a loan you are not confident you can repay.

## The Documents Lenders Ask For

Gathering documents is where many applications stall. Prepare them before you apply, and the process moves much faster. The exact list varies by lender, but most will ask for most of the following.

Your CAC certificate and other registration documents prove your business exists. Your Tax Identification Number and tax clearance certificate show you are registered for tax and up to date. Your bank statements for the last six to twelve months show your sales pattern. Your National Identification Number, your passport photograph, and proof of address confirm who you are.

You will also need a short loan proposal: what your business does, what you will use the money for, how much you need, how it will earn a return, and how you will repay month by month. Include your monthly sales for the past year and your main costs. If you have collateral, bring the title documents or vehicle papers. If the lender asks for guarantors, bring their identification and photographs too. Keep copies of everything, and never pay an agent who promises to guarantee approval for a fee.

## How to Apply: A Step-by-Step Process

Step one is to decide your amount and purpose. Write them down in one sentence: "I need one million five hundred thousand naira to buy stock for the December season, and I will repay it over twelve months from sales." If you cannot write that sentence, you are not ready.

Step two is to compare at least three lenders. Do not take the first offer. Ask each lender for the interest rate, the total amount you will repay, the repayment schedule, any fees, and what happens if you pay late. Compare the total repayment, not just the monthly figure. A loan with a low monthly payment but a very long period can cost far more in total.

Step three is to prepare your documents and your proposal. Use the checklist in the previous section. Ask a trusted person, such as your accountant or a fellow business owner who has borrowed before, to read your proposal and point out gaps.

Step four is to submit your application and attend the interview or site visit. Be honest. If your sales dropped in a particular month, say so and explain why. Lenders expect real businesses to have ups and downs. Dishonesty is discovered quickly, and it ends the application.

Step five is to read the offer letter carefully before you sign. Check the interest rate, the total repayment, the repayment dates, the penalties for late payment, and whether the rate can change. If anything is unclear, ask the lender to explain it in plain language. Do not sign a document you do not understand.

Step six is to use the money exactly as planned. This is where discipline matters most. A loan for stock must buy stock, not a new phone or a party. Keep receipts for what you buy with the loan, and keep the lender's money in a separate mental account until the purchase is done.

## Understanding Interest, Fees and the True Cost

The interest rate is the headline number, but it is not the whole cost. Always work out the total amount you will repay from the first payment to the last, including every fee.

Ask whether the interest is calculated on a reducing balance or a flat rate. With a reducing balance, interest is charged only on the amount you still owe, so it falls as you repay. With a flat rate, interest is charged on the original amount for the whole period, which usually costs more. Two loans with the same stated rate can cost very different amounts depending on the method.

Ask about fees: processing fees, insurance, account maintenance charges, and late payment penalties. Ask what happens if you miss a payment by a few days, and what happens if you want to repay early. Some lenders charge a fee for early repayment, while others allow it freely. Repaying early can save you interest, so this detail matters.

Before you sign, write down the total repayment and ask whether the extra cost is worth what the loan will earn you. If you borrow one million naira and repay one million three hundred thousand naira, the loan costs three hundred thousand naira. Will the stock or equipment earn you more than that? If not, the loan is not worth it.

## Building Your Credit Record Before You Need a Loan

The best time to prepare for a loan is before you need one. Lenders trust a business with a visible track record, and you can build that record in ordinary months.

Keep your business money in your business bank account, and let your sales pass through it. A bank statement that shows regular deposits is powerful evidence. If your customers pay you by transfer, Opay, PalmPay or mobile money, make sure those receipts land in a traceable account rather than disappearing as cash.

Pay your small obligations on time: rent, supplier invoices, staff salaries, and tax. Lenders notice patterns, and a business that pays suppliers late every month looks like one that will pay a lender late too. Start small if you have never borrowed: a small loan from a microfinance bank or your cooperative, repaid on time, creates a positive credit record that makes the next loan easier. Keep your tax records clean, and register your business properly if you have not done so. A CAC registration, a Tax Identification Number, and a business bank account are the three foundations of borrowing.

## Repaying Without Stress: A Practical Plan

Getting the loan is only half the job. Repaying it on time, every time, is what protects your business and your reputation.

Set up a separate repayment habit from the day the money arrives. Open a simple routine: every week, move the repayment amount for that week into a separate account or wallet that you do not touch for anything else. When the repayment date arrives, the money is already there. This small habit prevents the most common repayment problem: spending the money on other things and then scrambling at the due date.

Pay early when you have a good week, and confirm with your lender that extra payments reduce your balance without a penalty. Talk to your lender early if trouble comes: if sales drop sharply, call before the repayment date, explain what happened, and ask for a short extension. Lenders prefer a borrower who communicates to one who disappears. Never borrow from one lender to repay another; that is the fastest route into a debt trap. Keep a simple loan tracker with the amount, each payment date, the amount paid, and the balance remaining. ## Mistakes That Get Applications Rejected

The most common reason for rejection is poor records: a business that cannot show six months of bank statements or sales records gives the lender nothing to assess. The second is asking for too much. Lenders approve amounts that match your income, so ask for what your sales can support. The third is an unclear purpose. "I want to expand" tells the lender nothing; be specific about what you will buy and why. The fourth is a bad credit history, including unpaid loans and bills, so settle small outstanding debts before you apply. The fifth is applying to many lenders at once, which can mark your credit record and make you look desperate. Apply to one or two at a time.

## Alternatives Worth Considering First

A loan is not always the best answer. If the need is not urgent, a disciplined savings plan may get you there without interest. A cooperative loan is often cheaper and more flexible than a bank loan, especially if you are already a member. Supplier credit can fund stock if you have a good payment history with that supplier, and a customer deposit of half the order value can reduce what you need to borrow. Grants from government programmes and business support organisations do not need to be repaid, but they are competitive and tied to a specific purpose. Check SMEDAN and the Bank of Industry for current opportunities, and be wary of anyone who asks you to pay a fee to access a grant.

## Your Loan Application Checklist

Work through this checklist before you visit any lender. First, write down the exact amount you need and what it will buy. Second, calculate how the loan will earn its cost back and what your monthly repayment will be. Third, gather your CAC documents, Tax Identification Number, bank statements for the last six to twelve months, and identification documents. Fourth, write a short loan proposal with your purpose, your sales history, and your repayment plan. Fifth, compare at least three lenders on total repayment, not just the monthly figure. Sixth, confirm whether you need collateral or guarantors, and arrange them in advance. Seventh, check your credit record and settle any small outstanding debts.

Take this list to your first meeting with a lender. It shows that you are serious and organised, and it makes the lender's job easier. Organised borrowers get faster decisions.

## Final Word: Borrow to Earn, Not to Survive

A business loan is a tool. Used well, it buys stock that sells, equipment that serves more customers, and space that brings in more income. Used badly, it buys things that do not earn, and leaves you paying for them long after they are gone.

Borrow only for a clear purpose, borrow an amount your sales can support, compare lenders on total cost, and repay on time every time. Build your records before you need the money, so that when opportunity arrives, you are ready for it. Start this week: organise your records and write down the one purpose a loan would serve.
