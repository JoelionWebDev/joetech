Every January, thousands of Nigerian business owners sit down and declare a big number. "This year I will make ₦10 million." "This year I will double my sales." The energy is real. The intention is good. And by March, most of those targets are forgotten, because nobody broke them down into something a person can actually do on a Tuesday morning.

A sales target that lives only in your head is a wish. A sales target that is written down, broken into daily actions, and checked every week is a plan. The difference between the two is the difference between businesses that grow and businesses that stay stuck at the same level year after year.

This guide will show you how to set sales targets your small business can actually hit in Nigeria. You will learn how to start from your real numbers, not from a random figure; how to break a monthly target into daily and weekly actions; how to handle the slow weeks that every Nigerian business faces; and how to review your targets without lying to yourself.

## Why Most Sales Targets Fail

Before you set a new target, it helps to understand why the old ones failed. Most targets fail for one of five reasons.

**The target was a guess.** "Let me target ₦2 million this month" sounds good, but if you made ₦400,000 last month, the jump has no basis. A target with no connection to your actual sales is not ambitious. It is fiction.

**The target was too big, too fast.** Growing from ₦500,000 a month to ₦5 million a month in thirty days is not a plan. It is a lottery ticket. Big jumps need big changes: new products, new customers, new marketing, or new staff. Without those changes, the number is unreachable.

**Nobody broke it into daily work.** A monthly target of ₦900,000 means nothing until you know what it means per day. If you work 26 days in the month, that is about ₦34,600 per day. Now you have something to aim at each morning. Without the daily number, you drift until the 25th of the month and then panic.

**Nobody tracked it.** A target you check once at the end of the month is useless. By then it is too late to fix anything. Targets need weekly, even daily, check-ins.

**Life happened and the plan had no room for it.** Fuel scarcity, a public holiday, a family emergency, a rainy week that kept customers at home, a network outage that killed your online sales for two days. Nigerian business is full of disruptions. A target that assumes a perfect month will fail in the first imperfect week.

Good targets avoid all five traps. Here is how to build one.

## Step 1: Start With Your Real Numbers

You cannot set a realistic target without knowing where you stand today. This step takes an hour, and it is the most important part of the process.

**Step 1a: Find your monthly sales for the last three months.** Open your records, your bank statements, your notebook, your POS reports, or your sales spreadsheet. Add up what you actually sold each month. If you do not have records, start keeping them today, and use your best honest estimate for now. A fashion designer in Ikeja might find she sold ₦420,000 in September, ₦510,000 in October, and ₦380,000 in November. That tells her the real range of her business.

**Step 1b: Calculate your average.** Add the three months together and divide by three. For our designer, that is ₦1,310,000 divided by 3, which is about ₦436,000 per month. That average is your baseline. It is the honest starting point for any target.

**Step 1c: Find your average order value.** Divide your total sales by the number of orders or customers. If the designer made ₦436,000 from 40 orders, her average order is about ₦10,900. This number is powerful, because it tells you exactly how many orders you need to hit any target. To reach ₦600,000 at ₦10,900 per order, she needs about 55 orders. Now the target is not a mystery. It is 55 orders.

**Step 1d: Find your best and worst months.** Your worst month shows you what happens when things go wrong. Your best month shows you what is possible when things go right. A realistic target sits somewhere above your average, within reach of your best month, and far below fantasy.

**Step 1e: Know your profit, not just your sales.** Sales of ₦1 million sound impressive, but if you spent ₦850,000 on stock, transport and other costs, your profit is ₦150,000. Set targets for both sales and profit. A business that chases sales while ignoring profit can grow itself into trouble.

If you have no records at all, do not let that stop you. Start tracking today. A simple notebook or a Google Sheet with date, customer, item, amount and payment status is enough. After thirty days you will have your first real baseline.

## Step 2: Choose a Target That Stretches You Without Breaking You

A good target is ambitious but believable. The standard advice is to aim for 10 to 25 percent above your current average. This range works well for Nigerian small businesses, because it demands real effort without demanding a miracle.

Here is how the maths works for different situations:

**If your average is ₦400,000 a month**, a 20 percent increase gives you a target of ₦480,000. That is ₦80,000 extra. At an average order of ₦10,000, that is eight more orders in the month, or roughly two extra orders per week. That is a target you can picture yourself hitting.

**If your average is ₦1,500,000 a month**, a 15 percent increase gives you ₦1,725,000. That is ₦225,000 extra. It might mean ten more corporate clients, or one new product line, or a small advertising spend that brings in new customers.

**If you are just starting out**, your target will be based on effort rather than history. Set a target for activity: twenty customer conversations a week, ten quotes sent, five new contacts a day. Sales will follow activity, and after two or three months you will have real numbers to base targets on.

**If December or a festive season is coming**, plan for it honestly. A caterer in Lagos knows December is her biggest month. Her December target should be higher than her August target, because demand is genuinely higher. A school supplies seller knows September is big. Seasonal targets should follow seasonal demand, not wishful thinking.

Avoid the trap of copying someone else's target. A competitor's ₦5 million month means nothing to you if your business is at a different stage, in a different area, with different costs. Your target should be based on your numbers, your market and your capacity.

## Step 3: Break the Target Into Daily and Weekly Actions

This is the step that turns a number on paper into money in your account. A monthly target is too big to act on directly. Break it down until it becomes something you can do today.

**Monthly to weekly.** Divide your monthly target by four. A target of ₦600,000 becomes ₦150,000 per week. Check every Sunday night whether the week hit ₦150,000. If it did not, you still have three weeks to catch up.

**Weekly to daily.** Divide your weekly target by the number of days you work. If you work six days a week, ₦150,000 becomes ₦25,000 per day. Now you know your daily number. Every morning, you know what "a good day" looks like.

**Daily sales to orders.** Divide your daily target by your average order value. If your daily target is ₦25,000 and your average order is ₦5,000, you need five orders a day. Five orders is a concrete goal. You can plan your day around it: post on WhatsApp status in the morning, follow up with three old customers at noon, run a small ad in the evening.

**Orders to conversations.** Not every conversation becomes a sale. If one in five people who ask about your product actually buys, and you need five orders a day, you need twenty-five conversations a day. Now you know exactly how much outreach your target requires. This is where most targets are won or lost, because outreach is the work that fills the pipeline.

**Write the daily numbers where you can see them.** Put them on a whiteboard, on a card on your desk, or as the wallpaper on your phone. A target you see every morning is a target you remember. A target buried in a notebook is a target you forget.

## Step 4: Track Your Progress Every Week

A target without tracking is a decoration. Set up a simple tracking system and use it without fail.

**Use a simple spreadsheet.** Google Sheets is free and works on any phone. Create a sheet with columns for date, target for the day, actual sales for the day, and a running total for the month. Fill it in every evening. It takes two minutes. At the end of each week, compare your running total with where you should be. If you are behind, you know early enough to act.

**Hold a weekly review, even if it is just you.** Every Sunday evening, or Monday morning, spend fifteen minutes answering three questions: Did I hit this week's target? If not, why? What will I do differently next week? Write the answers down. A pattern will appear. Maybe your slow days are always Tuesdays. Maybe your best sales come from WhatsApp status, not Instagram. Maybe customers buy more when you post in the morning. These insights are worth more than any business book.

**Celebrate small wins.** If you hit your weekly target, acknowledge it. Buy yourself a good lunch. Tell your team. Small celebrations keep motivation alive through a long month. A target is a marathon, and nobody finishes a marathon without encouragement along the way.

**Do not hide from bad weeks.** A bad week is information, not a failure. If you missed your target because a supplier delayed your stock, the lesson is to order earlier. If you missed it because you posted nothing for four days, the lesson is that marketing is not optional. Write it down and adjust.

## Step 5: Adjust When Reality Changes

No plan survives contact with reality unchanged, and that is fine. Adjusting a target is not quitting. It is managing.

**Adjust when something big changes.** If you add a new product, open a new branch, or lose a major customer, your target should change to match. A target set in January may not fit your business in June.

**Adjust for the season.** A December target for a gift seller should be higher. A January target, after the festive spending, should be lower. Planning for the season is smart, not weak.

**Adjust in small steps.** If you are far behind by the second week of the month, do not panic and do not slash the target to zero. Ask what is realistic for the remaining two weeks, set a revised target for the rest of the month, and note why the original target was missed. The note matters more than the number, because it stops the same mistake next month.

**Never adjust a target to make yourself feel better.** There is a difference between a sensible revision and an excuse. If you missed the target because you did not do the work, the honest response is to do the work, not to lower the target. Be honest with yourself in your weekly review.

## What to Do When You Keep Missing Your Targets

If you miss your target three months in a row, something is wrong, and it is usually one of four things.

**The target is too high.** Your baseline may have been wrong, or the market may have changed. Fuel price increases, for example, can reduce customers' spending power across the whole economy. If your target assumed last year's conditions, update it for this year's reality.

**The activity is too low.** You set a target of ₦600,000 but you only spoke to five new people all month. The maths cannot work. Increase the activity first, then judge the target.

**The price is too low.** If you are selling a lot but earning little, your problem is pricing, not volume. A designer who sews twenty dresses at ₦8,000 each earns ₦160,000. The same designer sewing fifteen dresses at ₦15,000 each earns ₦225,000 with less work. Review your prices every quarter.

**The product or market is wrong.** If you have done the activity, set fair prices, and still cannot hit the target, the market may be telling you something. Maybe the area is saturated. Maybe customers want a different product. Maybe your shop is in the wrong location. This is hard to hear, but it is better to hear it in three months than in three years.

## Getting Your Team Behind the Target

If you have staff, your target is their target too. Share it with them, and make it simple enough for everyone to understand.

**Explain the daily number.** "We need ₦25,000 a day" is something a sales assistant can understand and act on. "We need to grow revenue" is not.

**Give the team a role.** The person on the shop floor can upsell. The person on WhatsApp can follow up with old customers. The rider can mention the new product on delivery. Everyone should know one thing they can do to help hit the number.

**Reward the team when the target is hit.** A small bonus, a shared meal, or a half-day off costs little and builds a team that cares about the number. A team that shares in the win will work harder for the next one.

**Be honest when it is missed.** Do not blame the team for a target that was unrealistic. Review the numbers together, find the real cause, and fix it together.

## Your Sales Target Checklist

- [ ] Add up your real sales for the last three months and find your average
- [ ] Calculate your average order value and your monthly profit, not just sales
- [ ] Set a monthly target 10 to 25 percent above your average
- [ ] Break the target into weekly, daily, order and conversation numbers
- [ ] Write the daily number where you can see it every morning
- [ ] Track actual sales every evening in a simple spreadsheet
- [ ] Hold a fifteen-minute weekly review every Sunday or Monday
- [ ] Adjust the target when seasons or big changes demand it, and note why
- [ ] Review your prices every quarter to make sure the target is profitable
- [ ] Share the target with your team and reward them when it is hit

A sales target is not a number you announce once and forget. It is a daily habit: a number in the morning, work during the day, and an honest check in the evening. Do that for three months, and you will know your business better than most business owners ever will. The target will stop being a guess, and it will start being a tool that actually moves your income forward.
