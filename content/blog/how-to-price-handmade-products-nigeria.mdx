If you make handmade products in Nigeria — beaded bags, Ankara dresses, scented candles, handmade soap, crochet wear, leather slippers, cakes, hair bonnets, resin art or home decor — you have probably asked yourself this question: am I charging too little, or too much?

Many talented makers undercharge. They price by guessing, or by copying a competitor, or by charging only for materials and forgetting their time. The result is the same: they sell a lot, work long hours, and still have no money left at the end of the month. Others overcharge without explaining the value, so customers admire the work on Instagram and buy nothing.

Pricing is not guesswork. It is a simple calculation based on your real costs, your time, your market and the value your customer receives. This guide shows you how to price handmade products in Nigeria step by step, with naira examples you can copy and adapt to your own craft.

## 1. Why Most Handmade Sellers Undercharge

The most common pricing mistake is forgetting to pay yourself. If you spend six hours making a beaded handbag and charge only for beads and thread, you have worked for free. Your time is a cost, even if you enjoy the work.

Another mistake is comparing yourself to mass-produced imports. A handmade Ankara tote bag is not the same as a factory-made bag from China. Yours is unique, made to order, and often made with better finishing. Customers who want the cheapest bag will buy the import. Your customer is the person who wants something special and is willing to pay for it.

Finally, many makers do not track costs at all. They buy materials in bits, forget transport and packaging, and never add up what a product truly costs. Without that number, any price is a guess. The first step to better pricing is knowing your true cost per product.

## 2. Know Your True Cost Per Product

Before you set a price, calculate what one product costs you to make. Write it down for each product, and update it when material prices change, which they do often in Nigeria.

Start with materials. List everything that goes into one product: fabric, beads, thread, glue, zips, lining, labels, candles wax, fragrance oil, soap base, shea butter, or flour and sugar for a cake. Use the actual price you paid, not the price you wish you paid. If a roll of Ankara costs ₦12,000 and makes four bags, each bag uses ₦3,000 of fabric.

Add consumables and packaging. This includes thread, needles, glue sticks, gift boxes, nylon bags, ribbons, stickers and thank-you cards. Packaging may seem small, but ₦500 here and ₦800 there adds up across 50 orders.

Add transport and delivery costs. If you spend ₦2,000 on Bolt or bus fare to buy materials for ten products, add ₦200 to each one. If you pay a dispatch rider ₦1,500 to deliver an order in Lagos, decide whether delivery is included in your price or charged separately, and state it clearly.

Add your labour. Decide your hourly rate and multiply it by the hours a product takes. If your target is ₦2,000 per hour and a crochet top takes five hours, your labour cost is ₦10,000. This is not profit; it is your wage for the work.

Add overheads. These are the shared costs of running your business: phone data, electricity or generator fuel, market stall rent, Instagram ads, and wear on your sewing machine or tools. Divide your monthly overhead by the number of products you make in a month, and add that share to each product. If your overhead is ₦30,000 a month and you make 30 products, add ₦1,000 to each one.

## 3. Use a Simple Pricing Formula

Once you know your cost, use a formula that guarantees profit. A reliable starting point for handmade products is:

Price = (Materials + Labour + Overheads) x 2

Or, if you prefer to think in margins, add a profit margin of 40 to 60 percent on top of your total cost. The exact margin depends on your niche, your skill level and how much demand you have.

Here is a worked example. Ada makes handmade scented candles in Abuja. Each candle costs ₦2,500 in wax, fragrance, wick and jar, ₦1,500 in labour for one hour, and ₦500 in packaging and overheads. Her total cost is ₦4,500. Using the times-two formula, her price is ₦9,000. That leaves ₦4,500 profit per candle, which pays for her time, her marketing and her growth.

Another example: Tunde makes leather slippers in Lagos. Materials cost ₦6,000, labour is ₦4,000 for two hours, and overheads are ₦1,000. Total cost is ₦11,000. At times two, the price is ₦22,000. If similar slippers sell for ₦15,000 in the market, Tunde needs to decide whether his quality, design and brand justify ₦22,000, or whether he can reduce costs or target a different customer.

If your calculated price is far above what customers will pay, do not simply slash it and work for free. Instead, reduce costs, simplify the design, use a cheaper but still good material, or target customers who value handmade quality. Your price should reflect your cost and your value, not your fear.

## 4. Research What Customers Will Actually Pay

Your costs set your minimum price. Your market sets your realistic price. You need both numbers before you decide.

Look at competitors who sell similar handmade products in Nigeria, not just anyone selling the same item. Check Instagram shops, Jumia, Konga, Etsy sellers who ship to Nigeria, and market stalls in Balogun, Wuse or Onitsha. Note their prices, their quality, their packaging and their delivery time. If three good sellers charge ₦18,000 to ₦25,000 for a handmade tote bag, that is your market range.

Ask your customers directly. When someone enquires on WhatsApp, note what they expect to pay. Run a poll on your Instagram Stories: "Would you pay ₦15,000 for this handmade bag?" Test different prices with small batches. If a product sells out in a day, your price may be too low. If nobody buys after a week of good marketing, your price may be too high, or your photos and description may not show the value.

Consider your customer groups. A student in Ibadan, a working professional in Lekki, and a bride ordering aso-ebi souvenirs have different budgets. You can serve more than one group with different products: a simple version at ₦8,000, a standard version at ₦15,000, and a premium version at ₦30,000. This is called tiered pricing, and it helps customers choose without leaving your shop.

## 5. Price for Different Sales Channels

The same product may need a different price on WhatsApp, Instagram, Jumia and a physical market stall, because each channel has different costs.

On WhatsApp and Instagram, you control the price and keep most of the money, but you pay for your own marketing, data and delivery. This is usually your most profitable channel, so keep your best prices here and reward direct buyers with a small discount or free gift.

On Jumia and Konga, the platform takes a commission, often 10 to 20 percent, and may charge for logistics and advertising. Add that commission to your price on those platforms, or you will lose money on every sale. If your WhatsApp price is ₦15,000, your Jumia price may need to be ₦17,500 to leave you the same profit.

For wholesale orders, offer a lower price per unit, but only if the order is large enough to save you time and marketing costs. For example, if a shop in Lagos wants 50 handmade soaps, you might offer 15 percent off your retail price, with payment of at least 50 percent upfront. Never offer wholesale prices for a single item, or you will train customers to expect a discount every time.

## 6. Use Psychological Pricing and Clear Packages

How you present a price matters as much as the number itself. Small changes in presentation can make a fair price feel more attractive.

Use round numbers for premium products and charm prices for everyday ones. A handmade bridal bouquet at ₦50,000 feels premium and simple. A handmade soap at ₦4,950 feels like a careful bargain. In Nigeria, many customers prefer round numbers for bank transfers, so ₦15,000 is easier to pay than ₦14,750. Test what works for your customers.

Offer bundles. Instead of selling one candle for ₦9,000, offer a set of three for ₦24,000. The customer saves ₦3,000, and you sell three products in one order, saving on delivery and marketing. Bundles work well for soaps, candles, gift sets, baby items and party souvenirs.

Show the value, not just the price. In your product description, list what the customer gets: materials used, size, colours available, how long it takes to make, and how to care for it. A photo of you making the product, or a short video of the process, helps customers understand why handmade costs more than factory-made.

## 7. Handle Discounts, Haggling and Free Work

Nigerian customers love to haggle, and handmade sellers often give in too quickly. A discount given too easily teaches customers that your first price was not honest. Protect your price with clear rules.

Set a discount policy and stick to it. For example, offer 10 percent off for first-time buyers, or a free gift with orders above ₦30,000, or a loyalty discount after five orders. Do not offer a discount just because someone asks. Instead, offer value: "I cannot reduce the price, but I can include a free gift bag and faster delivery."

When a customer says "last price," stay polite and firm. You can say: "This is handmade to order, and the price covers quality materials and my time. I can offer you the smaller size for ₦12,000 instead of ₦18,000." That gives them a choice without cutting your profit to zero.

Never do free work for exposure, unless you have a clear written agreement about what you will get in return. A celebrity or influencer who promises exposure should agree in advance to post on a specific date, tag your shop, and share your WhatsApp link. Even then, limit free work to one product, not an ongoing supply.

For custom orders, always collect a deposit of 50 to 70 percent before you start. Custom products cannot be resold easily if the customer changes their mind, so the deposit protects your materials and time. State this in writing on WhatsApp before you begin.

## 8. Add Delivery, Payment and After-Sales Costs

A price that ignores delivery and payment costs will lose you money, especially in Lagos and Abuja where transport is expensive and traffic is unpredictable.

Decide who pays for delivery and say so before the customer orders. Many Nigerian sellers charge delivery separately, based on location. For example, delivery within Lekki might be ₦2,000, to Ikeja ₦3,500, and to Abuja from Lagos ₦5,000 or more by interstate courier. Use a dispatch rider or a courier like GIG Logistics, DHL or a trusted local rider, and confirm the fee before you quote the customer.

If you offer free delivery, build the average delivery cost into your product price. If your average delivery costs ₦2,000 and you sell 20 products a month, that is ₦40,000. Either add ₦2,000 to each product or set a minimum order for free delivery, such as free delivery on orders above ₦25,000 within Lagos.

Keep a small fund for mistakes. If one product in twenty needs a remake, that cost should come from your profit margin, not from your personal savings. Good pricing leaves room for this.

## 9. Raise Your Prices Without Losing Customers

As your skill, demand and costs grow, your prices should grow too. Many makers keep the same prices for years while material prices double, and then wonder why they are struggling.

Raise prices in small steps, not sudden jumps. If you charge ₦15,000 now, move to ₦17,500, then to ₦20,000 over a few months, rather than jumping straight to ₦25,000. Customers accept gradual increases more easily, especially if quality improves at the same time.

Give customers notice. Post on your WhatsApp Status and Instagram: "From 1 December, our prices will change slightly because material costs have increased. Order before then to enjoy current prices." This is honest, gives loyal customers a chance to order early, and often brings a rush of orders.

Improve something visible when you raise prices. Better packaging, faster delivery, a free thank-you card, or a new design makes the increase feel justified. Customers pay more happily when they can see what the extra money buys.

Track what happens after a price rise. If sales stay steady, your new price is right. If sales fall sharply, check whether the problem is price, or whether your photos, marketing or delivery time need work. Do not panic and cut prices after one slow week; give a new price at least two to three weeks to settle.

## 10. Track Your Numbers and Review Every Quarter

Pricing is not a one-time decision. It is a habit. Set aside one hour every three months to review your prices and your profits.

For each product, write down your current cost, your current price, how many you sold, and your profit per product. If a product sells well but makes little profit, raise its price or reduce its cost. If a product makes good profit but sells slowly, improve your photos, your description or your marketing before you cut the price.

Keep a simple record in a notebook or a Google Sheet. Columns can be: product name, materials cost, labour cost, overheads, total cost, selling price, profit per unit, units sold, and total profit. At the end of the month, add up your total profit and compare it with your target. If you wanted ₦150,000 profit and made ₦80,000, you need more sales, higher prices or lower costs.

If you sell on Instagram, use WhatsApp Business labels to track who bought what, who paid, and who is waiting for delivery. Good records prevent mix-ups and help you follow up with customers for repeat orders.

## 11. Real Pricing Examples for Common Handmade Products

Examples help more than theory, so here are realistic starting points for popular handmade products in Nigeria. Adjust them to your own costs and city, and always calculate your own numbers before you copy a price.

A handmade Ankara tote bag in Lagos might sell for ₦15,000 to ₦25,000, depending on size, lining and finishing. Materials might cost ₦5,000 to ₦8,000, labour ₦4,000 to ₦6,000, and packaging and overheads ₦1,000 to ₦2,000. At ₦20,000, the maker keeps a healthy profit and can afford good photos and delivery.

A set of four handmade scented candles might sell for ₦28,000 to ₦36,000 as a gift set. Single candles might sell for ₦7,500 to ₦9,500. Gift sets are good for birthdays, weddings and corporate gifts, and they often sell better in November and December.

A handmade natural soap bar might sell for ₦3,500 to ₦5,500, while a gift set of three soaps with a sponge and a small towel might sell for ₦12,000 to ₦15,000. Soap is a low-price product, so you need volume or gift sets to make good profit.

A custom birthday cake for 20 people in Lagos might sell for ₦45,000 to ₦85,000, depending on design, flavours and layers. Cake makers must include ingredients, gas or electricity, baking time, decorating time, the cake box and delivery. A cake that looks cheap but took eight hours to decorate is underpriced.

Use these examples as a guide, not a rule. Your price must cover your costs in your city, with your materials and your time. If your numbers say ₦22,000 and the example says ₦18,000, trust your numbers.

## Conclusion

Pricing handmade products in Nigeria is a skill you can learn, and it gets easier with practice. Start by knowing your true cost per product, including materials, labour, packaging, transport and overheads. Use a simple formula to set a price that leaves you a profit. Check what customers will pay, price differently for different channels, and present your price with clear packages and honest value.

Do not be afraid to charge what your work is worth. Customers who value handmade quality will pay a fair price when they understand what goes into it. The customers who only want the cheapest price were never your customers anyway.

Review your prices every three months, raise them in small steps as your costs and skills grow, and keep simple records so you always know which products make you money. Your hands make beautiful products. Your pricing should make sure those products also build a business that pays you well and lasts.
