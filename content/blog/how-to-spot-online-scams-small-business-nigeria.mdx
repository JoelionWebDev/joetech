If you run a small business in Nigeria, you are already a target. Whether you sell clothes in Lekki, run a restaurant in Ikeja, supply building materials in Abuja, or sell hair from your WhatsApp status in Port Harcourt, scammers are watching. They know how you work: you are busy, you answer your own phone, you want every sale, and you often decide quickly without a team to double-check you.

That is what fraudsters count on. They do not need to hack a bank. They only need one rushed moment — one fake transfer alert you believe, one supplier you pay before confirming, one link you click while serving a customer — and your money is gone.

This guide explains the scams that target Nigerian small businesses most often, what to do in the first minutes after you suspect fraud, and how to build simple habits that protect your business every day. You do not need expensive software. You need awareness, a few clear rules, and the discipline to follow them even when a customer says they are in a hurry.

## Why Small Businesses Are Easy Targets

Large companies have finance departments and fraud teams. A small business in Lagos or Onitsha often has one person doing everything: taking orders on WhatsApp, confirming payments, arranging delivery, and posting on Instagram. That speed is your advantage, but it is also your weak point.

Many Nigerian small businesses operate mainly on WhatsApp and Instagram. There is no shopping cart with automatic payment confirmation. You rely on screenshots, trust, and voice notes. That system works well with honest customers, but it is easy to fake.

Scammers also know that data is expensive and time is short. You may be using one phone for business and personal life, checking messages between customers or on a bus. Fraudsters design their messages to look urgent and believable at a glance.

When sales are slow, a big order feels like a blessing. A customer who wants twenty pieces at once, or a supplier offering goods far cheaper than the market price, is tempting. Scammers offer exactly what you want to hear, then rush you before you can think. Many owners are also embarrassed to report fraud, so the same criminals move on to the next shop with the same trick. Talking openly about scams is part of the protection.

## The Fake Payment Alert Scam

This is the most common scam facing Nigerian sellers today, especially on WhatsApp, Instagram, and Facebook. A customer places an order, says they have transferred the money, and sends a screenshot of a debit alert. It looks real. The name, amount, and time match. You pack the goods and send them by dispatch rider. Later you check your bank app properly and discover no money ever arrived.

The screenshot was edited. With free apps, anyone can create a fake alert in minutes. Some scammers send a fake SMS that looks like it came from your bank. Others use a real transfer that they quickly reverse, or money from a stolen account that the bank later flags.

Protect yourself with four rules. First, never release goods based on a screenshot alone. Confirm the payment in your own bank app or by dialling your bank USSD code. Open the app yourself. Wait until you see the credit in your available balance, not just a notification.

Second, agree a clear payment rule and stick to it: payment must reflect before dispatch, no exceptions. Put this rule in your WhatsApp bio, your Instagram highlights, and your automated reply. Honest customers will understand. Scammers will disappear.

Third, watch for pressure. A real customer who has paid will wait calmly. A scammer will say, "My rider is already outside," or "I have sent it three times, check well." Urgency is the weapon. Slow down on purpose.

Fourth, keep a simple payment log: customer name, amount, date, and time. If you use Paystack or Flutterwave, use a proper payment link for each order. It confirms payment automatically and is much harder to fake than a screenshot.

## The Overpayment and Refund Trick

A customer deliberately pays more than the price — for example, ₦150,000 for goods worth ₦80,000 — then asks you to refund the ₦70,000 balance to a different account, claiming it was a mistake.

What really happens is that the first payment was made with a stolen card, a hacked account, or a fake transfer that will bounce. You refund real money from your own account, and then the original payment is reversed. You lose both the goods and the refund.

Never refund an overpayment to a different account, and never refund in a hurry. Tell the customer you will confirm the payment has fully cleared, which can take a full working day, and that any refund will go back to the exact same account that paid you. A genuine customer will accept that. A scammer will argue or vanish. The same caution applies to customers who ask you to buy something extra for them or send money to a dispatch rider. Your business is not a bank.

## Fake Suppliers and Cheap Goods Offers

You will see them in WhatsApp groups, Facebook Marketplace, Instagram, and Telegram: a supplier offering bales of clothes, phones, hair, or shoes at half the normal price. The pictures look professional. The testimonials look convincing. You pay a deposit or the full amount. Then the excuses start. The goods are on the way. The driver had an accident. Then the number stops answering and the page disappears.

Never pay a new supplier in full before you have verified them. Start with a small test order you can afford to lose, or pay on delivery where possible. If they insist on full payment upfront for a first order, treat that as a warning sign.

Verify the business beyond its Instagram page. Ask for a CAC registration name, a physical shop address, and a video call where they show you actual stock — not just photos. Search the business name on Google and ask for two real customers you can call. A genuine supplier will not be offended.

Visit in person if the amount is large. If the supplier claims to be in Onitsha Main Market, Trade Fair, or Computer Village, go there or send someone you trust. Transport costs far less than losing ₦200,000 or ₦500,000 to a ghost supplier. Be suspicious of prices that make no business sense. If everyone sells a phone for ₦180,000 and one seller offers it for ₦95,000, that is not a bargain. Keep a list of verified suppliers and reorder from them. Loyalty to an honest supplier is cheaper than a cheap scam.

## Cloned Websites and Fake Pages

Scammers clone the websites and social media pages of real businesses. They copy your logo, photos, prices, and reviews, then create a nearly identical page with a slightly different username or web address. If your handle is @joetech_ng, theirs might be @joetech__ng. If your site is joetech360.ng, theirs might be joetech360.com.ng.

Customers find the fake page first, pay the scammer, and then come to you angry when nothing arrives. Your reputation suffers even though you did nothing wrong.

Reduce this risk by keeping your contact details consistent everywhere: the same phone number, WhatsApp link, and website on your Instagram bio, Facebook page, Google Business Profile, and flyers. Tell customers: "We only receive payments into this one account."

Claim and verify your Google Business Profile so customers see your real address and reviews when they search. Search for your own business name once a month on Google, Instagram, and Facebook. If you find a fake page, report it for impersonation, warn your customers, and keep screenshots.

Use a proper website with your own domain, not only social media. A website with secure payment through Paystack or Flutterwave is harder to clone than an Instagram page, and it gives customers one trusted place to verify you. If a customer says they paid a fake account, do not argue. Explain clearly that it was not your account, show your official details, and help them report the fake page.

## Phishing Links and WhatsApp Hacking

Phishing is when a criminal sends a message that looks like it came from your bank, WhatsApp, or a delivery company, with a link to click. The link opens a page that looks like the real login page. You enter your password or OTP, and the criminal now controls your account.

Common examples include: "Your account will be blocked, click here to verify," "You have won a giveaway, claim your prize here," and "Your parcel is waiting, pay ₦2,500 for delivery." They create panic so you click without thinking.

Never click a link in a message to log into your bank or social media. Open the app yourself or type the address yourself. Never share your OTP, PIN, or password with anyone. Real banks will never ask for your OTP or PIN on a call or chat.

Turn on two-factor authentication everywhere: WhatsApp, Instagram, Facebook, Gmail, and your bank app. On WhatsApp, set a two-step verification PIN. Even if someone gets your OTP once, they still cannot take over your account without the PIN. Keep that PIN somewhere safe.

Avoid logging into your bank on free Wi-Fi in hotels or malls. Use mobile data instead. Keep your phone locked, and do not leave it unlocked on a shop counter.

If your WhatsApp is hacked, act fast. Tell your contacts immediately not to send money to any new account shared from your number. Reinstall WhatsApp, re-verify, warn customers on your other channels, and report to your bank and the police with screenshots if money was lost.

## Job, Loan and Grant Scams

Some scams target you directly as a business owner looking for capital or staff. You may see: "You have been selected for a ₦500,000 federal grant, pay ₦15,000 processing fee," "CBN loan approved, pay to release your funds," or "Work-from-home job, earn ₦150,000 weekly, pay for training first."

The pattern is always the same: a big promise, a small upfront fee, and pressure to pay now. Real grants do not ask you to pay a fee into a personal account before you receive money. Real employers do not ask job seekers to pay for training. Real lenders explain interest, repayment, and terms in writing before you pay anything.

Check the source before you respond. Visit the official website by typing the address yourself. Call the organisation on the number listed on its official website, not the number in the message. Search the programme name plus "scam" on Google. If the only evidence is a forwarded broadcast and a personal account number, walk away.

The same caution applies to people who offer to boost followers or get you verified for a fee paid upfront. Ask for a written agreement, a business account for payment, and proof of past work you can verify. Never pay the full amount before any work is done.

## What to Do in the First Hour After Fraud

If you think you have been scammed, speed matters, but panic does not help.

1. Stop all further payments. Do not send more money to "complete" a transaction or unlock goods. That is how a small loss becomes a big one.
2. Take screenshots of everything: the chat, receipt, account number, phone number, website link, and profile page. Do not delete the conversation.
3. Call your bank immediately and report the transaction. Ask them to flag the receiving account. Get a reference number for your complaint.
4. Report the number and page to WhatsApp, Instagram, or Facebook for fraud, and report a fake website to Google Safe Browsing.
5. Report to the police and the Economic and Financial Crimes Commission, with your screenshots, bank statement, and a written summary. Even if the money is not recovered, your report helps build a case.
6. Warn your customers. Post a short warning on your WhatsApp status and stories with the number or account to avoid and your only official payment details. You are protecting your community, not advertising a mistake.

## Daily Habits That Keep Your Business Safe

Separate your business money from your personal money. Use a dedicated business account for payments. That makes it easier to spot a missing payment and harder for a scammer to drain your personal savings.

Create a payment checklist and post it where you work: open the bank app yourself, see the credit in the balance, match the amount and sender name, only then pack and dispatch. Train any staff or family helper to follow the same checklist, with no exceptions for "urgent" customers.

Use strong, different passwords for your business email, WhatsApp, Instagram, and bank apps. Do not use your date of birth or business name as a password. Limit who can move money. Do not share your bank login, OTP, or POS PIN with staff. Keep the final payment check with you or one trusted person.

Keep your apps updated, because updates often fix security holes. Talk to your customers about safety. Add a line to receipts and your WhatsApp catalogue: "We only receive payment into this account. Please confirm with us on this number before you pay anyone else."

Review your accounts weekly. Spend thirty minutes every Friday checking bank statements, your Paystack or Flutterwave dashboard, and social media logins for strange locations or messages you did not send. Early detection saves money.

Train your staff in fifteen minutes: never release goods on a screenshot alone, never share an OTP, and never pay a supplier or refund a customer without checking with you first. Show them a fake alert beside a real one. Make it normal to ask, "This customer is rushing me, can you check this payment?" That question is worth more than any software.

## Building Trust So Customers Choose You

The best defence against scams is trust. When customers trust you, they will check with you before paying and report fake pages to you.

Be consistent with your name, phone number, and account details. Reply promptly and deliver what you promised. Share photos and videos of your real shop, products, and team. Customers who have seen your face and shop are less likely to be fooled by a fake page.

Collect genuine reviews and display them on Google. Ask happy customers for a review or a short video testimonial. Be transparent about prices, delivery fees, and your refund policy in writing before a customer pays. A customer who knows your exact process will notice when a scammer offers a different price or account. Stay calm when a scam attempt happens. A short warning — "This number is not ours. Please only pay into our official account" — protects your customers and your brand.

## Conclusion: Slow Down, Verify, Then Sell

Scammers succeed because they make you act fast. Your best protection is the opposite: slow down, verify, then sell. Check the payment in your own bank app. Verify a supplier before you pay. Type a website address yourself instead of clicking a link. Ask for proof before you refund. Report fraud quickly and warn others.

You built your business with hard work and money you could not afford to lose. Do not let one rushed moment undo it. Share this guide with your staff, your market association, and your customers. The more people who know these tricks, the harder it becomes for scammers to find a victim. Start with the basics: a proper website on your own domain, a verified Google Business Profile, and a clear payment process your customers can follow.
