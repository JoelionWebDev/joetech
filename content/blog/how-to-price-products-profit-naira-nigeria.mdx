Most Nigerian small businesses do not have a sales problem. They have a pricing problem. A fashion vendor in Lekki sells twenty dresses a week and still cannot pay rent. A food seller in Surulere is busy from morning to night and still borrows money to restock. A skincare brand in Abuja runs adverts every day, gets orders every day, and ends the month with an empty account. The common thread is price: too low, costs never added up properly, or copied from a competitor with completely different costs.

Pricing in naira is harder than it looks because costs keep moving. A bag of flour that cost ₦45,000 in January can cost ₦62,000 by June, so a price that made sense three months ago can quietly become a loss today. This guide gives you a method you can repeat every time your costs change.

## Start With Your True Cost, Not Your Guess

Every pricing mistake starts here. Most sellers know the cost of the main item and forget everything else. If you buy a dress for ₦12,000 and sell it for ₦20,000, you think you made ₦8,000. You did not. You also paid for transport to the market, packaging, the nylon bag, the thank-you card, the delivery rider or fuel, the bank transfer charge, the advert that brought the customer, and the time you spent answering messages. Add those up and the real profit may be ₦3,000, or nothing at all.

Sit down with a notebook or a spreadsheet and list every cost attached to one unit of what you sell. Use these five buckets:

1. **Product cost:** what you pay your supplier, including any import duty, clearing cost or market levy.
2. **Getting it to you:** transport, fuel, dispatch rider, waybill, or delivery from your supplier in Onitsha, Aba, Lagos Island or China.
3. **Getting it ready:** packaging, labels, printing, sewing adjustments, cooking gas, electricity, data, and any material you use to finish the product.
4. **Getting the customer:** advert spend divided by the number of orders, WhatsApp Business costs, influencer payment, market stall rent share, or the cost of the free sample you gave away.
5. **Getting it to the customer:** delivery fee you absorb, rider cost, packaging for dispatch, and any refund or replacement you had to cover.

Work it out with a real example. Suppose you sell handmade body butter. Your shea butter, oils, fragrance and jar cost ₦3,200 per jar. Transport and market runs add ₦200 per jar. Your label, seal and branded bag add ₦350. Adverts bring in one order for every ₦900 spent, so that is ₦900 per jar. Delivery within Lagos costs ₦1,500 and you charge the customer ₦1,000, so you absorb ₦500. Your true cost is ₦3,200 + ₦200 + ₦350 + ₦900 + ₦500, which is ₦5,150. If you sell that jar for ₦6,000, your profit is ₦850, not ₦2,800. If you sell it for ₦5,500, you are almost working for free. If a customer begs for a discount to ₦5,000, you are paying them to take your product.

Do this exercise for your best-selling product today. Most owners who do it for the first time discover that two or three of their products lose money.

## The Three Pricing Methods That Actually Work in Nigeria

There are three honest ways to set a price. Most Nigerian SMEs should use a mix of all three, not just one.

**Cost-plus pricing.** This is the foundation. Take your true cost and add your target margin. If your true cost is ₦5,150 and you want a 40 percent margin on the selling price, divide the cost by 0.6. That gives ₦8,583, so you round to ₦8,500 or ₦9,000. The formula matters because many sellers add 40 percent to the cost instead, which gives ₦7,210, a much smaller margin than they intended. Learn the difference. Margin on price is what protects you when costs rise; markup on cost is what quietly shrinks your profit.

**Competitor-based pricing.** Look at what others charge, but do it properly. Pick five direct competitors selling to the same customer in the same city. Note their prices, their quality, their delivery speed, their packaging and their reviews. If four of them sell a similar Ankara dress for ₦28,000 to ₦35,000 and you sell yours for ₦15,000, you are not being generous. You are telling customers your dress is worth less, and you are probably losing money. If you charge ₦45,000, you need a clear reason: better fabric, better finishing, faster delivery, or a stronger brand. Competitor pricing tells you the range the market accepts. It does not tell you your price. Your costs tell you your price.

**Value-based pricing.** This is where real profit lives. A customer does not buy a cake. She buys a birthday that her child will remember, a cake that arrives on time, and the relief of not worrying about it. A client does not buy a logo. He buys a brand that makes his business look trustworthy enough to charge more. When your product saves time, removes stress, solves an urgent problem or carries status, it is worth more than the cost of its materials. A bridal makeup artist in Ikeja who charges ₦150,000 is not selling powder and brushes. She is selling confidence on the most photographed day of a woman's life, and a guarantee that the makeup will last through tears, sweat and dancing until 2 a.m. Price the outcome, not the ingredients.

Use cost-plus to set your floor, competitor pricing to check your range, and value to set your final price. Never price below your floor.

## Know Your Numbers: Margin, Markup and Break-Even

Three numbers run every profitable business. If you cannot calculate them, you are guessing.

**Gross margin** is the profit left after the direct cost of the product, divided by the selling price. If you sell a shoe for ₦25,000 and its true product cost is ₦15,000, your gross profit is ₦10,000 and your margin is 40 percent. Aim for at least 30 to 40 percent on physical products in Nigeria, because rent, salaries, adverts and bank charges still have to come out of that margin. Digital products and services can target 60 to 80 percent because there is no stock cost, but they still carry advert and time costs.

**Markup** is the profit divided by the cost, not the price. In the shoe example, ₦10,000 divided by ₦15,000 is a markup of about 67 percent. Do not confuse the two. A seller who says "I add 50 percent" usually means markup, which gives a margin of only 33 percent. Both numbers are useful, but margin is the one that tells you whether the business survives.

**Break-even** is the number of units you must sell to cover your fixed monthly costs. Add up your rent, salaries, internet, subscriptions, transport and other monthly bills. Suppose your shop in Yaba costs you ₦250,000 a month in fixed costs, and you make ₦5,000 profit on each handbag. Divide ₦250,000 by ₦5,000 and you get 50 bags. You must sell 50 bags before you make a single naira of profit. Everything after that is your real income. If you sell 40 bags, you lost money that month even though the shop looked busy. Knowing your break-even number changes how you plan adverts, promos and stock. It tells you exactly how many sales you need, instead of hoping the month works out.

Review these numbers monthly. Costs move, and your prices must move with them.

## How to Raise Your Prices Without Losing Customers

The fear of raising prices keeps many Nigerian businesses poor. Owners imagine that every customer will leave. In practice, a well-handled increase loses a few price-sensitive buyers and keeps the customers who value you, while your profit per sale jumps. Here is how to do it.

First, raise prices on new customers before existing ones. New buyers have no old price in their head. Test the new price for two weeks. If orders hold steady, roll it out to everyone.

Second, give notice and a reason. A short WhatsApp broadcast or status update works: "From 1 December, our small chops platter moves from ₦15,000 to ₦18,000 because the cost of chicken, oil and packaging has risen sharply. We have kept our quality the same and delivery remains free within Lekki." Customers accept honest reasons. They resent silent increases discovered at checkout.

Third, raise in small steps rather than one shocking jump. Moving from ₦10,000 to ₦12,000 is easier for a customer to accept than jumping to ₦15,000 at once. If you need a large increase, split it across two months and add a small visible improvement at the same time, like better packaging or faster delivery, so the customer feels the extra value.

Fourth, keep an entry-level option. If your premium hair costs ₦120,000, keep a good quality option at ₦65,000. Customers who cannot afford the top tier stay with you instead of leaving for a competitor, and many of them upgrade later.

Fifth, never apologise for your price. Apologising tells the customer the price is wrong. State it calmly, explain the value, and let the customer decide. The right customers will pay. The wrong ones were never profitable anyway.

## Pricing Mistakes That Quietly Kill Nigerian Businesses

Watch for these five mistakes. Most struggling sellers are making at least two of them.

**Copying a competitor's price without knowing their costs.** A wholesaler in Balogun Market who buys 500 pieces at once pays far less per piece than you do buying ten. An importer with a direct factory contact has a different cost base. A seller running adverts from a personal account with no shop rent has different overheads. Copying their price is like copying someone else's exam answers without knowing the question. Use their price as information, never as your decision.

**Giving discounts that erase the profit.** A 20 percent discount on a product with a 30 percent margin leaves you with a 10 percent margin before rent and adverts. A "buy one get one free" offer on the same product means you lose money on every pair. Discounts are a marketing tool, not a kindness. Before any promo, calculate the discounted price against your true cost. If the promo price is below cost plus a small margin, change the promo. Offer a free gift with a high perceived value and low cost instead, like a small sample, a branded tote bag bought in bulk, or free delivery within a limited area.

**Forgetting your own salary.** You are not a volunteer. Your time answering DMs at midnight, packaging orders on Sunday, and running to the market has a value. Add a fair monthly salary for yourself into your fixed costs, even if you do not withdraw it yet. If the business cannot pay you, it is not profitable. It is a hobby funded by your savings. Many owners discover their business "makes money" only because they pay themselves nothing. Put your salary in the calculation and price accordingly.

## Practical Pricing Strategies for Different Nigerian Businesses

Different businesses need different tactics. Here are strategies that work in the Nigerian market right now.

**For fashion and beauty sellers:** use tiered pricing. A basic, standard and premium version of the same product lets customers choose their level. A wig seller can offer a basic closure wig at ₦45,000, a standard frontal at ₦85,000, and a luxury raw-hair frontal at ₦150,000. Most buyers pick the middle option, which is exactly where your best margin should sit. Add a clear price anchor: show the premium option first so the standard option feels reasonable by comparison.

**For food businesses:** price per portion and control portion size precisely. Weigh your ingredients for a week, cost each portion, and set the price from that. Sell combos instead of single items: a rice, chicken and drink combo at ₦5,500 feels like a deal and earns you more than selling the rice alone at ₦3,500. Always cost the free extras, because that free bottle of water or free delivery is not free to you.

**For service providers:** stop charging per hour when the client cares about the result. A website designer who charges ₦350,000 for a complete five-page business website, with a clear list of deliverables, sells confidence. Package your services into starter, growth and premium packages, each with named deliverables, and require a deposit of 50 to 70 percent before you start work.

**For online sellers using Instagram and WhatsApp:** build delivery costs and the cost of rejected pay-on-delivery orders into your price, and offer a small discount, such as ₦1,000 off, for upfront payment by transfer.

## How to Test Prices Like a Professional

Guessing is not a strategy. Testing is. You can test prices with simple methods that cost almost nothing.

Run a simple test. Offer the same product at ₦18,000 to one group and ₦20,000 to another for one week, and track profit, not just orders. Selling 20 units at ₦18,000 with ₦5,000 profit each earns ₦100,000, while selling 17 units at ₦20,000 with ₦7,000 profit each earns ₦119,000. The higher price wins even though fewer people bought.

Review your prices every month. Pick one day, such as the first Monday of the month, and check your supplier prices, fuel, delivery rates and advert costs. If any cost has risen by more than 5 percent, adjust your prices that week.

## A Simple Pricing Worksheet You Can Use Today

Use this worksheet for each product. Fill it in with real numbers from your last order, not estimates from memory.

1. Supplier cost per unit: ₦____
2. Transport and clearing per unit: ₦____
3. Packaging and finishing per unit: ₦____
4. Advert cost per order: ₦____
5. Delivery cost you absorb per order: ₦____
6. Bank, transfer and platform charges per order: ₦____
7. Your time cost per order: ₦____
8. Total true cost (add lines 1 to 7): ₦____
9. Your target margin: ____ percent
10. Minimum selling price (line 8 divided by 1 minus your margin): ₦____
11. Competitor price range: ₦____ to ₦____
12. Your final price: ₦____
13. Profit per unit (line 12 minus line 8): ₦____
14. Break-even units per month (monthly fixed costs divided by line 13): ____

If line 13 is below ₦1,000 on a product that takes you an hour to sell and deliver, it is not worth your time unless it brings customers who buy other things. Do this worksheet for your top five products, drop or reprice anything that fails, and put your advert money behind the products that pass.

## When to Offer Discounts, and When to Refuse

Discounts have a place, but only when they are planned. A launch discount for the first week of a new product brings in early buyers and reviews. A bulk discount rewards a customer who buys five pieces at once and saves you four separate delivery costs. A loyalty discount keeps a repeat customer who orders every month. A clearance discount moves old stock that would otherwise sit and tie up your money.

Refuse discounts when the customer is simply testing you. The customer who says "I will buy if you reduce it by half" is not your customer, and the one who promises many referrals in exchange for a cheap price rarely brings any. Politely hold your price and offer free delivery within Lagos instead; it costs you less than a deep discount and feels generous. Never run a discount that lasts forever. A "promo price" that never ends is just your price, and customers learn to wait for the next promo instead of buying now.

## Conclusion: Price for the Business You Want

Pricing is not about being the cheapest seller in the market. The cheapest seller is usually the first to close, because there is no profit left to pay rent, restock, or survive a bad month. Pricing is about building a business that can pay you, grow, employ people, and still be here in three years.

Start today. Calculate your true cost for your best-selling product. Set a floor price you will never go below. Check your competitors for range, price your value honestly, and review your numbers every month. Raise your prices when your costs rise, and say so plainly. The customers who value your work will stay, and your business will finally pay you properly for the work you do.

Your price is a message. Make sure it says this product is worth it, and the person behind it deserves to be paid well.
