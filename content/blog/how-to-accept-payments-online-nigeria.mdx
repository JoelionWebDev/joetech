Getting paid should be the easiest part of running your business. Too often, it is the part where customers quietly disappear. A customer says they want your product, you send your account number, and then nothing. They meant to pay, they got distracted, or the payment step felt like too much work. Every extra bit of friction between "I want it" and "I have paid" costs you sales.

The good news is that accepting payments online in Nigeria has never been easier. You do not need a big budget, a developer, or a registered company to start. A bank account, a phone, and one or two payment options are enough to begin. This guide walks through the main ways Nigerian small businesses get paid online — bank transfer, payment links, Paystack, Flutterwave, cash on delivery, and POS — and how to choose the combination that fits your business.

## Start With Bank Transfer — It Is Still the Most Common Way Nigerians Pay

For most Nigerian small businesses, bank transfer is where payment starts, and for many, it is still where most payments happen. Your customers already know how to do it: they open their bank app, type in your account number, confirm your name, and send the money. There is no new app to download and no learning curve. That familiarity is exactly why it works — the payment method your customers already trust is the one they will actually use.

First, decide which account customers will pay into. A business account in your business name looks more professional, builds trust, and keeps your business money separate from your personal money, which makes your records far easier to manage. If you do not have a business account yet, you can start with a personal account and open a business account as soon as you can — many Nigerian banks let you open one with basic business documents, and some fintech apps offer business accounts you can set up from your phone.

Second, make your account details easy to copy, and confirm payment quickly. Send the details as plain text, not as a screenshot, so the customer can copy and paste the account number into their bank app without retyping it and making a mistake. Include the bank name, the account name exactly as it appears, and the account number in one short message. When the money arrives, check your bank alerts and reply fast — "Payment received, thank you" — because that confirmation turns a nervous first-time buyer into a repeat customer. Bank transfer has one weakness: it depends on the customer doing the work, leaving your chat, opening their bank app, and coming back with proof. That is why it works best as your foundation, not your only option.

## Add a Payment Link — The Easiest Upgrade After Bank Transfer

A payment link is a web link that takes your customer straight to a secure payment page where they can pay with their card or by bank transfer, without you having to build a website or an online store. You create the link, send it on WhatsApp or put it in your bio, the customer taps it, enters their card details or chooses bank transfer, and the money lands in your account. For a business that sells mainly through WhatsApp, Instagram, or Facebook, a payment link is often the single most useful upgrade you can make — and it means you can accept card payments with no website at all.

Payment links are offered by the big Nigerian payment companies, and by several banks and fintech apps as well. The setup is usually straightforward: create a free account with the payment company, complete the verification steps they ask for, create a link for a product or a service with its price in Naira, and share the link wherever you sell. You can make one link per product, or one general link where the customer types the amount. Some payment companies also let you create a simple storefront page listing your products with prices and payment buttons, which is a good stepping stone if you do not have a website yet.

The biggest advantage of a payment link is that it removes steps. The customer does not have to ask for your account number, wait for your reply, copy details, and send a screenshot. They tap, pay, and you both get a confirmation. That matters most on social media, where attention is short: a customer browsing your Instagram page at night will pay immediately if the link is right there, and will forget by morning if they have to message you first and wait for a reply. Payment links also give you better records — each payment comes with the customer's name and the amount in one dashboard. Do note that payment companies charge a small fee on each transaction, so check the current fees on the company's website before you choose, and factor the fee into your prices.

## Paystack or Flutterwave — How to Choose Between the Big Two

When Nigerian business owners talk about accepting payments online, two names come up more than any others: Paystack and Flutterwave. Both are Nigerian payment companies, both are widely trusted, and both let you accept card payments, bank transfers, and more, through payment links, a checkout on your website, or a simple storefront. If you are choosing your first payment gateway, you will almost certainly be choosing between these two, and the honest answer is that both are good — the right choice depends on your business, not on which one is "better" in general.

Paystack is known for being straightforward to set up and easy to use, especially for small businesses and for businesses that sell through links and simple pages. Its dashboard is clean, its payment links are quick to create, and it works well with many Nigerian websites and online stores. If your business is mostly WhatsApp and Instagram sales, or a small website with a few products, Paystack is a very natural fit, and many small businesses never need anything else.

Flutterwave is known for its breadth: it supports more payment methods and more currencies, and it is often the choice for businesses that sell to customers outside Nigeria as well as inside it, or that want features like invoicing and a storefront in one place. If you sell to customers in other African countries or abroad, Flutterwave is worth a close look. In practice, many growing businesses end up using both, or switching as their needs change, and that is fine. Pick one, complete the verification, create your first payment link, and test it yourself with a small payment before you share it with customers. Payment companies will ask for identification and bank details during verification, so have those ready before you begin.

## Cash on Delivery and POS — For Customers Who Prefer to Pay in Person

Not every Nigerian customer wants to pay online before they receive their goods, and for some products, asking for payment upfront will lose you the sale. Cash on delivery — the customer pays when the goods arrive — is still a normal and expected option in Nigeria, especially for physical products sold through social media and marketplaces. If you sell products delivered by dispatch rider, offering cash on delivery can open your business to a large group of customers who would never pay in advance to a seller they have just found online.

Cash on delivery needs clear rules, or it will cost you money. Agree the delivery fee upfront, and be clear about whether it is paid by the customer or included in the price. Confirm the customer's address and phone number before you dispatch, and call to confirm the order before the rider leaves — a quick confirmation call prevents the most common loss in cash on delivery, which is a rider arriving to find nobody at home or a customer who changed their mind. Some sellers ask for a small commitment fee, such as the delivery fee paid in advance, to confirm the order is serious. That is a reasonable policy, and stating it clearly upfront avoids arguments later.

A POS machine is the in-person equivalent of a payment link. If you have a shop, a stall, an office, or you meet clients face to face, a POS terminal lets customers pay by card on the spot. You can get one from your bank or from a payment company, and there are also mobile POS devices that connect to your phone. Keep it charged and have a backup plan — a payment link or your account details — for the day the network is slow or the terminal fails, because a customer standing in front of you with a card is a sale you do not want to lose. For services, the equivalent of cash on delivery is a deposit: a small amount paid upfront when a client books, with the balance paid on completion. State the deposit policy clearly before the work starts and apply it consistently.

## Protect Yourself and Your Customers From Payment Fraud

As soon as you start accepting payments online, you need to think about fraud, because the same tools that make payment easy for honest customers make it easier for dishonest ones too. The most common problem for Nigerian small businesses is fake payment alerts: a customer sends you a doctored screenshot of a transfer, or an SMS that looks like a bank alert, and pressures you to release the goods quickly. The rule that protects you is simple and non-negotiable: never release goods or start work based on a screenshot alone. Always confirm the money in your own account first — check your bank app, your balance, or your payment dashboard — before you dispatch anything.

Be careful with your own account details, too. Share your account number only with people who are actually buying from you, and never share your bank password, your one-time codes, or your payment dashboard login with anyone, including someone who claims to be from your bank or your payment company. Real banks and payment companies will never ask you for those. If someone calls or messages asking for them, it is a scam, no matter how official they sound.

For your customers' safety, only send them payment links from your official accounts, and make sure your links lead to a page that shows your business name. A customer who taps a link and lands on a page with a different name will rightly be suspicious. Keep your business name consistent everywhere — your WhatsApp, your Instagram, your payment links, and your bank account — so customers can see at a glance that they are paying the right person. Keep records of every payment you receive, even the small ones: a simple notebook or spreadsheet with the date, the customer's name, the amount, and what it was for is enough. Good records protect you in a dispute and help you know which products are selling.

## Make Getting Paid Easy, and Say So Clearly

The final step is the simplest, and it is the one most businesses skip: tell your customers, clearly and everywhere, how they can pay you. Put your payment options in your WhatsApp bio, in your Instagram highlights, at the bottom of every product post, and on your website's contact page. When a customer asks for your price, send the price together with the payment link or the account details in the same message, so they can pay immediately while they are still interested. Do not make them ask twice — every extra message is a chance for them to drift away.

Think about your customer's journey. They see your product on Instagram, they message you on WhatsApp, you answer their questions, they decide to buy, and then what? If the next step is obvious — a payment link right there in the chat, a clear account number, a simple instruction — they pay. If the next step is unclear, slow, or confusing, they leave, and they often buy the same thing from a competitor whose payment process was easier. In a crowded market, the business that is easiest to pay usually wins, even when its prices are a little higher.

Review your payment setup every few months. Are customers still asking questions about how to pay? Are payments failing? Are you losing sales because a method is missing? Add the option customers keep asking for, remove the one nobody uses, and keep testing the flow yourself. If you have a website, ask your developer to connect your payment company to it — most Nigerian store platforms have ready-made integrations, so it is usually a quick job. Payment is not a one-time setup; it is part of how your business serves its customers, and it deserves the same attention as your products and your marketing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the easiest way for a small business in Nigeria to start accepting payments online?**

Start with bank transfer, because your customers already know how to use it and it costs you nothing. Then add one payment link from a payment company such as Paystack or Flutterwave, so customers can pay by card in one tap without messaging you first. Those two options together cover most customers, and you can add more later as you grow.

**Do I need a registered business or a CAC certificate to use Paystack or Flutterwave?**

Not necessarily to start. Both companies allow individuals and sole traders to sign up, although they will ask for identification and bank details during verification. A registered business gives you a more professional setup and access to more features, so registering your business is worth doing, but it should not stop you from accepting payments in the meantime.

**How much do payment companies charge per transaction?**

The fee differs from one company to another and can change, so check the current fees on the company's own website before you choose. As a rule, expect a small percentage of each transaction. Factor the fee into your prices so it does not eat into your profit.

**Is it safe for my customers to pay through a payment link?**

Yes, when the link comes from a reputable payment company and shows your business name on the payment page. Customers should check that the page shows your business name before entering their card details, and you should only share links from your official accounts.

**What should I do if a customer sends a fake payment alert?**

Do not release the goods or start the work. Politely tell the customer you will confirm the payment in your account first, then check your bank app or payment dashboard yourself. If the money is not there, tell them plainly that the payment has not reflected and ask them to check with their bank.
