Bank transfer is the lifeblood of Nigerian small business. Your customer in Abuja sees your product on Instagram, messages you on WhatsApp, and pays by transfer before you dispatch. No card machine, no cash handling, no waiting. It is fast, familiar, and almost free.

It is also the number one way Nigerian businesses lose money. Every week, vendors in Lagos, Onitsha, and Port Harcourt discover — too late — that the transfer alert a customer showed them was fake or the screenshot was edited. A single fake transfer of ₦85,000 can wipe out a week's profit for a small vendor; a fake alert for a ₦250,000 order can put a business in real trouble.

The good news is that almost all transfer fraud is preventable. You do not need expensive software or a finance degree. You need a clear payment process, a habit of verifying before you dispatch, and a few simple tools that cost little or nothing. This guide gives you that system, step by step, in plain language.

## Why Bank Transfers Are Risky for Nigerian Businesses

To protect yourself, you need to understand how the scams actually work. The most common ones are simple, and that is why they succeed.

**The fake alert.** A customer shows you a debit alert or a screenshot that looks exactly like a real bank message. It may even arrive as an SMS on their phone. But no money entered your account. The alert was generated by a fake-alert app or edited in a photo app. Screenshots are easy to fake; your own bank balance is not.

**The wrong-account trick.** A customer claims they sent money to your account, but the money went to a different account — sometimes their own, sometimes an accomplice's. When you check, there is no credit. They insist, show a receipt, and pressure you to release the goods.

**The delayed or reversed transfer.** A customer sends money and then reports it as an error or fraud, asking their bank to reverse it. If you have already dispatched the goods, you lose both the goods and the money.

**The overpayment scam.** A customer "mistakenly" sends ₦150,000 for a ₦50,000 order and asks you to refund the ₦100,000 difference quickly — to a different account. The original transfer is fake or will bounce, but your refund is real money out of your account.

Every one of these scams has the same weakness: it depends on you releasing goods or services before you have confirmed the money is truly in your account. Your entire safety system is built around one rule: **confirm first, dispatch second. No exceptions.**

## Step 1: Set Up a Proper Business Account

Everything starts with having the right account. If you are still receiving business payments into your personal savings account, that is your first fix.

**Open a dedicated business account.** A current account in your business name — not your personal name — makes verification easier and protects you in disputes. Most Nigerian banks now offer SME accounts with low or zero opening balance, and fintech options like Moniepoint, Opay Business, and Kuda Business let you open one from your phone with your CAC documents or BVN. Your customers also trust a business-name account more: when they see "Joetech Ventures" instead of "Igono Joel" on the transfer screen, they know they are paying a real business.

**Keep business money separate from personal money.** This is not just good practice; it is what makes reconciliation possible. When all business payments land in one account, you can check that account and know exactly what came in. When business and personal money mix, you will never be sure whether a payment arrived.

**Turn on all notifications.** Enable SMS alerts, email alerts, and push notifications from your bank app. Set the alert threshold to ₦0 if your bank allows it, so you are notified of every credit, no matter how small. You want your own bank telling you the money arrived — not the customer.

**Use one main receiving account, and say so clearly.** Put your account details in one place — your WhatsApp bio, your Instagram bio, your website's payment page — and use the same account for every order. Multiple accounts create confusion and make it easy for a scammer's payment to "go missing" in the shuffle.

## Step 2: Create a Payment Process Your Customers Understand

A clear process protects you and reassures honest customers. Write it down, pin it on your WhatsApp, and put it on your website or Instagram highlights so every customer sees the same rules.

**Publish your account details in full.** Your bank name, account number, and account name — exactly as they appear. Customers should never have to ask "what is your account number?" in a DM. When the details are public and consistent, a scammer cannot quietly substitute their own account number in a chat.

**State your confirmation rule upfront.** Tell customers plainly: "Please send your payment and allow 5–10 minutes for confirmation. Your order will be prepared once payment is confirmed in our account." Honest customers accept this without argument. Customers who pressure you to dispatch before confirmation are exactly the ones the rule exists for.

**Always send a receipt or acknowledgement.** Once you confirm a payment, message the customer: "Payment of ₦25,000 received. Thank you! Your order will be dispatched tomorrow morning." This creates a written record and prevents the "I paid but you said I didn't" argument later.

**Set a payment deadline for orders.** For example: "Orders are held for 24 hours after the price is quoted. After that, please confirm the price again, as prices may change."

## Step 3: Verify Every Payment Before You Dispatch

This is the heart of the whole system. No matter how busy you are, no matter how much the customer is rushing, verify every payment in your own bank account before you hand over goods or start work.

Here is the verification routine, in order:

1. **Check your bank app, not the customer's phone.** Open your own banking app or USSD balance and look for the credit. The amount, the sender's name, and the time should match the order. This is the only check that truly matters.
2. **Wait for the SMS or email alert from your bank.** Your bank's own alert is reliable. A screenshot of an alert on someone else's phone is not.
3. **Match the amount exactly.** If the order is ₦32,500 and the credit is ₦32,000, do not dispatch. Contact the customer and resolve the difference first.
4. **Check the sender's name.** If the name on the transfer does not match the customer's name, ask who sent it. Third-party payments are common, but you should know who paid before you dispatch.

If a customer shows you a screenshot and your account shows nothing, say so calmly and plainly: "I have checked our account and the payment has not reflected yet. Please confirm with your bank and send me the transaction receipt. Once it reflects, we will dispatch immediately." Do not argue about the screenshot. Stick to what your account says.

## Step 4: How to Spot a Fake Alert in Seconds

Fake alerts are getting better, but they still have tells. Learn these and teach them to your staff.

- **The SMS comes from a strange sender ID or a personal number.** Real bank alerts come from your bank's short code or sender name. An alert that arrives as a normal SMS from an unknown number is suspicious.
- **The balance does not add up.** A genuine alert shows your correct balance after the credit. A fake one often shows a wrong balance, or no balance at all.
- **The alert arrives before the customer even "sends" the money.** If the alert appears suspiciously fast, or the customer shows it to you within seconds of claiming to have sent it, be careful — real transfers can be instant, but pressure plus speed is a red flag.
- **The customer refuses to let you check your own account.** Any customer who says "don't worry, just check this screenshot" or rushes you to release goods is a warning sign. Honest customers are happy to wait two minutes while you confirm.
- **The receipt has odd fonts, spacing, or spelling errors.** Compare it with a receipt from a transfer you know is real. Fake receipts often have slightly wrong fonts, misaligned text, or bank names spelled oddly.
- **The transaction reference does not match your bank's format.** If you can, ask the customer for the session ID or reference number and check it against your bank statement.

When in doubt, trust your bank app over everything else. If the money is not in your account, it has not been paid — no matter what any screenshot says.

## Step 5: Use Payment Links and Fintech Tools

Direct transfers are not your only option, and for many businesses they are not the safest one. Payment links and fintech tools add a layer of verification that a raw transfer does not have.

**Paystack and Flutterwave payment links.** Both let you create a payment link for a specific amount and send it to the customer on WhatsApp. The customer pays by transfer, card, or USSD through the link, and you get an instant confirmation in your dashboard when the payment succeeds. There is a small fee per transaction, but it is far cheaper than losing an order to fraud.

**Moniepoint and Opay for business.** Many small vendors in markets like Onitsha, Alaba, and Balogun now use Moniepoint or Opay business accounts because transfers reflect instantly, alerts are fast, and the apps show a clear transaction history you can check in front of the customer.

**Bank transfer with a reference.** If you stick with direct transfers, ask every customer to include their name or order number in the transfer narration or description. This makes reconciliation far easier when you have ten transfers in a day and need to match each one to an order.

## Step 6: Decide Your Policy on Prepayment, Part Payment, and Delivery

Your payment policy should match the risk of each type of order.

**Full prepayment for custom or made-to-order goods.** If you are sewing a dress, baking a cake, printing branded shirts, or ordering stock specially for a customer, take full payment — or at least 70% — before you start work. Custom work cannot be resold if the customer disappears, and a deposit is your only protection. State this clearly before you take the order, not after.

**Part payment for large orders.** For orders above ₦100,000, a 50% deposit with the balance on delivery is fair to both sides. The deposit commits the customer; the balance on delivery gives them confidence. Confirm the deposit the same way you confirm any payment — in your own account first.

**Payment on delivery for standard goods, with care.** If you offer pay-on-delivery within Lagos or Abuja, confirm the customer's address and phone number before dispatch, and make sure your rider collects before handing over the goods. Consider charging a small delivery fee upfront, even when the goods are pay-on-delivery, to filter out unserious buyers.

**Never dispatch on a promise.** "I will transfer when the goods arrive," "my husband will send it tonight," "the network is bad, I will send it in the morning" — these may all be true, but they are not payment. Polite rule: goods leave your shop when money enters your account, or when you have a deposit you are comfortable losing. No exceptions, even for regular customers. In fact, especially for regular customers, because familiarity is exactly what scammers exploit.

## Step 7: Keep Records and Reconcile Daily

Fraud often hides in bad records. If you do not know what came in today, you cannot know what is missing.

**Reconcile every evening.** Spend ten minutes at the end of each day: list today's orders, list today's bank credits, and match them. Any order without a matching credit gets flagged and followed up the next morning. This single habit catches most problems while they are still fresh and fixable.

**Save every receipt and reference.** Screenshots of your own bank credits, transfer references, and customer payment confirmations should be saved in a folder, not deleted from your phone after a week. If a dispute arises a month later, your records are your evidence.

**Use a simple invoice for every order.** Even a WhatsApp message counts: "Order: 2 ankara dresses, ₦45,000. Paid: ₦45,000 on 26 Nov. Balance: ₦0." It takes 20 seconds and it ends arguments before they start. ## Step 8: Train Your Staff — They Are Your Weakest Link or Your Best Defence

If you have staff who handle payments, they need the same rules you follow, written down and practised.

- **Only one or two people verify payments.** Designate who checks the bank app and who authorises dispatch, and make sure everyone knows.
- **Never accept a screenshot as proof.** Train staff to say, "Let me confirm in our account, please give me one minute." Customers respect a business with a process.
- **Staff must never give out their personal account for business payments.** Every business payment goes to the business account. No exceptions, even "just this once."
- **Run a quick drill.** Show your staff a fake alert and a real one side by side, and ask them to spot the difference. Five minutes of training can save you ₦100,000.

## What to Do If You Have Already Been Scammed

If the money is gone and the goods are gone, act quickly and methodically.

1. **Gather everything.** Screenshots of the chat, the fake alert, the customer's number, the account details, and your bank statement showing no credit.
2. **Report to your bank immediately.** Call your bank's customer care or visit a branch and report the fraud. If the money went to a real account, your bank can place a lien or flag the receiving account.
3. **Report to the police and the EFCC if the amount is large.** For significant losses, file a report with the police and consider a petition to the EFCC. Bring all your evidence. Recovery is not guaranteed, but a report creates a record and sometimes leads to a freeze on the receiving account.

## Common Mistakes That Cost Nigerian Businesses Money

- **Releasing goods on a screenshot alone.** The single most expensive mistake. Always confirm in your own account.
- **Using your personal account for business.** It makes verification messy and disputes harder to prove.
- **No written payment policy.** When the rules are only in your head, staff improvise — and scammers exploit the confusion.
- **Rushing because the customer is rushing.** Pressure is a scammer's favourite tool. A real customer will wait three minutes.
- **Not reconciling daily.** By the time you notice a missing payment a week later, the trail is cold.

## Your Safe Payments Checklist

- [ ] Business account opened in your business name, separate from personal money.
- [ ] Bank SMS, email, and app notifications switched on for every credit.
- [ ] Account details published in your WhatsApp bio, Instagram bio, and website.
- [ ] Written payment policy: confirm first, dispatch second, no exceptions.
- [ ] Verification routine memorised: check your own bank app, match the amount and sender name, wait for large orders.
- [ ] Payment links set up (Paystack or Flutterwave) for remote orders.
- [ ] Daily reconciliation: match every order to a bank credit before closing.
- [ ] Payment log kept: date, customer, amount, reference, confirmed tick.
- [ ] Staff trained: only designated people verify payments, screenshots are never proof.
- [ ] Invoice or written confirmation sent for every order.
- [ ] Fake-alert warning signs known by you and your staff.

## Final Word

Bank transfer is not going anywhere in Nigeria — it is how your customers prefer to pay, and it will stay that way for years. The answer is not to stop accepting transfers; it is to accept them properly. One business account, one clear process, one verification habit, and one daily reconciliation are all it takes to close the door on the scams that cost Nigerian small businesses millions of naira every year.
