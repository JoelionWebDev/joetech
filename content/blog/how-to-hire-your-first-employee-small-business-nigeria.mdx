Hiring your first employee is one of the biggest moments in the life of a small business. It means the business has grown beyond what one person can carry. It also means a new monthly cost, a new responsibility, and a new relationship that can either lift the business or drag it down. Done well, your first hire frees you to focus on growth. Done badly, it becomes a source of stress, lost money and endless arguments.

Many Nigerian small business owners delay hiring for too long. They work eighteen-hour days, answer every customer message themselves, package every order, and handle every complaint, until they burn out or start losing customers because they cannot keep up. Others hire too quickly, bringing in a cousin or a friend with no clear job and no clear pay, and then wonder why nothing improves.

This guide will help you hire your first employee properly: knowing when you are ready, defining the job, finding good candidates, interviewing them, setting pay fairly, and keeping a good person once you find them.

## How to know you are ready to hire

The first question is not who to hire. It is whether you are ready. Here are the signs.

You are turning away work. Customers message you and you reply hours late, or you tell them you are fully booked. Orders pile up and you miss delivery dates. A fashion designer in Surulere told us she was losing two or three orders a week because she could not sew fast enough alone. Her first hire, a junior tailor, paid for herself within six weeks because the orders she had been turning away finally got done.

You spend most of your time on tasks that do not need you. If you spend four hours a day replying to the same customer questions, packaging orders, or running to the market, those are tasks someone else can do. Your time is better spent on sales, marketing, and the parts of the business only you can do.

Your income can carry a salary. This is the hard test. A hire is a fixed monthly cost. Before you hire, work out what the business earns in a normal month after the cost of goods, transport, data, and other expenses. Then ask whether you can pay the salary for at least three months even if sales dip. If the answer is no, you are not ready yet, or you should start with a part-time or commission-based arrangement instead.

You have work that is regular, not occasional. A one-off busy week does not justify a full-time employee. But if the same tasks appear every day, week after week, that is a real job waiting to be filled.

If you recognise two or more of these signs, it is time to start planning your first hire.

## Decide what the job actually is

The most common hiring mistake is hiring "someone to help" without defining what help means. The person arrives, nobody knows what they should do, and both sides get frustrated.

Before you advertise, write down the job. Start with the tasks. What exactly will this person do each day? A sales assistant in a Lekki boutique might greet customers, arrange the display, answer WhatsApp messages, record sales, and package online orders. A rider for a food business in Yaba might collect orders, deliver within two hours, and return with payment confirmations.

Then write down the hours. Will they work Monday to Saturday, 8am to 6pm? Will they work half days? Be specific. Many disputes start because the owner expected the employee to be available on Sundays and the employee never agreed to that.

Then decide the skills the job truly needs. A sales assistant needs to be polite, honest, and able to use WhatsApp and a calculator. A delivery rider needs to know Lagos roads and ride safely. A junior tailor needs to sew straight seams and follow instructions. You do not need a university degree for most first hires. You need reliability, honesty, and a willingness to learn.

Finally, decide what success looks like in the first three months. For a sales assistant, it might be: answers all WhatsApp messages within thirty minutes, records every sale correctly, and no customer complaints about rudeness. Write these down. They become the basis for your probation review.

Keep the job description to one page. It should state the job title, the main duties, the working hours, the location, the pay range, and how to apply. A clear job description attracts serious candidates and scares off people who are not a fit.

## What to pay your first employee

Pay is where many small businesses get stuck. Pay too little and you get poor work and high turnover. Pay too much and the business cannot survive. Here is how to think about it.

Find out what the market pays. Ask other business owners in your area what they pay for a similar role. A sales assistant in a Lagos shop typically earns between ₦60,000 and ₦120,000 a month, depending on the area, the hours, and the experience. A rider earns a salary plus fuel allowance, or a per-delivery rate. A cleaner or shop attendant earns less. Your location matters: salaries in Lekki are usually higher than in Ikorodu or Sango-Ota, because transport and living costs are higher.

Consider the total cost, not just the salary. Transport allowance, feeding, and occasional bonuses all add up. If you pay ₦80,000 salary plus ₦20,000 transport, your real monthly cost is ₦100,000. Budget for the full amount.

Think about how the pay connects to performance. A small base salary plus commission on sales can work well for a sales role, because the employee earns more when the business earns more. A food vendor in Ikeja pays her sales assistant ₦70,000 a month plus a small commission on every order above a daily target. The assistant is motivated to sell, and the owner only pays the extra when sales are strong.

Be honest about what you can afford. It is better to offer ₦70,000 and pay it on time every month than to promise ₦120,000 and pay late. Late salary destroys trust faster than a modest salary. If you cannot afford a full-time salary yet, consider part-time work, a three-day week, or a commission-only arrangement with a clear agreement. Whatever you agree, put it in writing.

## Where to find good candidates

Your first employee does not have to come from a recruitment agency. In fact, for most small businesses, the best candidates come from simpler sources.

Ask people you trust. Tell your suppliers, your regular customers, and other business owners in your market that you are hiring. A fabric seller in Balogun market found her best sales assistant through another trader who knew a young woman looking for work. Personal recommendations carry useful information: the person recommending them is putting their own reputation on the line.

Post the job where your customers are. A short post on your WhatsApp status, your Instagram page, and your Facebook page can reach hundreds of people for free. State the job title, the location, the main duties, and how to apply. Ask applicants to send a short message about themselves and their experience. This alone filters out people who cannot follow simple instructions.

Use local job groups and notice boards. Many neighbourhoods have WhatsApp groups for jobs and services. Churches, mosques, and community associations are also good places to share a vacancy, especially if you want someone local who can reach your shop easily.

Consider walk-in applications. Put a small notice in your shop window or at the market stall. Some of the most reliable staff are people who live nearby and already know your business as customers.

## How to interview properly

You do not need a formal panel or a boardroom. You need a structured conversation that tells you whether this person can do the job and whether you can trust them.

Start with a phone chat. Before you invite anyone in person, call them for five minutes. Ask what they have done before, why they left their last job, and whether they can work the hours you need. This saves you from wasting time on people who cannot meet the basics.

Then invite the best three or four candidates for a face-to-face meeting. Keep it simple and friendly, but ask real questions. Here are questions that work well for a first hire:

- "Tell me about the last job you had. What did you do every day?"
- "What would you do if a customer was angry about a late delivery?"
- "How do you handle money? Have you handled cash or transfers for a business before?"
- "Why do you want this job, and what do you hope to learn?"
- "Can you give me the name of someone who can confirm your character, like a former employer or a community leader?"

Listen more than you talk. Watch for honesty. A candidate who admits they have never used a POS machine but is willing to learn is more trustworthy than one who claims to know everything. Check their story. Call the referee they gave you. Ask how long they worked there and whether they were reliable. A five-minute call can save you months of trouble.

## The written agreement

A written agreement protects both you and the employee. It does not need to be a lawyer's document. A simple letter, signed by both of you, is enough to start.

It should cover: the job title and duties, the working hours and days, the salary and payday, the transport or other allowances, the probation period, the notice period if either side wants to end the arrangement, and the rules on handling money and business property.

Set a probation period of one to three months. Probation is a trial period during which both sides can decide if the arrangement works. Say this clearly at the start, and set a date for a review. At the review, talk honestly about what went well and what needs to improve.

Give the employee a copy of the agreement and keep a copy yourself. If you can, have the agreement witnessed by a third person. This simple step prevents most "you never told me" arguments.

## The first week: set them up to succeed

The first week decides whether a new hire stays or leaves. Many owners throw a new employee into the deep end with no training and then complain that they are slow. Invest the first week properly.

Day one: show them the business. Explain what you sell, who your customers are, and what good service looks like. Show them where things are kept, how orders are recorded, and how payments are confirmed. Give them the login details they need, and no more.

Days two to five: let them work alongside you. Watch them do each task, correct gently, and answer questions. Write down the most important steps for each task so they can refer to the notes later. A simple checklist for opening the shop, taking an order, or packaging a delivery is worth its weight in gold.

Be clear about money rules from day one. Who handles cash, how it is recorded, and what happens at the end of each day. Every naira should be accounted for. Use a simple sales book or a spreadsheet, and reconcile the cash against the record daily. This protects the employee from suspicion as much as it protects you.

Introduce them to your regular customers and suppliers by name. People work better when they feel part of the business, not like a stranger behind the counter.

## How to manage and keep a good employee

Hiring is only the beginning. Keeping a good person is where most small businesses struggle.

Pay on time, every time. Nothing destroys morale faster than late salary. Choose a payday, for example the 28th of the month, and stick to it. If sales are slow one month, pay the salary first and cut other expenses. Your employee has rent and transport costs that do not wait.

Communicate clearly and respectfully. Tell your employee what you expect, praise good work when you see it, and correct mistakes privately, not in front of customers. Shouting at staff in front of customers damages your business reputation and your employee's dignity. If there is a serious problem, discuss it calmly after closing time.

Give feedback regularly, not just when something goes wrong. A short weekly chat of ten minutes is enough: what went well this week, what could be better, and what is coming next week. Employees who know where they stand work with more confidence.

Handle problems early and honestly. If sales are short, investigate calmly and check the records before accusing anyone. If an employee is regularly late, say so on the second occasion, not the twentieth. Small problems become big ones when they are ignored.

## Mistakes to avoid with your first hire

Learn from the mistakes other small business owners make.

Do not hire without a clear job. "Come and help me" is not a job description. It leads to confusion and resentment.

Do not skip the written agreement. Verbal promises are forgotten or remembered differently. Write it down.

Do not pay late. If you cannot pay on time, you cannot afford the hire yet.

Do not expect the employee to read your mind. Tell them exactly what you want, show them once, and check that they understood.

Do not ignore character. Skills can be taught. Honesty and reliability cannot. A skilled employee who steals is far worse than an honest beginner who needs training.

Do not keep a bad hire out of guilt. If after proper training and fair warning the person still cannot do the job, or cannot be trusted, let them go respectfully and promptly. Keeping them harms the business, your customers, and the employee, who would be better off in a role that suits them.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much should I pay my first employee in Nigeria?

It depends on the role, the location, and the hours. A sales assistant in Lagos often earns between ₦60,000 and ₦120,000 a month. Check what similar businesses near you pay, add transport and any allowances to get the true cost, and never promise a salary you cannot pay on time every month.

### How long should the probation period be?

One to three months is normal for a first hire in a small business. Use the time to train the person, check their reliability, and review their work honestly at the end of the period.

### What if I cannot afford a full-time salary yet?

Start with part-time work, a three-day week, or a commission-based arrangement with a clear written agreement. You can move to full-time when the business income is steady enough to carry the salary for at least three months.

### How do I know if a candidate is honest?

Ask for a referee, such as a former employer or a community leader, and call them. Give the candidate a small practical test, set clear money rules from day one, and reconcile cash and records daily. Trust builds over time, but good systems protect you from the start.
