Every Nigerian small business owner reaches the same painful moment. Your supplier in Balogun Market raises the price of fabric. The cost of fuel for your generator climbs again. The dispatch rider who delivers your orders in Lekki now charges ₦3,500 instead of ₦2,500. Your data, your packaging, your transport to Mile 12 to buy stock — everything creeps up. Yet your selling price stays where it was six months ago, and your profit quietly disappears.

Raising prices feels dangerous. You imagine your customers seeing the new price on your WhatsApp status and quietly moving to the seller down the road. That fear is real. But keeping your prices frozen while your costs rise is not loyalty to your customers. It is a slow way of closing your business.

The truth is that most Nigerian customers will accept a fair price increase if it is handled well. They already live with rising prices everywhere — in the market, at the fuel station, on their DSTv subscription. What they do not accept is a sudden jump with no warning, no explanation, and no improvement in what they receive. This guide walks you through exactly how to raise your prices without losing the customers who matter, with real naira examples and scripts you can copy.

## Know When It Is Time to Raise Your Prices

The first step is to stop guessing and look at your numbers. A price increase is not a mood; it is a response to facts. Open your notebook, spreadsheet, or bank app and check these signs.

Your profit margin has shrunk. If you bought a dress for ₦12,000 and sold it for ₦18,000 six months ago, your gross profit was ₦6,000. If that same dress now costs you ₦15,000 from your supplier and you still sell at ₦18,000, your profit is down to ₦3,000 — and that is before transport, packaging, and the rider. When your margin falls below what you need to survive, the price must move.

Your costs have risen repeatedly. One small increase from a supplier can be absorbed. Three in a row cannot. If the price of your raw materials, your fuel, or your delivery has gone up more than once in a few months, waiting longer only deepens the loss.

You are turning away work because you are too busy. A tailor in Surulere who has a two-week waiting list is underpriced. When demand exceeds what you can supply, a modest increase brings your workload and your income back into balance.

Your competitors have already moved. If the hair vendor in your WhatsApp group now sells the same bundle for ₦45,000 and you are still at ₦35,000, you are not being kind to your customers. You are funding their discount from your own pocket.

## Work Out the New Price Before You Announce Anything

Never announce a price increase before you know the exact new number. Sit down and calculate it properly, because a careless increase creates a second problem: you raise prices, lose a few customers, and still do not make enough profit.

Start with your true cost per item or per job. Add everything: the cost of the product, transport to buy it, packaging, delivery, the share of your generator fuel and data for that sale, and any bank or Paystack charges. Many sellers forget the small costs, and the small costs are exactly what eat the profit.

Take the example of Adaeze, who sells skincare products from her shop in Lekki Phase 1. Her best-selling body cream costs her ₦8,000 to buy, ₦500 in transport, ₦300 in packaging, and ₦1,000 for delivery within Lekki, making her true cost ₦9,800. She sells it for ₦12,000, leaving ₦2,200 — too thin once rent and staff costs are shared across her sales. If she raises the price to ₦14,500, her margin becomes ₦4,700, enough to keep the business healthy without shocking her customers.

Next, decide how big the increase should be. As a rule, a single increase of 10 to 20 percent is usually accepted far better than a huge jump. If you sell Ankara fabric at ₦10,000 per yard and your costs have risen sharply, moving to ₦11,500 feels reasonable. Moving straight to ₦15,000 feels like a different shop entirely. If your costs demand a bigger jump, raise the price in two steps over a few months instead of one large leap.

Finally, test the new price on paper. If you sell 100 items a month at ₦10,000, you earn ₦1,000,000 in revenue. If you raise the price to ₦11,500 and lose 10 customers, you sell 90 items and earn ₦1,035,000 — more money, less work, fewer deliveries. Even with a small drop in volume, a well-judged increase usually leaves you better off.

## Give Your Customers Notice, Not a Surprise

The single biggest mistake Nigerian sellers make is changing the price overnight. A customer who ordered last week at ₦20,000 opens your status this week and sees ₦25,000 with no warning. She feels cheated, even if the new price is fair. Notice is what turns a price increase from an insult into a normal business update.

Give at least one to two weeks of notice. Post it on your WhatsApp status, send it as a broadcast to your regular customers, and pin it in your Instagram bio or your shop notice board. Tell them the date the new prices start. This gives them time to buy at the old price if they want to, and it shows respect.

Use simple, honest language. You do not need a long essay. A message like this works: "Dear valued customers, from Monday, 1st December, our prices will change slightly because the cost of materials, fuel, and delivery has gone up. We have kept our old prices for as long as we could. Thank you for understanding and for your continued support."

For your most loyal customers, tell them personally before the public announcement. A phone call or a direct WhatsApp message to your top ten buyers makes them feel valued instead of blindsided. "Mama T, I wanted you to hear it from me first. From next month, the price of your usual order will be ₦28,000 instead of ₦25,000. The cost of flour and gas has gone up, but your quality will stay the same." That personal touch keeps relationships intact.

## Explain the Reason in One Honest Sentence

Customers do not need a lecture on the economy. They need one honest sentence that makes the increase make sense. The reason should be true, specific, and brief.

"The price of imported hair has gone up twice this year, so our prices will reflect that from next month." That is honest and specific. "Due to the current economic situation in the country" is vague and sounds like an excuse. Be concrete: mention the cost that actually changed. Fuel, flour, fabric, packaging, delivery — pick the one or two that matter most to your business.

Do not lie about the reason. If your real reason is that you were undercharging and your business was not sustainable, you can say so in a dignified way: "We have kept our prices the same for two years while our costs have continued to rise. To keep serving you with the same quality, we need to adjust our prices." Customers respect honesty far more than a made-up story.

## Add Value So the Increase Feels Fair

A price increase is much easier to accept when the customer feels they are getting something extra. You do not need to spend a lot of money. Small improvements in the experience can make a higher price feel like better value.

Improve your packaging. A fashion seller in Lagos Island who starts wrapping orders in a neat branded bag instead of a plain nylon bag immediately looks more premium. The bag costs ₦200 extra, but it supports a ₦2,000 price increase in the customer's mind.

Add a small extra. A baker in Gbagada who raises the price of a cake from ₦25,000 to ₦28,000 can include a free pack of cupcakes for orders above a certain amount. The cost is small, but the feeling of getting more softens the increase.

Improve your communication. Reply faster on WhatsApp. Send a thank-you message after delivery. Confirm the order clearly. Many Nigerian customers will stay with a seller who communicates well even at a higher price, because the alternative — a cheaper seller who never replies — costs them time and stress.

## Protect Your Best Customers With a Gentle Transition

Not every customer should feel the increase in the same way on the same day. Your loyal buyers — the ones who order every week, who refer their friends, who pay on time — deserve a gentler transition. Protecting them is not unfair; it is good business, because replacing a loyal customer costs far more than keeping one.

Create a simple loyalty benefit. It does not need to be complicated. Buy five, get a small discount on the sixth. Or offer your regulars a slightly lower rate than new customers for the first month after the increase. Make it clear this is a thank-you, not a permanent discount.

Consider a smaller increase for your most price-sensitive product. If you sell three sizes of a product, raise the price of the premium size more and the basic size less. That way, a customer who is struggling can still buy from you, while those who want the best still pay for it.

## Handle Objections Calmly and Confidently

Even with notice, a reason, and added value, some customers will complain. That is normal. How you respond decides whether they stay or leave.

When a customer says "Your price is too high now," do not argue and do not panic. Reply calmly: "I understand, and I appreciate you telling me. The cost of our materials has gone up, and we have kept our old price for as long as we could. The quality and quantity remain the same." Then stop. Do not offer a discount immediately. Many customers are testing whether the price is firm. If you drop the price at the first complaint, you teach them that complaining works.

When a customer says "The seller down the road sells it cheaper," do not insult the other seller. Say: "You are free to compare, and I understand. What I can promise is the quality you already know, our delivery on time, and our after-sales support." Confidence is persuasive. Desperation is not.

When a customer threatens to leave, let them go gracefully. "Thank you for your patronage. You are welcome back any time." Some will return within weeks when the cheaper option disappoints them.

## Raise Prices in Small Steps, Not One Big Shock

If your costs have risen a lot, you may be tempted to fix everything with one large increase. Resist that temptation. Customers accept a series of small increases far more easily than one dramatic jump.

Imagine you sell jollof rice packs at a canteen in Ikeja for ₦2,000. Your costs have risen so much that you really need ₦3,000. Jumping straight to ₦3,000 is a 50 percent increase, and your lunch crowd will notice immediately. Raising to ₦2,500 now and ₦3,000 in two months feels like two small adjustments, even though the final price is the same.

Set a simple rhythm for yourself. Review your prices every three or four months. Check your costs, check your margins, and ask whether a small adjustment is needed. A short review every quarter prevents the crisis where you suddenly realise you have been losing money for a year.

## Keep Your Quality Exactly the Same — or Better

Nothing destroys trust faster than raising the price and quietly reducing the quality. Customers notice immediately when the portion gets smaller, the fabric gets thinner, or the service gets rushed. They may not complain, but they will not come back.

If you raise the price of a food pack, keep the portion the same. If you raise the price of a dress, keep the fabric and the finishing the same. If you raise the price of a service, keep the time and attention the same. The deal is simple: the customer pays more, and in return nothing gets worse.

If you genuinely cannot keep the quality at the new price, you have a different problem, and a price increase will not fix it. Sort out your supply chain first. Find a better supplier, renegotiate, or change your product mix. Raising prices on a falling product only speeds up the loss of customers.

## Watch What Happens and Adjust

After the new prices take effect, watch your numbers for the first month. This is not the time to panic at the first quiet week. Some customers will pause, compare, and then return. Give it at least three to four weeks before you judge the result.

Talk to your customers. Ask your regulars how they feel about the service. Listen for real complaints about quality or delivery, and separate those from simple grumbling about price.

If the increase clearly failed — if your sales fell by half and did not recover after a month — look for the cause before you reverse the price. Did you give enough notice? Did you explain the reason? Sometimes the problem is not the price but the timing or the communication. Fix that first, and only reduce the price as a last step.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much should I raise my prices by?**
A single increase of 10 to 20 percent is a good guide for most small businesses. If your costs have risen more than that, split the increase into two steps a few months apart. Always calculate your true costs first so the new price actually restores your margin.

**How much notice should I give my customers?**
Give at least one to two weeks of notice for products, and a full month for services like cleaning, gym memberships, or school fees. Tell your most loyal customers personally before the public announcement so they hear it from you first.

**What if my customers say they will go to a cheaper seller?**
Stay calm and do not argue or insult the other seller. Explain your reason briefly, restate the quality and service they already know, and let them decide. Some will return when the cheaper option disappoints them. Chasing them with discounts teaches them that threats work.

**Should I apologise when I raise my prices?**
A brief, polite acknowledgement is fine, but do not over-apologise. You are adjusting your price because your costs rose, which is a normal part of running a business. A confident, respectful announcement earns more trust than a long apology.

**What if I lose customers after the increase?**
Expect a small drop at first, and judge the result after three to four weeks. If your revenue and profit are higher despite selling slightly less, the increase worked. Only consider reversing the price if sales fall sharply and do not recover after a month.

**Should I offer a discount to soften the increase?**
A short grace period at the old price for loyal customers is better than a discount on the new price. A discount trains customers to expect lower prices, while a grace period rewards loyalty and then lets the new price stand.

## What to Do Next

1. Open your records and calculate your true cost per item or service, including transport, packaging, delivery, fuel, and bank charges.
2. Work out your new price, aiming for a 10 to 20 percent increase, and test it on paper to confirm your profit improves even if a few customers leave.
3. Write a short, honest announcement giving one clear reason for the change and the date the new prices start.
4. Tell your most loyal customers personally, one to two weeks before the public announcement.
5. Post the announcement on your WhatsApp status, broadcast list, and social media pages, and pin it where customers will see it.
6. Add a small improvement — better packaging, faster delivery, or a small extra — so the new price feels like fair value.
7. Prepare three calm replies for the most common objections: too expensive, the other seller is cheaper, and I will think about it.
8. Track your sales, revenue, and profit for the first month, and judge the result after three to four weeks, not after the first quiet week.
