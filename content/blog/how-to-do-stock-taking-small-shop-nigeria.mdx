Ask a shop owner in Lagos how much stock they have, and you will often get a shrug. They know roughly what is on the shelves, they know when something is finished because a customer asked for it and it was not there, and they know they spent money on goods last week — but they do not know the exact value of what they own, what is missing, or which products are quietly eating their money by sitting unsold for months.

That is what stock taking fixes. Stock taking, also called inventory counting, is the process of physically counting everything in your shop, writing it down, and comparing it with what your records say you should have. It sounds boring, and many small business owners in Nigeria never do it. But it is one of the most valuable things you can do for your business, because it tells you the truth about your money. Your stock is your money sitting on shelves in the form of goods. If you do not know what you have, you do not know what your business is worth, and you cannot tell whether you are making a profit or slowly losing money to theft, damage, or dead stock.

## Why Stock Taking Matters for a Small Shop

The first reason is that it finds missing goods. In any shop, some stock disappears. Sometimes it is theft by staff or by customers. Sometimes it is damage that was never recorded — a bottle that broke, a carton that got wet in the rain, a product that expired. Sometimes it is a simple mistake, like selling two items but recording one. Without stock taking, these losses stay hidden, and you keep ordering more goods to replace stock that was never actually sold. A shop that does regular stock taking often discovers that 2 to 5 percent of its stock value is lost each year. On ₦2,000,000 of stock, that is ₦40,000 to ₦100,000 — money that should have been profit.

The second reason is that it stops you from buying what you already have. Every shop owner has ordered goods only to discover a forgotten carton at the back of the store. Stock taking shows you exactly what is on your shelves and in your store room, so you buy only what you need. That frees up cash for products that actually sell.

The third reason is that it shows you what is not selling. Every shop has dead stock — products that have sat on the shelf for months because customers do not want them, the price is too high, or the style is out of fashion. Stock taking forces you to look at these items, and once you see them, you can do something about them: reduce the price, bundle them with a popular product, or move them to a better position in the shop. Dead stock is money that is stuck. The longer it sits, the more it costs you, because that money could have been used to buy products that sell.

The fourth reason is that it helps you price correctly. When you know exactly what you paid for each item and how many you have, you can calculate your real profit on each product. You may discover that your best-selling product actually makes very little profit, while a product you barely notice makes the most money.

Finally, stock taking gives you the records you need for bigger things: a loan application, a tax filing, or an insurance claim if your shop is burgled or flooded. A bank that asks for your stock value will not accept "I think I have plenty". They want a number, and stock taking gives you that number.

## How Often Should You Do Stock Taking?

For a small shop, a full stock count once a month is a good target, with a quick partial count every week. A full count means counting everything in the shop and the store room. A partial count means counting your most valuable or most stolen items — phones, perfumes, expensive fabrics, or the products that move fastest.

If your shop is small — one room, a few hundred items — a full count may take two to four hours. If your shop is bigger, it may take a full day. Choose a time when the shop is quiet, such as a Sunday morning or an evening after closing, so you are not counting while customers are buying and changing the numbers.

The most important rule is consistency. Stock taking once is useful, but stock taking every month is powerful, because it lets you compare. You can see which products are selling faster, which ones are slowing down, and whether your losses are growing or shrinking. Put a fixed date in your calendar — for example, the last Sunday of every month — and treat it as a business appointment you do not cancel.

## What You Need Before You Start

You do not need expensive equipment. You need a stock sheet, a pen, a calculator, and a way to record the results. A stock sheet is a simple table with columns for the product name, the quantity you count, the cost price per unit, and the total value. You can rule this in a notebook, print it from a spreadsheet, or use a simple app on your phone.

Next, stop all sales and deliveries during the count. If a customer buys something in the middle of your count, your numbers will be wrong. If a supplier delivers new stock, it will mix with what you are counting. Close the shop, put a sign on the door, and tell your staff that no goods move in or out until the count is finished.

Prepare your records too. Gather your purchase invoices, your sales book, and any records of damaged or returned goods. You will need these after the count, when you compare what you counted with what your records say you should have. If you do not keep any records at all, the count itself becomes your starting record — which is fine. Many small shops start their record-keeping with their first stock take.

## Step by Step: How to Count Your Stock

Step one: count everything, one product at a time. Go shelf by shelf, and count each product by hand. Do not estimate. Count the actual items — the bottles, the pieces, the cartons. For products sold by weight, like rice or garri, weigh them. For products in cartons, open the cartons and count the units inside, at least for a sample, because a carton that should contain 24 pieces may contain 20.

Step two: write down what you count immediately. Do not count ten products and then write them down from memory. Write each count on the stock sheet as you go. If two people are counting, one person counts and the other writes, and they call out the numbers to each other. This is faster and more accurate than counting alone.

Step three: mark what you have counted. Put a small tick or a sticker on the shelf or the carton after you count it, so you do not count the same items twice or miss a shelf. This is a small step that prevents big errors.

Step four: count the store room and any stock kept at home or in another location. Many shop owners forget the goods stored at home, in a back room, or with a supplier. All of it counts as your stock, and it all needs to be in the record.

Step five: note the condition of the stock as you count. Write down items that are damaged, expired, or close to expiring. Write down items that are dusty, faded, or out of fashion. These notes are as valuable as the numbers, because they tell you what needs to be cleared, discounted, or thrown away.

Step six: add up the value. For each product, multiply the quantity you counted by the cost price — what you paid for it, not what you sell it for. Add all the products together, and you have the total value of your stock. This is the number that tells you how much of your money is sitting on your shelves.

## Comparing Your Count with Your Records

Counting is only half of stock taking. The other half is comparing what you counted with what your records say you should have. This is where you find the problems.

For each product, work out what you should have: your opening stock, plus what you bought, minus what you sold, minus what was damaged or returned. Compare that number with what you actually counted. If the numbers match, good. If they do not, you have a difference — a shortage or an overage — and you need to find out why.

A small difference is normal. A few missing sachets of detergent or a small counting error is expected. But a large difference is a warning. If you should have 50 bottles of perfume and you count 38, that is 12 bottles missing, and at ₦8,000 each, that is ₦96,000 gone. That is not a counting error. That is theft, damage, or a recording mistake, and it needs to be investigated.

If the records are correct and stock is still missing, look at the pattern. Is the same product always short? Is the shortage worse on days when a particular person is on duty? Is the missing stock the expensive, small, easy-to-hide kind? The pattern usually points to the cause. Talk to your staff calmly and privately. Accusations without evidence destroy trust, but a quiet conversation about a pattern of losses often brings out the truth.

## Dealing with Dead Stock and Expiring Goods

Every stock take will reveal products that have not sold for a long time. Do not ignore them. Dead stock is money that is not working for you, and the longer it sits, the less it is worth.

First, sort your dead stock into three groups. Group one is products that are still good but priced too high or displayed badly. Reduce the price by 20 to 30 percent, move them to the front of the shop, or bundle them with a popular product. A slow-selling cream bundled free with a popular soap will move faster than the cream alone.

Group two is products that are close to expiring. If you sell food, drugs, or cosmetics, check expiry dates during every stock take. Put items that expire within three months at the front, reduce the price, and tell your regular customers. Never sell an expired product — it can harm a customer and destroy your reputation, and it is against the law. Dispose of expired goods properly and record the loss.

Group three is products that simply will not sell — out-of-fashion clothes, a brand nobody wants, a size nobody buys. Cut the price sharply and clear them, even at cost price or a small loss. Getting ₦5,000 back for goods you paid ₦7,000 for is better than getting nothing when they finally end up in the bin. The money you recover can buy products that sell.

After each stock take, make a simple list of your slowest products and decide what to do with each one. Do not reorder dead stock. If a product has not sold in three months, stop buying it, no matter how cheap the supplier offers it.

## Using Your Stock Records to Order Smarter

Stock taking is not just about counting — it is about ordering better. Your stock records tell you exactly how fast each product sells, and that tells you how much to order and when.

Work out the sales rate for your most important products: how many units do you sell in a week or a month? If you sell 20 cartons of noodles a month and you have 5 cartons left, you have about one week of stock. That tells you when to reorder. If you sell only 2 cartons of a particular biscuit a month and you have 10 cartons, you have five months of stock — do not order more.

Set a reorder level for each important product: the quantity at which you place a new order. The reorder level should cover the time it takes for the supplier to deliver, plus a small safety margin. If your supplier takes three days to deliver and you sell 5 cartons a day, your reorder level should be at least 15 cartons, plus a few extra for safety. When your stock falls to that level, order immediately — do not wait until you are out of stock, because an empty shelf means lost sales.

Your stock records also show you which products make you the most money. A product that sells fast but has a thin margin may be less valuable than a product that sells slowly but has a large margin. Focus your money and your shelf space on the products that give you the best return, and reduce the space given to products that take up room but earn little.

## Simple Tools for Stock Taking

You do not need to buy expensive inventory software to do stock taking well. A notebook and a pen are enough to start, and many successful small shops in Nigeria run on a notebook for years. The important thing is that the records are complete, consistent, and kept in one place.

If you want to go digital, a simple spreadsheet on your phone works well. Google Sheets is free, works on any Android phone, and lets you add up values automatically. Create columns for product name, opening stock, purchases, sales, counted stock, difference, cost price, and total value. Update it after each stock take, and you will have a record that grows more useful every month.

There are also free and cheap inventory apps designed for small shops, such as Loyverse, which lets you record sales and track stock on your phone, and works offline when your MTN or Airtel network is down. Some point-of-sale apps used by Nigerian shops also include stock tracking. The best tool is the one you will actually use every day, so choose the simplest one that fits your shop.

Whatever tool you use, keep a backup. If your records are in a notebook, take a photo of each page with your phone. If they are on your phone, save a copy to Google Drive. Losing your stock records is almost as bad as losing the stock itself.

If you sell online as well as in your shop, your stock taking should cover both. Goods you have listed on WhatsApp, Instagram, or Jumia are still your stock, and selling the same item twice — once in the shop and once online — is a common and embarrassing mistake. A single stock record that covers all your sales channels prevents it.

## Your Monthly Stock Taking Routine

Here is a simple routine you can follow every month. On the last Sunday of the month, close the shop after trading hours. Tidy the shelves and group similar products together. Gather your stock sheets, your sales book, and your purchase invoices. Count everything, shelf by shelf, writing down each count as you go. Count the store room too. Add up the total value of your stock. Compare the count with your records and investigate any big differences. List your dead stock and your expiring goods, and decide what to do with each one. Update your reorder levels and place orders for the products that are running low. Then file the stock sheet safely, and take a photo of it as a backup.

The whole process takes a few hours, once a month. The first time will be the slowest, because your shop is not organised for counting and your records may be incomplete. But each month it gets faster and easier, and the information gets more valuable. After three or four months, you will know your shop in a way you never did before — what sells, what does not, what is missing, and how much money is sitting on your shelves.

Stock taking is not a punishment or a chore. It is how a serious business owner knows their business. Do it every month, and your shop will run better, your money will go further, and the surprises — the empty shelf, the missing perfume, the carton you forgot you had — will become rare instead of routine.
