December in Nigeria is a busy month. Markets are crowded, customers are buying for Christmas, and business owners are working long hours to meet the rush. Then January comes, and many business owners start the new year exactly the way they ended the old one — tired, disorganised, and repeating the same mistakes.

A year-end review stops that cycle. It is a simple, honest look back at your business over the past year: what you earned, what you spent, what worked, what failed, and what you will do differently next year. It does not need a consultant, a fancy spreadsheet, or a whole week. It needs a few quiet hours, your records, and the willingness to be honest with yourself.

Many Nigerian small business owners never do a year-end review. They know roughly how the year went, but they do not know their real profit, their best product, or their most loyal customers. They carry the same problems into the new year because they never stopped to name them.

This guide explains how to do a year-end review for your small business in Nigeria, step by step. You will learn how to review your money, your customers, your products, your marketing, and your time, and how to turn what you learn into a simple, clear plan for the new year.

## Why a Year-End Review Matters

A year-end review matters because memory is unreliable. In December, you may remember the good month in June and forget the three slow months that followed. You may remember the big customer who paid well and forget the ten small customers who never paid at all. A review replaces feelings with facts.

First, a review shows you whether your business actually made money. Many small businesses in Nigeria look busy but are not profitable. They sell a lot, but they spend almost everything on stock, transport, and other costs. A review tells you the truth about your profit, and that truth helps you make better decisions.

Second, a review shows you what to keep and what to stop. Every business has products that sell well and products that sit on the shelf. Every business has marketing that works and marketing that wastes money. A review helps you keep the good and stop the bad.

Third, a review helps you set goals that are real. Instead of saying "I want to grow next year," you can say "I want to increase my monthly profit from N150,000 to N250,000 by selling more of my best product and stopping my worst one." That is a goal you can plan for and measure.

## Before You Start: Gather Your Records

A review is only as good as the records you use. Before you start, gather everything you have.

Collect your sales records: your notebook, your POS records, your bank statements, your WhatsApp chats with customers, and your invoices. If you kept a sales book, bring it. If you did not keep one, gather what you have — bank alerts, transfer records, and receipts — and do your best. This year, note where your records are weak, so you can keep better records next year.

Collect your expense records: receipts for stock, transport, rent, data, packaging, staff wages, and any other costs. Check your bank statements for expenses you forgot. Many business owners are surprised by how much they spent on small things — airtime, transport, and food for staff — that added up over the year.

Find a quiet time and a quiet place. A year-end review needs focus. Do it on a Sunday afternoon, or early in the morning before the shop opens. Bring a notebook and a pen, or open a simple spreadsheet. You will need about three hours. Do not rush it.

## Step 1: Review Your Money — Sales, Costs, and Profit

Start with money, because money is the clearest measure of how your business did.

First, add up your total sales for the year. Go month by month if you can. Write down each month's sales. You will see patterns: which months were strong, which months were slow, and whether your sales grew over the year or stayed flat.

Next, add up your total costs. Include the cost of stock or materials, transport, rent, staff wages, data and airtime, packaging, and any other business expense. Be honest. Do not leave out costs because they feel small. Small costs add up.

Then, subtract your costs from your sales. That is your profit before other expenses like tax or loan repayments. If your sales were N5 million and your costs were N4 million, your profit was N1 million. Write that number down. It is the most important number in your review.

Now ask three questions. Which months made the most profit, and why? Which costs grew the most, and can you reduce them? Are you charging enough for your products? Many Nigerian small businesses discover at this stage that their prices are too low. If your profit is thin, a small price increase on your best product may help more than selling more.

## Step 2: Review Your Customers — Who Bought, Who Left, Who Returned

Your customers are your business. A year-end review should tell you who they are and how they behaved.

First, count your customers. How many different customers bought from you this year? How many of them bought more than once? How many bought only once and never returned? Your repeat customers are your most valuable asset. They cost you nothing to find, and they buy again without being convinced.

Next, identify your best customers. Who spent the most money with you? Who ordered most often? Who referred other customers to you? Write down their names. These are the people to thank, to reward, and to protect. A short thank-you message in December, or a small discount in January, can keep them loyal for another year.

Also review your complaints. What did customers complain about most this year? Delivery, quality, price, or communication? If three or more customers complained about the same thing, that is not a customer problem. It is a business problem, and you should fix it in January.

Finally, check where your customers found you. Did most come from Instagram, from WhatsApp, from referrals, or from walk-ins? Put more effort next year into the channels that brought you the best customers, and less into the ones that brought you nothing.

## Step 3: Review Your Products and Services

Every product you sell should earn its place. A year-end review is the time to judge them honestly.

List your products or services and, for each one, note how much it sold, how much profit it made, and how much trouble it caused. Some products sell well and make good profit. Keep those. Some products sell well but make little profit because the costs are high. Consider raising their price or finding cheaper materials. Some products barely sell at all. They take up space, time, and money. Consider dropping them.

Think about your best seller. What makes it your best seller? Is it the price, the quality, or the demand? Can you sell more of it next year? Can you create a similar product that your customers will also want?

Think about your worst seller. Why did it not sell? Was the price wrong? Was the product poor? Did customers not understand it? If you cannot fix it, stop selling it. Dropping a bad product is not failure. It is good business. It frees your money and your time for products that work.

## Step 4: Review Your Marketing — What Brought Customers

Marketing costs money and time, so you should know what worked and what did not.

List everything you did to find customers this year: Instagram posts, WhatsApp status, paid ads, flyers, referrals, market days, and any other effort. For each one, ask: did it bring customers, and how much did it cost?

If you ran paid ads, check the results. How much did you spend, and how many customers did you get? If you spent N50,000 on ads and got two customers, the ads did not work. If you spent nothing on WhatsApp status and got twenty customers, your status is your best marketing tool.

If you posted on social media, check which posts got the most orders, not just the most likes. Likes do not pay rent. Orders do. A post with ten likes and five orders is better than a post with five hundred likes and no orders.

Decide what to keep, what to stop, and what to try. Keep the marketing that brought real customers. Stop the marketing that cost money and brought nothing. Try one new marketing idea in the new year, and measure it properly.

## Step 5: Review Your Time and Your Team

A business is not only money and products. It is also time and people. A year-end review should look at both.

First, review your own time. How many hours did you work each week? Were you working on the right things, or were you busy with tasks that did not earn money? Many business owners spend hours on small tasks — replying to messages, running errands — and no time on the big tasks that grow the business, like finding new customers or improving products. Decide what you will stop doing, what you will do less of, and what you will do more of next year.

Second, review your staff, if you have any. Who performed well? Who needs training? Who caused problems? Be honest. A staff member who is rude to customers can cost you more than their salary saves you. Plan any training, changes, or difficult conversations for January, not December, when everyone is busy.

Third, review your suppliers. Who delivered on time and at a fair price? Who disappointed you? A reliable supplier is worth keeping, even if they are slightly more expensive. An unreliable supplier who is cheap can cost you customers. Decide which suppliers to keep and which to replace.

Fourth, review your systems. Did you keep good records? Did you track your stock? Did you follow up on orders? If your systems are weak, your business will stay small. Choose one system to improve in the new year — for example, keeping a daily sales book, or using a simple spreadsheet to track stock.

## Step 6: Set Clear Goals for the New Year

A review without goals is just a history lesson. The point of looking back is to plan forward.

Set three to five goals for the new year. Not twenty. Three to five goals you can remember and work on every week. Make each goal specific and measurable.

A good goal sounds like: "Increase monthly profit from N150,000 to N250,000 by June." Or: "Get fifty new repeat customers by March." Or: "Launch two new products in the first quarter." Or: "Stop selling on credit to new customers." A bad goal sounds like: "Grow my business." That is a wish, not a goal.

For each goal, write down the first three steps you will take in January. If your goal is to increase profit, your steps might be: raise the price of your best product by ten percent, stop selling your worst product, and find a cheaper supplier for packaging. Small steps, taken in January, are better than big plans that never start.

Also set a financial goal for the year: how much profit do you want to make, and how much do you want to save? Many Nigerian business owners never save, because they never decide to. Decide now. Even a small monthly saving — N20,000 or N50,000 — will give you a cushion for slow months and money to invest when an opportunity comes.

Write your goals down and put them where you will see them — on your wall, in your notebook, or on your phone. Review them every month. A goal you never look at is a goal you will forget.

## Step 7: Make a Simple Plan for January

January is a slow month for many Nigerian businesses. After the Christmas rush, customers spend less. A good plan turns a slow month into a useful month.

Plan your January in three parts. First, fix what the review showed was broken. Chase unpaid debts. Fix the delivery problem. Drop the bad product. Raise the price that was too low. Do these fixes in the first two weeks of January, while you have time.

Second, thank your best customers. Send them a New Year message. Offer them a small discount or a free gift with their next order. Loyal customers who feel appreciated will keep buying, even in a slow month.

Third, prepare for the year ahead. Restock your best products. Update your prices. Clean your shop. Update your social media profiles and your WhatsApp catalogue. A clean, organised start to the year sets the tone for everything that follows.

Write your January plan on one page. Keep it simple. A one-page plan you follow is better than a twenty-page plan you never read.

## A Real Example: A Fashion Seller Reviews Her Year

Consider a fashion seller in Abuja who sells dresses online and from a small shop. In December, she sits down with her notebook and her bank statements for a year-end review.

She adds up her sales: N6 million for the year. She adds up her costs: N4.8 million. Her profit is N1.2 million — about N100,000 a month. She is disappointed. She thought she had earned more.

She looks closer. Her best product is a simple Ankara dress that sells for N25,000 and costs N12,000 to make. Her worst product is an imported dress that sells slowly and costs a lot to stock. She decides to drop the imported dress and focus on the Ankara dress, with two new designs in January.

She checks her customers. Most of her sales come from Instagram, but her repeat customers come from WhatsApp. She decides to spend more time on WhatsApp and less on Instagram ads that brought her likes but few orders.

She checks her debts. Three customers owe her N180,000 for dresses they took on credit. She decides to chase those debts in January and to stop selling on credit to new customers.

She sets three goals for the new year: increase monthly profit to N200,000, get thirty new repeat customers, and save N50,000 every month. She writes them down and puts them on her shop wall.

Her review took one afternoon. It gave her a clear picture of her business and a simple plan for the new year. That is what a year-end review does.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

First, do not skip the review because your records are messy. A rough review is better than no review. Do your best with what you have, and keep better records next year.

Second, do not blame the economy for everything. The economy is hard, but your review should focus on what you can control: your prices, your products, your customers, and your costs.

Third, do not set too many goals. Five goals are enough. Twenty goals mean you will achieve none of them.

Fourth, do not keep the review in your head. Write it down. A review you write down is a plan. A review you only think about is a daydream.

Fifth, do not do the review alone if you have a partner or a trusted staff member. A second person may see things you missed, and they may remember problems you forgot.

## Your Year-End Review Checklist

Here is a simple checklist you can use this December. Tick each item as you finish it. Add up your total sales for the year. Add up your total costs. Calculate your profit. List the customers who owe you money. Identify your best and worst products. Identify your best customers. Review your marketing and stop what did not work. Review your time, your staff, and your suppliers. Set three to five goals for the new year. Write a one-page plan for January.

Do the review before the new year starts, not after. Choose a quiet day in December, gather your records, and work through the checklist. It will take a few hours, but those hours will save you months of guesswork next year.

Your business has taught you a lot this year — about money, customers, products, and yourself. Take the time to learn the lessons before you move on. Review the year, keep what worked, fix what did not, and start the new year with a clear plan. That is how small businesses grow into big ones.
