Many Nigerian small businesses make sales every day but still do not know whether they are making a profit. Money comes in through bank transfer, POS and cash, and it goes out for stock, transport, data, rent and personal needs, all from the same pocket. At the end of the month, the owner cannot say how much the business earned, how much it spent, or how much is left. A simple spreadsheet fixes that. You do not need accounting software, an accountant, or a degree in finance. You need one file, a few columns, and ten minutes a day to record what happened. This guide shows you how to build that spreadsheet, use it every day, and turn it into decisions that keep your business alive and growing, whether you run a shop in Lagos, a food business in Abuja, or an online store selling across Nigeria.

## Why a Spreadsheet Beats a Notebook and Your Memory

A notebook is better than nothing, but it is hard to search, hard to add up, and easy to lose. Your memory is worse: after a busy week, you will forget the ₦3,500 you spent on transport on Tuesday and the ₦12,000 you paid a supplier in cash on Thursday. A spreadsheet adds up automatically, so you do not need a calculator, and it shows you patterns a notebook cannot. You can see at a glance that you spent more on delivery in the second week of the month, or that weekends bring most of your sales, or that one product brings most of your profit. A spreadsheet is also free. Google Sheets costs nothing, works on a phone, and saves automatically, so you will not lose your records if your phone spoils. Excel works too if you already have it. The point is not the tool. The point is having one place where every naira in and every naira out is written down, on the day it happens, so your decisions are based on facts instead of feelings.

## Step 1: Choose Your Tool and Set Up One File

Start with Google Sheets if you have a Gmail account, because it is free, it works on any phone, and you can open it from your shop, your home, or a customer's location. Open sheets.google.com, create a blank spreadsheet, and name it clearly, for example "My Business Finances 2026". If you prefer Excel, create the same file on your laptop and save a copy to your phone or Google Drive so you are not stuck when you are away from the computer. Inside the file, create five tabs along the bottom. Name them Income, Expenses, Stock, Cash Flow, and Monthly Summary. Five tabs sound like a lot, but each one has a simple job, and you will only use one or two of them on a normal day. Do not build a complicated file with dozens of columns. A file you find confusing is a file you will stop using, and a file you stop using is worse than no file at all. Keep it simple, keep it in one place, and protect it with your Google account password so nobody else can open it.

## Step 2: Build Your Income Sheet in Ten Minutes

Your Income tab records every sale. Set up these columns across the top: Date, Customer or Order, Product or Service, Quantity, Amount Received, Payment Method, and Notes. Each row is one sale. For example, on 4 November, a customer named Ada buys two Ankara dresses for ₦35,000 each, pays ₦70,000 by bank transfer, and you write that in one row. If you sell many small items in a day, such as food or phone accessories, do not write a row for every single item. Instead, write one row per day with the total sales for that day, and keep the paper receipts or your POS report as backup. At the bottom of the Amount Received column, use the SUM formula to add everything up. In Google Sheets, click the last empty cell in that column and type =SUM, then select the amounts above it. Now your total income updates itself every time you add a sale. Add a filter to the Payment Method column so you can see how much came by transfer, how much by POS, and how much in cash. That matters in Nigeria, because cash is easy to spend without recording, and transfers leave a bank trail you can check.

## Step 3: Build Your Expenses Sheet the Same Way

Your Expenses tab records every naira that leaves the business. Set up these columns: Date, What Was Paid For, Category, Amount, Payment Method, and Receipt or Notes. Categories keep your spending organised. Use simple ones that fit your business: Stock, Transport, Rent, Data and Airtime, Packaging, Salaries, Marketing, Food and Drinks for the shop, Repairs, and Personal Withdrawal. Every time you spend money, write it down the same day. A ₦2,000 delivery, a ₦5,000 data subscription, a ₦15,000 pack of nylon bags, and a ₦40,000 stock purchase all go in the same sheet. At the bottom, add a SUM formula for the Amount column, just like the Income tab. Once a week, sort the sheet by Category and look at the totals. You may be surprised. Many small business owners discover they spend more on transport and feeding than they realised, or that small daily expenses add up to more than rent. You cannot fix a leak you cannot see, and this sheet shows you the leaks. If you buy stock with a transfer, note the supplier name in the Notes column, so you can trace a payment later if a delivery is short or late.

## Step 4: Record Sales Every Day, Without Exception

A spreadsheet only works if it is up to date. The habit matters more than the design. Pick a fixed time every day to record your sales and expenses, for example 8pm after you close, or first thing in the morning before you open. Set a phone alarm for that time. Spend ten minutes: open the Income tab, add the day's sales, open the Expenses tab, add the day's spending, and close the file. If you sell through WhatsApp, your chat history is your record: scroll through the day's chats, note who paid, and add the amounts. If you sell in a shop, use your POS end-of-day report and your cash tin. Count the cash at the end of the day, write the total down, and put the cash in a safe place. Do not wait until Sunday to record a whole week. By then you will have forgotten half of it, and the numbers will be guesses. If you miss a day, catch up the next morning while the details are still fresh. If you have a staff member who handles sales, ask them to write down every sale in a small book during the day, and you transfer it to the spreadsheet at night. That way, you keep control even when you are not in the shop.

## Step 5: Separate Your Business Money From Your Personal Money

This is the hardest rule for many Nigerian small business owners, and the most important one. If your business money and your personal money live in the same account, you will never know whether the business is profitable, because personal spending will eat the profit without you noticing. Open a separate bank account or a separate wallet for the business, even if it is just a second account with your current bank, or a dedicated OPay or PalmPay account for business transactions. Pay all business income into that account, and pay all business expenses from it. Then pay yourself a fixed salary or allowance from the business, for example ₦100,000 a month, or ₦25,000 a week, depending on what the business can afford. Record that salary as Personal Withdrawal in your Expenses tab. If you need extra money for a personal emergency, record it as a withdrawal too, and do not hide it. A business that pays its owner a steady salary is easier to manage than one where the owner takes money whenever they feel like it. It also makes the Monthly Summary honest: if the business cannot pay your salary and still make a profit, you need to raise prices, cut costs, or sell more, and your spreadsheet will show you that clearly.

## Step 6: Track Stock So Your Money Does Not Disappear Into Shelves

Stock is money sitting on a shelf. If you do not track it, you will keep buying what you already have, run out of what sells fastest, and lose money to damage, theft, or expiry. Your Stock tab should have these columns: Product, Opening Quantity, Bought This Month, Sold This Month, Closing Quantity, Cost Price, and Selling Price. Update it once a week. Count what is on the shelf, write the number down, and compare it with what your Income tab says you sold. If the numbers do not match, find out why before the gap grows. Maybe a staff member forgot to record a sale, maybe a product was damaged, or maybe items are going missing. For a food business, track your main ingredients the same way: bags of rice, cartons of chicken, litres of oil. For a service business, track your materials: hair products, cleaning chemicals, printing paper. You do not need to track every small item. Track the items that cost you the most money and the items that bring you the most sales. When you know your cost price and your selling price for each product, you can calculate your profit per item, and you can stop spending time on products that look busy but earn almost nothing.

## Step 7: Do a Monthly Profit Check That Takes Thirty Minutes

At the end of every month, open your Monthly Summary tab and answer four questions. First, how much did the business earn? That is the total from your Income tab. Second, how much did it spend? That is the total from your Expenses tab, including stock, rent, transport, and your salary. Third, what is the profit? Subtract the second number from the first. If income was ₦850,000 and expenses were ₦640,000, your profit is ₦210,000. Fourth, where did the money go? Look at your expense categories and find the biggest three. Those are the costs to watch next month. Write the four answers at the top of your Monthly Summary tab, month by month, so you can compare January with February, and this year with last year. A business that earns more in December than in January is normal for many Nigerian traders, but you will only see the pattern if you keep the monthly record. If the month shows a loss, do not panic and do not hide the number. Ask why: did you buy too much stock, did sales drop, did a big expense like rent fall in that month? A loss you understand is a problem you can fix. A loss you ignore will repeat itself.

## Step 8: Plan Cash Flow, Savings and Big Expenses

Profit and cash are not the same thing. You can make a profit on paper and still have no cash in your account, because your money is tied up in stock, or because customers owe you. Your Cash Flow tab helps you see the cash you actually have. Each week, write down your opening bank balance, add the money that came in, subtract the money that went out, and write the closing balance. Then look ahead to the next month: what big payments are coming? Rent in Lekki can be ₦1,200,000 a year, a new freezer can cost ₦450,000, and a bulk stock order can cost ₦300,000. If you do not plan for those, they will hit you as emergencies. Set aside a small amount every week for the next big expense. If rent is due in six months, divide the rent by 24 weeks and save that amount weekly in a separate savings account or a fixed wallet. Do the same for tax, for equipment repairs, and for the December rush when you need extra stock. Keep a small emergency fund too, even if it is only ₦50,000 to start with. A generator repair or a sudden price increase should not close your shop. When customers owe you, keep a list of who owes what, follow up on WhatsApp politely but firmly, and stop giving credit to anyone who does not pay on time.

## Step 9: Avoid the Mistakes That Kill Small Business Finances

The most common mistake is not recording cash sales, because cash feels like free money. It is not. Every cash sale belongs in the Income tab. The second mistake is forgetting small expenses, such as ₦1,000 for pure water, ₦2,500 for bike delivery, or ₦4,000 for airtime. Small expenses add up, and over a month they can reach tens of thousands of naira. The third mistake is pricing without knowing your costs. If you buy a dress for ₦18,000, pay ₦2,500 for delivery, spend ₦1,500 on packaging, and sell it for ₦22,000, your real profit is only ₦0 after those costs, and you have worked for nothing. Use your spreadsheet to calculate the true cost of each product before you set a price. The fourth mistake is borrowing to pay for daily expenses instead of fixing the underlying problem. A loan can help you buy stock for a busy season, but it should not pay your rent every month. The fifth mistake is keeping everything in your head because you are too busy. Ten minutes a day is less time than you spend scrolling on your phone, and it is the difference between a business that grows and one that quietly runs out of cash. ## Final Thoughts

Managing your business finances with a spreadsheet is not about becoming an accountant. It is about knowing, every day, how much came in, how much went out, and how much is left. Start today with one file, five tabs, and ten minutes a day. Record your sales, record your expenses, separate your personal money from your business money, and check your profit at the end of the month. After three months, you will know which products make you money, which costs are eating your profit, and how much you can afford to pay yourself. That knowledge is worth more than any loan, because it tells you the truth about your business. A business that knows its numbers can plan, can grow, and can survive a slow month. A business that does not know its numbers is guessing, and guessing is expensive. Open your spreadsheet today, write down today's sales, and keep the habit going. Your future self will thank you for it.
