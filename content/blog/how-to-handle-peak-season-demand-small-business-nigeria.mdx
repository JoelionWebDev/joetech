Every year, the same thing happens to Nigerian small businesses. December arrives, and suddenly everyone is buying. Customers who have been quiet all year want gifts, party outfits, foodstuffs, hampers, hair, shoes and decorations. A fashion seller in Lekki who normally sells ten dresses a week suddenly gets fifty orders. A foodstuff seller in Mile 12 sees queues from morning till night. A baker in Abuja gets more cake orders in two weeks than in the previous two months. For many businesses, the last six weeks of the year bring in more money than the previous six months combined.

But peak season is also when small businesses break. Stock runs out in the middle of the rush. Staff are overwhelmed and start making mistakes. Money comes in fast and disappears just as fast. Deliveries arrive late, customers get angry, and some never come back. The difference between a business that grows during peak season and one that collapses under it is not luck. It is planning. This guide shows you how to prepare for peak demand in Nigeria, whether your busy season is December, Eid, Easter, Valentine's Day, wedding season or the back-to-school rush.

## What Peak Season Looks Like in Nigeria

Peak season in Nigeria follows the calendar of festivals, school terms and cultural events. December is the biggest peak for most businesses because of Christmas, Detty December parties, weddings and end-of-year celebrations. Nigerians abroad return home with money to spend, companies buy gifts for staff and clients, and families stock up on food and clothes. Sales can easily double or triple for retailers, fashion sellers, food vendors, event planners, photographers, makeup artists and transport businesses.

Other peaks matter too. Ramadan and Eid bring a rush for foodstuffs, clothing and gifts in the north and among Muslim customers everywhere. Easter brings travel, food and fashion demand. Valentine's Day is a short but intense peak for gift sellers, florists, restaurants and hotels. Wedding season, which runs strongly from November to February, keeps makeup artists, photographers, caterers, decorators and tailors busy for months. Back-to-school season in August and September is the biggest peak for sellers of school uniforms, bags, shoes, books and stationery.

Your peak depends on what you sell and who your customers are. A seller of school items peaks in August, not December. A caterer peaks during wedding season and festive periods. The first step is to know your own calendar. Look at your sales records from last year. Which months were your best? Which weeks were the busiest? If you do not have records, think back honestly. When did customers call you the most? When did you run out of stock? Write those months down, because that is your peak season, and that is what you need to plan for.

## Step 1: Study Last Year and Forecast This Year

Good peak season planning starts with looking backwards. Before you buy a single item, sit down with your sales records, your bank statements, your notebooks or your WhatsApp chats and work out what happened last peak season. How much did you sell in your best week? Which products sold out first? Which products sat on the shelf? How many customers did you serve per day at the busiest point? How much money did you spend on stock, and how much profit did you make?

Write the numbers down. If you sold 200 units of your best product last December and you expect similar or slightly higher demand this year, you need at least 200 units, plus a safety margin of maybe 20 percent. If a product did not sell at all last December, do not buy more of it this year just because you have it in stock. Focus your money on the products that actually move. This sounds obvious, but many business owners buy based on what they like rather than what their customers bought.

Also think about what has changed since last year. Have your prices gone up? Have you gained new customers through social media? Is there a new competitor nearby? Is the economy tighter, meaning customers may spend less per person but more people may buy? A realistic forecast is not a guess. It is last year's numbers adjusted for what you know about this year. If you are a new business without last year's numbers, start small. Order a moderate amount, watch your sales daily during the first week of the peak, and reorder quickly. It is better to sell out and restock than to be stuck with unsold goods in January.

## Step 2: Stock Up Early Before Prices Rise

One of the biggest mistakes Nigerian business owners make is waiting until peak season starts before buying stock. By then, prices have already gone up. Suppliers know demand is high, so they raise prices. Transport costs rise because every trader is moving goods at the same time. Markets are crowded, and the items you want may already be finished. Buying early saves money and saves stress.

Aim to buy your peak season stock four to six weeks before the rush begins. For December, that means buying in late October or early November. For back-to-school, buy in July. Go to your suppliers while they still have full stock and while prices are still normal. Negotiate firmly. If you are buying in bulk, ask for a discount. If you are a regular customer, remind the supplier of your loyalty and ask for the best price. Pay attention to the exchange rate if you import goods, because a sudden rise in the dollar can wipe out your profit if you buy late.

Store your stock properly. Peak season stock is money sitting on your shelf, and it needs protection. Keep goods in a dry, safe place, away from rain, rats and theft. If you sell foodstuffs, check expiry dates and store items so the oldest stock is sold first. If you sell fashion, keep items clean and pressed so they look new when customers arrive. Count your stock as it arrives and write it down. Knowing exactly what you have prevents the embarrassing situation of promising a customer an item you do not actually have.

## Step 3: Get Your Money Ready Before the Rush

Peak season needs cash. You need money to buy stock, pay extra staff, run adverts and cover delivery costs, and you need it before the sales start coming in. Many small businesses fail at peak season not because customers did not come, but because they did not have enough money to serve them. A shop that cannot buy enough stock cannot sell enough, no matter how many customers walk in.

Start saving for peak season at least two months ahead. Set aside a fixed amount every week from your regular sales. If your peak stock will cost ₦500,000, saving ₦60,000 a week for eight weeks gets you there. Open a separate savings account or use a locked savings option so the money is not spent on daily expenses. If you use a cooperative or ajo contribution, time your collection to land just before your buying period. The point is to have the cash ready when suppliers are still cheap.

Be careful with borrowing. Some business owners take loans or buy stock on credit for peak season. This can work if you are confident about your sales, but it is dangerous if you overestimate demand. Only borrow what you are sure you can repay from peak season sales, and understand the interest and the repayment date before you commit. Avoid borrowing from loan apps with very high interest rates unless you have no other option and the numbers clearly work.

During peak season, track your money daily. Write down every sale and every expense, even small ones like transport and packaging. At the end of each day, count your cash and check it against your records. Money leaks fast during a rush, through unrecorded sales, careless spending and small thefts. A business that makes ₦2 million in December but cannot account for ₦500,000 of it has not really succeeded. Keep your peak season profit safe by moving it into a separate account regularly instead of leaving all the cash in your shop.

## Step 4: Hire and Train Temporary Help

You cannot handle peak season alone if your normal workload doubles or triples. You need extra hands, and you need them trained before the rush starts, not in the middle of it. Decide early how many extra people you need and what they will do. A fashion shop might need one extra person to handle sales and another to handle packaging and deliveries. A food business might need extra hands for cooking, packing and dispatch. A salon might need an extra stylist or an assistant to manage bookings and attend to waiting customers.

Hire people you can trust, and agree on pay before they start. Be clear about the working hours, the daily wage or monthly pay, and how long the job will last. In Nigeria, many small businesses hire students on holiday, family members, or casual workers from their neighbourhood. Whoever you hire, train them properly. Show them how to greet customers, how to handle products, how to record a sale, how to package an order and how to answer common questions. Write down the important rules, such as prices and discount limits, so they do not have to guess.

Give your temporary staff clear roles. One person should be in charge of sales, another of packaging, another of deliveries. When everyone knows their job, work flows smoothly even when the shop is full. Hold a short meeting every morning during peak season, even if it is just five minutes. Tell your team what to expect that day, which items are running low, and which orders are urgent. A team that communicates well can handle twice the customers of a team that does not.

Also take care of your regular staff. Peak season means longer hours and more pressure. Pay them fairly, give them breaks, provide food or transport money where you can, and thank them. A tired, unhappy team makes mistakes and treats customers badly. A motivated team will work hard for you through the busiest weeks and come back next year.

## Step 5: Set Up Systems for Orders and Deliveries

When orders are few, you can manage them in your head or in a chat. When orders are many, that system collapses. During peak season, you need a simple order system that anyone on your team can follow. Create a notebook or a spreadsheet with columns for the date, customer name, phone number, items ordered, amount paid, balance remaining, delivery address and delivery status. Record every single order the moment it comes in. Never trust your memory when you are handling fifty orders a day.

Confirm every order clearly with the customer. Send a message restating what they ordered, the total amount, the delivery fee, and when they should expect it. Ask them to confirm. This prevents disputes later about what was ordered or how much was paid. If you take deposits, record the deposit and the balance clearly, and remind the customer of the balance before delivery.

Plan your deliveries carefully. Lagos traffic gets worse in December, and a delivery that normally takes one hour can take three. Group deliveries by area so one rider covers Lekki in the morning and Ikeja in the afternoon, instead of crisscrossing the city. Use a reliable dispatch rider or a logistics company, and agree on rates before peak season starts, because delivery prices also rise during the rush. Tell customers honestly how long delivery will take. It is better to promise three days and deliver in two than to promise one day and deliver in three. Late deliveries are the biggest source of peak season complaints.

If you sell in a physical shop, manage the crowd. Make sure your shop is clean, well arranged and easy to move around in. Put your best sellers where customers can see them immediately. Keep popular items within easy reach so staff can serve quickly. If you have a queue, serve customers in order and be polite about waiting times. A customer who waits ten minutes in a well-organised shop will come back. A customer who waits ten minutes in chaos will not.

## Step 6: Promote Early and Keep Customers Informed

Peak season marketing should start weeks before the rush, not on the day it begins. Customers plan their December spending in November. They choose where to buy gifts, clothes and food well before the last week. If you start promoting in mid-December, many of them have already spent their money elsewhere. Start your peak season promotion at least three to four weeks early.

Announce your peak season offers clearly. Tell your customers what is new, what is on offer, and when they should order to get delivery on time. Set an order deadline and say it plainly, for example "Order by 15 December for guaranteed delivery before Christmas". Deadlines create urgency and they protect you from a flood of last-minute orders you cannot fulfil. Post on your WhatsApp status regularly, share your catalogue, and remind your regular customers personally. A short message to your best customers, such as "Our December collection is ready, and our early customers get a discount this week", brings sales before the rush even starts.

Keep customers informed during the rush. If an item is running low, say so. If delivery will be slower than usual, warn customers in advance. If you are closing early on a particular day, announce it. Customers forgive delays when they are told in advance. They do not forgive being ignored or misled. Reply to messages as fast as you can, and if you cannot reply immediately, set an automatic message that tells the customer when you will respond.

After peak season, follow up. Thank your customers, ask for feedback, and let them know about your January offers. Many businesses make all their money in December and then go quiet in January. The smart ones use the goodwill from a good peak season to keep customers buying in the new year.

## What to Do When Things Go Wrong

Even with the best planning, something will go wrong during peak season. A supplier will fail to deliver. A rider will get stuck in traffic. A product will sell out faster than expected. What matters is how you respond. Stay calm, tell the customer the truth, and offer a solution. If an item is finished, suggest a similar one or offer to deliver as soon as it is back in stock. If a delivery is late, call the customer before they call you. People are far more understanding when you communicate first.

Keep a small emergency stock of your best sellers if you can. When your main stock runs out, this reserve buys you time to restock. Keep the phone numbers of two or three alternative suppliers, so you are not stranded if your main supplier disappoints you. Keep some cash in reserve for unexpected costs, like an urgent delivery or a repair. Peak season rewards the business that can solve problems quickly.

## Review, Rest and Prepare for Next Year

When peak season ends, do not just move on. Sit down within a week and review what happened. What sold well? What did not sell? How much profit did you make? Which days were the busiest? What complaints did you get? What would you do differently? Write the answers down and keep them somewhere safe. This review is the foundation of next year's plan, and it will save you from repeating the same mistakes.

Then rest. Peak season is exhausting, and you and your team need a break. Close for a day or two if you can, or at least slow down. A rested business owner makes better decisions in January than one who is burnt out. Use the quiet weeks of January to clean your shop, count your remaining stock, update your records, and plan your next peak. The businesses that handle peak season best are the ones that treat it as a cycle: plan, execute, review, rest, and then plan again, a little better each year.
