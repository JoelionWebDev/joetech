Open the Paystack website and the Moniepoint website side by side. Then open the website of a random Lagos business that sells phones at Computer Village and also "does tech." You can tell which ones are real tech companies in about three seconds, and it has almost nothing to do with how good their product is.

It is the identity. The real tech companies look calm. One or two colours. Plenty of empty space. Type you can read. A logo that survives being shrunk to the size of a fingernail on a phone screen. The other kind of site shouts: five colours, three fonts, a stock photo of a smiling man in a suit shaking hands, and a logo that looks like it was drawn in Microsoft Word.

Nobody teaches Nigerian tech founders this. They learn to code, they learn to pitch, and then they hand their brand to the cheapest designer on the street and hope for the best. Here is what separates a credible tech identity from one that quietly tells investors and customers not to trust you — with examples from the Nigerian startups that got it right, and the mistakes that keep giving founders away.

## The Test: Would a Stranger Trust You With Their Money?

A tech business asks for something no market trader asks for. It asks a stranger to type their card number into a website, or to keep their savings in an app. That is a trust transaction before it is a product transaction.

Think about Kuda. When Kuda launched, it was a bank with no branches, no queues, no marble banking hall. The only thing standing between Kuda and a customer thinking "is this real?" was how it looked. Kuda chose a loud purple and a plain white background, a simple wordmark, and a tone of voice that sounds like a clever friend, not a bank manager. The purple is doing serious work. It is the opposite of the dark blue every Nigerian bank used for fifty years. It says: we are not that kind of bank.

PiggyVest did the same thing with a deep blue. The blue says safe, steady, your money is not going anywhere. The product is savings, so the identity had to feel like a vault, not a party. The logo is a simple wordmark. The app is mostly white space with blue buttons. There is nothing on the screen competing for your attention, because when someone is about to lock away ₦50,000, the last thing they need is a screen that looks like a betting site.

Moniepoint went a different way and it still works, because the rules are the same. Its blue is brighter, its voice is louder, its whole identity is built for agents and small business owners standing in markets, not for office workers in Ikoyi. But look closely: it is still one main colour, one clean typeface, one logo used the same way everywhere. The identity fits its customer. It does not try to be Paystack.

That is the first rule of tech identity: look like you understand your customer, and like you will still exist next year.

## Clean Is Not Empty, and Modern Is Not Cold

Founders hear "clean and modern" and picture a blank white page with a tiny grey logo. That is not what Paystack looks like. Paystack's site is white and blue, yes, but it is full of real things: screenshots of the dashboard, names of real Nigerian businesses. Clean means one idea per screen, not zero ideas per screen. Modern means the type is readable and the buttons look like buttons.

The market-trader look is the opposite. It is the site that puts everything on the homepage at once: the logo, the slogan, three banners, a scrolling list of services, a photo gallery, a testimonial, a countdown timer and a WhatsApp button that follows you down the page. It is the digital version of a stallholder shouting all his prices at the same time. On a screen, shouting reads as panic.

A credible tech identity uses empty space the way a good speaker uses pauses. The space tells the visitor what matters. On the PiggyVest homepage, the headline gets the whole top of the screen to itself.

Here is a practical check. Screenshot your homepage, shrink it to phone size, and squint. If you can still tell what the company does and where the button is, your identity is working. If it turns into coloured noise, you have too much on the page.

## Mistake One: Too Many Colours

The single most common mistake is colour. A founder picks blue because it means trust, then adds green because it means growth, then orange because it means energy, then a designer adds a gradient because gradients are modern. The result is a brand that looks like a pack of assorted biscuits.

Count the colours on the Paystack site. Blue, white, dark navy text, and one green used only for success messages. That is it. Kuda: purple, white, black text. That is it. Flutterwave: orange and dark navy, with white. One strong colour, one dark colour, white space.

The discipline is simple: one main colour that people remember you by, one dark colour for text and structure, and white space doing the rest. A second bright colour is allowed only if it has a job — green for success, red for errors, nothing else. If a colour does not have a job, it does not go on the site.

A founder once showed me a fintech logo with seven colours and told me each one represented a value: blue for trust, green for growth, yellow for optimism, red for passion, purple for creativity, orange for energy, pink for community. I asked him which colour a customer would remember. He went quiet. The answer is none of them. A customer remembers one colour. Give them one worth remembering.

## Mistake Two: The Stock-Photo Logo

The second mistake is the logo that is not a logo. A globe with an arrow around it. A lightbulb. A handshake between two people in suits who have never been to Nigeria. A swoosh that looks like it fell off a sportswear brand. These images come free with design software, and every founder who uses them ends up looking like every other founder who used them.

A tech logo has a harder job than a market-stall sign. It has to work at 16 pixels wide as a browser tab icon, in white on a dark phone screen, and on a pitch deck projected in a room in Yaba. A globe with an arrow cannot do any of that. It turns into a smudge.

Look at what the Nigerian startups actually use. Paystack's mark is a simple geometric shape built from its own name. Flutterwave's is a wave made of clean lines. Kuda's is a wordmark with a small, simple symbol. None of them is a picture of what the company does. None of them needs to be. A logo is not an illustration of your product. It is a flag.

If your designer shows you a logo with a stock globe, a gradient swoosh, or a handshake, send it back. Ask for a mark that still reads when it is the size of a ₦100 coin. If it does not, it is not a logo. It is a picture.

## Mistake Three: Copying Silicon Valley

The third mistake is subtler, and it comes from admiration. A founder watches a video about a startup in California, sees words like "disrupt," "seamless," "ecosystem" and "revolutionise," and copies the language, the layout and the attitude. The result is a Lagos fintech that sounds like it was written by a man in San Francisco who has never queued at a bank in Ikeja.

Silicon Valley branding works in Silicon Valley because the audience shares the references. In Nigeria, your customer is a shop owner in Onitsha who wants to know whether her money will arrive today. She does not care about your ecosystem. She cares about the sentence "your money arrives in minutes," in plain English.

The Nigerian startups that got this right did not copy the Valley's words. They copied its discipline and kept their own voice. Paystack's writing is plain and specific: what it does, what it costs, who uses it. PiggyVest explains savings the way a trusted older sister would, not the way a venture capital deck would. Kuda jokes, but it jokes like a Lagos friend, not like a California brand account.

Copying Silicon Valley also shows up in the pictures. The hero image of a white team laughing around a laptop in a loft has nothing to do with your customer, and your customer knows it. The startups that look credible use real Nigerian people, real Nigerian shops, real Nigerian phones. Moniepoint's advertising shows agents and traders, because those are the people who use Moniepoint.

## Mistake Four: A Different Identity Everywhere

The last mistake is inconsistency, and it is the one that kills trust fastest. The logo is blue on the website, purple on Instagram, and green on the pitch deck because three different people made three different versions. The Twitter bio says one thing, the LinkedIn page says another, and the app has a third name entirely because the domain they wanted was taken.

An investor who sees that does not think "early-stage startup." An investor thinks "this team cannot keep a promise for three weeks." A customer thinks the same thing, in simpler terms: if they cannot keep their own colours straight, can they keep my money straight?

The fix is boring and it costs nothing. One document, one page, with the logo files, the colour codes, the two fonts, and three sentences about how the brand talks. Every designer they hire works from the same page. Your startup needs the same page, even if the team is three people in a flat in Lekki.

## Building Yours: A Short, Honest Checklist

If you are a tech founder and your identity needs work, start here.

First, pick one colour you want to be remembered by, and test it: put it on a white background with your name next to it, shrink it to phone size, and ask five strangers what kind of company it looks like. If they say "bank," "betting" or "I don't know," pick again.

Second, get a logo that survives being tiny. One mark, one wordmark, one colour version, one white version.

Third, rewrite your homepage in plain English. One headline that says what you do. One line that says who it is for. One button that says what happens next. Delete every word you would not say to a customer standing in front of you.

Fourth, replace the stock photos with real ones. Your team, your customers, your product on a real Nigerian phone. One honest photo beats ten perfect strangers shaking hands.

Fifth, write the one-page brand sheet and guard it. Logo, colours with their codes, two fonts, three voice rules. Everyone who touches the brand reads it first.

A tech identity is not decoration. It is the first proof you offer a stranger that you are serious, that you will still be here in a year, and that their money is safe with you. The Nigerian startups that earned that trust did not spend the most money on design. They made the fewest mistakes, and they made them early, when the mistakes were cheap.
