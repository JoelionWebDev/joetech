If you sell clothes, food, skincare, or any physical product in Nigeria, your supplier is one of the most important people in your business. The price you pay for materials or stock decides how much profit you make on every sale. Pay too much, and you work hard for almost nothing. Pay a fair price, and your business has room to grow.

Many small business owners in Nigeria accept the first price a supplier gives them, because they are afraid to negotiate, or because they do not know how. Others negotiate aggressively, push the price too low, and lose a good supplier. There is a better way: negotiate calmly, with facts, and build a relationship where both sides benefit.

This guide shows you how to find good suppliers, prepare before you negotiate, ask for a better price without offending anyone, and keep the relationship strong for years.

## Step 1: Know Your Numbers Before You Talk

You cannot negotiate well if you do not know your own costs. Before you speak to any supplier, work out three numbers.

First, what is your current cost per unit? If you buy fabric at 8,000 naira per yard, or chicken at 3,500 naira per kilo, write it down. Second, what do you sell the finished product for, and how much profit do you make per unit after all costs? Third, what price do you need from the supplier to make the profit you want?

For example, if you sell a dress for 25,000 naira and your total cost is 18,000 naira, your profit is 7,000 naira. If your supplier raises the fabric price by 2,000 naira, your profit falls to 5,000 naira. Knowing that number helps you decide how much you can accept, and where you must push back.

Also know the market price. Check what the same product costs from two or three other suppliers, in markets like Balogun, Mile 12, Oyingbo, or Onitsha Main Market, or from online wholesalers. You do not need to tell a supplier you are checking prices, but you need to know whether the price you are paying is fair. A supplier who charges 30 percent more than everyone else is either selling better quality, or taking advantage of you.

Write your target price down before you negotiate, and also write your walk-away price — the highest price you can accept and still make a profit. If a supplier will not meet that price, you need another supplier, not a bad deal.

## Step 2: Find and Compare Reliable Suppliers

A good price from an unreliable supplier is a bad deal. A supplier who delivers late, sends the wrong goods, or disappears after you pay can cost you far more than a slightly higher price from a reliable one.

Start by asking other business owners for recommendations. A tailor in Surulere, a baker in Lekki, or a skincare seller in Abuja will usually tell you who their best supplier is, especially if you are not a direct competitor. Business associations, WhatsApp groups for traders, and market associations are also good sources.

Visit suppliers in person when you can. Go to the market, see the goods, check the quality, and talk to the seller. Ask how long they have been in business, where they source their goods, and whether they can supply the quantity you need regularly. A supplier with a proper shop, a clear address, and regular customers is usually safer than a stranger who only chats on WhatsApp.

Compare at least three suppliers on four things: price, quality, reliability, and payment terms. The cheapest supplier is not always the best. A supplier who charges a little more but delivers on time, gives you consistent quality, and allows you to pay a deposit instead of the full amount upfront may be worth more to your business.

Keep a simple supplier list in your notebook or phone: the supplier's name, phone number, location, product, price, and notes on reliability. Update it every time prices change. That list is a business asset, and it makes negotiation much easier, because you always know your options.

## Step 3: Build the Relationship Before You Need a Discount

Negotiation works best when the supplier sees you as a regular, reliable customer, not as a stranger asking for a favour.

Start by buying small and paying on time. Order a small quantity first, check the quality, and pay promptly. Then order again, and again. Suppliers remember customers who pay on time and do not cause problems. After three or four regular orders, you are no longer a stranger. You are a customer worth keeping.

Be polite and respectful. Greet your supplier by name, ask about their family or their business, and thank them when they help you. In Nigerian markets, relationships matter as much as money. A supplier who likes you will tell you when prices are about to rise, hold stock for you, or give you a better price without you asking.

Be honest about your business. Tell your supplier what you sell, how much you buy each month, and how you plan to grow. A supplier who sees that you are growing will want to keep you, because your orders will grow too. Share your plans: "I buy 50 pieces a month now, but I plan to buy 200 a month by December." That gives the supplier a reason to offer you a better price now, in exchange for more business later.

## Step 4: Prepare Your Negotiation

Good negotiation happens before you open your mouth. Prepare like this.

First, know exactly what you want: a lower price per unit, free delivery, a longer payment period, or a discount for bulk buying. Pick one main goal, not five. Asking for everything at once makes you look unreasonable.

Second, gather your facts. How much do you buy each month? How long have you been buying from this supplier? What do competitors charge for the same product? What is your target price, and why is it fair? Facts are stronger than feelings. "I buy 100 pieces a month, and the market price is 5,000 naira, so I would like us to agree on 5,200 naira" is a negotiation. "Your price is too high" is a complaint.

Third, decide what you can offer in return. Negotiation is not just taking; it is trading. You can offer to buy in larger quantities, to pay in cash, to pay faster, to order regularly, or to commit to buying from them for the next three months. A supplier is far more likely to lower a price when you offer something valuable in return.

Fourth, choose the right time and place. Negotiate face to face when you can, at a quiet time, not when the market is crowded and the supplier is busy with other customers. If you must negotiate on the phone or WhatsApp, keep your message clear and polite, and give the supplier time to think.

## Step 5: Ask for a Better Price the Right Way

Many Nigerians are good at haggling in the market, but negotiating with a regular supplier is different. You are not trying to win a one-time bargain. You are trying to agree a price you can both live with for months.

Start by appreciating the supplier. "Thank you for the good quality you have been supplying. I want to keep buying from you, and I want us to agree a price that works for both of us." That opening sets a friendly tone.

Then state your request clearly, with a reason. "I am now buying 100 pieces a month, and I plan to increase to 200. At that volume, could we agree on 5,000 naira per piece instead of 5,500? That would help me keep my prices stable for my customers."

Listen to the supplier's reply. They may explain why the price is what it is — transport costs, dollar rates, or a rise in the cost of raw materials. Listen fully before you respond. Sometimes their reason is genuine, and you will need to find a different way to save money, like buying a larger quantity less often, or choosing a slightly different product.

If they say no, do not argue or threaten to leave immediately. Ask what they can do: "What quantity would I need to buy to get a better price?" or "Is there a different product of similar quality at a lower price?" Often there is a middle ground: a smaller discount now, with a bigger discount when your orders grow.

Use silence. After you make your request, wait. Many people feel uncomfortable with silence and fill it by accepting a worse deal or by talking too much. State your price, then wait for the supplier to respond.

And never lie. Do not claim another supplier offered you a price they did not, or that you will buy a quantity you cannot. Lies damage trust, and in a small market, the truth usually comes out.

## Step 6: Negotiate More Than Just Price

Price is important, but it is not the only thing you can negotiate. Sometimes a supplier cannot lower the price, but they can give you something else that saves you money.

You can negotiate payment terms. Instead of paying the full amount upfront, ask to pay a deposit and the balance on delivery, or to pay within seven days of delivery. That helps your cash flow, especially for large orders.

You can negotiate delivery. Ask for free delivery, or delivery to your shop instead of the market, or a discount on transport if you buy in bulk. Delivery costs add up, and free delivery is worth real money.

You can negotiate quality and consistency. Ask the supplier to guarantee the same quality every time, to replace damaged goods, or to let you inspect goods before you pay the balance. A guarantee against faulty goods is worth more than a small discount.

You can negotiate exclusivity or priority. If you buy a large quantity regularly, ask the supplier to hold stock for you during busy seasons, or to tell you first when new stock arrives. During Christmas or Sallah season, that priority can be the difference between having stock and having an empty shop.

You can also negotiate packaging or extras. A supplier who cannot lower the price may include free packaging, a few extra pieces, or a sample of a new product. Those extras have value, and they cost the supplier less than a price cut.

## Step 7: Buy in Bulk Wisely

Bulk buying is one of the easiest ways to get a better price, but only if you do it wisely. Buying a large quantity at a low price is good. Buying a large quantity of something that does not sell, or that goes bad, is a loss.

Before you buy in bulk, check three things. First, will the product sell before it expires or goes out of fashion? Food, cosmetics, and fashion items all have a shelf life or a season. Second, do you have space to store it properly? A large quantity stored in a damp room will spoil. Third, can you afford it without borrowing or emptying your business account? A bulk deal that leaves you with no cash for other costs is a bad deal.

Consider buying together with other small businesses. If you and two other sellers each need 50 pieces, you can order 150 pieces together and share the bulk discount. This works well for fabric, packaging, and ingredients. Agree clearly in writing who pays what, who collects the goods, and how they will be shared.

Keep records of bulk purchases: the date, the quantity, the price per unit, and how long it took to sell. That record tells you whether bulk buying actually saved you money, and how much to buy next time.

## Step 8: Pay Safely and Keep Good Records

Money and trust go together. Paying a supplier safely protects both you and your business.

For a new supplier, start small and pay in person where possible, so you can see the goods before you pay. For larger orders, pay a deposit first and the balance on delivery, after you have checked the goods. Avoid paying the full amount upfront to a supplier you do not know well, especially for goods that will be delivered later.

Use bank transfers, not cash, for large payments, so you have a record. Keep every receipt, transfer confirmation, and invoice in one place — a file on your phone or a folder at home. If there is a dispute, those records are your proof.

Keep a simple supplier record book: the date, the supplier, the product, the quantity, the price, the amount paid, and the delivery date. At the end of each month, review it. Which supplier gave you the best price? Who delivered late? Who gave you faulty goods? That review helps you decide who to keep, who to negotiate with, and who to replace.

## Step 9: Handle Price Increases Calmly

Prices in Nigeria rise often, because of fuel costs, exchange rates, and transport. A supplier who raises prices is not always trying to cheat you. Sometimes their own costs have risen, and they have no choice.

When a supplier raises a price, ask why, politely. "I noticed the price has gone up from 5,000 to 6,000 naira. Can you help me understand what changed?" Listen to the answer. If the reason is genuine — for example, the dollar rate or a transport increase — you may need to accept part of the increase, and adjust your own selling price.

If the increase is too large, negotiate in steps. Ask if you can keep the old price for one more order, or agree a smaller increase now and review it again next month. Offer to buy a larger quantity in exchange for holding the price, or ask for a different product at the old price.

If the price rise makes your business unprofitable, you have three choices: raise your own selling price, find a cheaper supplier, or change your product. Raising your selling price is often the right move, especially if your customers value quality. Explain the increase honestly to your customers, and keep your quality the same.

Never accept a price increase without checking the market first. A quick check with two other suppliers will tell you whether the increase is general, or whether your supplier is charging you more than others.

## A Real Example: A Fashion Seller Cuts Her Costs

A woman in Lagos sells ready-to-wear dresses. She was buying fabric from a supplier at 9,000 naira per yard, and her profit per dress was small. She visited three other fabric sellers in Balogun and found the same quality at 7,500 naira per yard.

She did not leave her supplier immediately. She went back to him, thanked him for his service, and said: "I buy 80 yards a month from you, and I plan to increase to 120 yards. I have seen the same fabric at 7,500 naira elsewhere, but I would rather stay with you. Can we agree on 7,800 naira per yard if I commit to 120 yards a month and pay in cash?"

The supplier agreed. She saved 1,200 naira per yard, which was 96,000 naira a month on 80 yards, and her profit per dress rose immediately. She kept a good supplier, and the supplier kept a growing customer. That is what good negotiation looks like.

## Your Negotiation Checklist

Before you negotiate, know your cost, your selling price, your target price, and your walk-away price. Compare at least three suppliers on price, quality, and reliability. Build the relationship by buying regularly and paying on time. Prepare one clear request and something valuable to offer in return. Ask politely, with facts and a reason, and listen to the supplier's answer. Negotiate payment terms, delivery, and quality as well as price. Buy in bulk only when the product will sell, you can store it, and you can afford it. Pay safely, keep records, and review your suppliers every month.

Your supplier is not your enemy. They are a partner in your business. Negotiate with respect, keep your promises, pay on time, and the best suppliers will want to keep you as a customer — and they will give you the prices that help your business grow.
