Tailoring is one of the oldest and most respected trades in Nigeria. Every week, people bring fabrics to a tailor for a wedding, a church programme, a naming ceremony, an office outfit, or a simple everyday dress. A good tailor is never short of work, because clothes must fit the person wearing them, and a well-sewn outfit can make an ordinary fabric look expensive.

Yet many talented tailors struggle to make a steady income. Some sew beautifully but charge too little. Some take too many jobs and deliver late. Some have no shop system, no records, and no way for new customers to find them. This guide is for anyone who wants to start a tailoring business properly, as a real business, not just a hobby. It covers learning the skill, choosing a niche, setting up your shop, pricing your work, and building a customer base that returns.

## Why Tailoring Still Works in Nigeria

Nigerians wear custom-made clothes for many occasions. Native wear, Ankara styles, lace outfits, corporate suits, and party dresses are often sewn to order. Even as ready-to-wear fashion grows, many customers still prefer a tailor who can sew a style exactly as they want it, in their size, with their fabric.

Tailoring also has a low starting cost compared with many businesses. If you already know how to sew, you can start from home with a machine and a few tools. You do not need a large shop on day one. You need skill, a machine that works well, and customers who trust you to deliver on time.

The trade rewards skill and reputation. A tailor who delivers on time, sews neatly, and treats customers well will get referrals every week. A tailor who disappoints customers will lose work, even if their sewing is good. In this business, your name is your brand.

## Step 1: Learn to Sew Well

Do not start a tailoring business with weak skills. Customers pay for fit, finishing, and style. If a dress is too tight, a trouser is too long, or a zip is badly fixed, the customer will not return, and they will tell others.

Learn through apprenticeship if you are new to sewing. Find a good tailor in your area and learn under them for six months to one year or more. You will learn how to take measurements, cut patterns, sew different styles, fix zips and linings, and press clothes properly. You will also learn how the master talks to customers, prices jobs, and manages deadlines.

If you already sew, improve your skills with a short course. Many fashion schools in Lagos, Abuja, and other cities teach pattern drafting, freehand cutting, and advanced styles such as suits, wedding gowns, and corporate wear. Learning a new skill, such as making suits or bridal wear, can raise your prices and attract higher-paying customers.

Practise on your own clothes and on clothes for family and friends. Sew different styles: a simple dress, a shirt, a skirt, a trouser, a native top, and a two-piece set. Check your work honestly. Are the seams straight? Is the hem even? Does the outfit fit well when worn? Ask the person wearing it for honest feedback. The more you practise, the faster and neater you become, and speed matters when you have many orders.

Learn to use a sewing machine well. Know how to thread it, change the needle, adjust the tension, and fix small problems. A tailor who cannot fix a simple machine fault will lose working days waiting for a repairer. Keep your machine clean, oiled, and covered when not in use.

## Step 2: Choose Your Niche

Tailoring covers many types of clothes. Trying to sew everything at the start can spread you too thin. Choose a niche that matches your skill and your market. Common niches include women's fashion, men's native wear, corporate suits, children's wear, wedding and bridal wear, Ankara styles, and uniform sewing for schools and organisations.

Think about your area. In a busy market area, everyday wear and Ankara styles may sell well. In an office area, corporate wear and suits may pay better. In a residential estate, women's and children's wear may bring steady orders. Visit other tailors in your area and notice what they sew most. That tells you where the demand is.

Your niche also affects your prices. Bridal wear and suits take more time and skill, so they command higher prices. Simple everyday clothes are faster to sew but pay less per piece. You can start with everyday styles to build volume, then add higher-value styles as your skill grows.

Do not be afraid to specialise. A tailor known as the best suit maker in the area, or the best for wedding dresses, will attract customers from far away. A tailor who sews everything averagely may struggle to stand out. Pick one area to be known for, and do it excellently.

## Step 3: Plan Your Startup Costs

Write down what you need before you spend money. Your main costs are machines, tools, shop rent, and working capital for the first few months.

A good industrial sewing machine is your most important tool. Prices vary, but a new industrial machine can cost from ₦250,000 upwards, while a fairly used one costs less. An overlock machine, which neats the edges of fabric, is also important for professional finishing. A good pressing iron, a large cutting table, scissors, measuring tapes, chalk, pins, and a mirror for fittings are other essentials. Budget between ₦400,000 and ₦800,000 for machines and tools if you are starting from scratch.

Shop rent depends on location. In Lagos, a small tailor shop in a busy area can cost ₦250,000 to ₦600,000 per year. In smaller towns, rent may be ₦100,000 to ₦250,000 per year. Many landlords ask for one year upfront. If rent is too high, start from home and save towards a shop. Many successful tailors started at home and moved to a shop after they had steady customers.

Add money for a signboard, a work table, chairs for customers, a changing area, and storage for fabrics. Keep some cash aside for transport, phone data, and small items such as thread, zips, buttons, and lining, which you will need to buy regularly. A realistic total for a small starter shop is between ₦700,000 and ₦1,500,000, depending on your location and whether you already own a machine. If you already have a machine and can work from home, you can start with far less.

Keep a record of every cost. Use a notebook or a simple app on your phone. Knowing your true costs helps you price your work correctly and avoid spending money you need for the business.

## Step 4: Set Up Your Shop for Work and Customers

Your shop should be clean, bright, and organised. Customers judge your work by your shop. A tidy shop with a good mirror, a changing area, and neatly stored fabrics looks professional. A messy shop with fabrics on the floor and thread everywhere makes customers doubt your finishing.

Arrange your space into work and customer areas. Your machine, cutting table, and iron should be in the work area, with good light. Your customer area should have chairs, a mirror, and space to display samples of your work. Display photos of outfits you have sewn, with the customer's permission. A small album of your best work helps new customers trust you.

Power is important. Sewing machines need electricity, and pressing needs a good iron. If your area has poor power supply, budget for a generator or a solar setup. Tell customers honestly if a delay is caused by power, and plan your work around the hours when light is available.

Keep your shop open at regular hours and display them clearly. Customers need to know when to bring fabric, when to come for fitting, and when to collect their clothes. If you close without notice, customers will go to another tailor.

## Step 5: Price Your Work Fairly

Pricing is one of the hardest parts of tailoring. Many tailors undercharge because they fear losing customers, then end up working long hours for little profit. Price your work based on time, skill, materials, and the complexity of the style.

Work out your sewing charge for common styles. A simple dress, a shirt, a skirt, a trouser, a native top, and a two-piece set should each have a clear price. A complex style with lining, beading, or a difficult cut should cost more than a simple style. A suit should cost more than a shirt. Write your price list and display it in your shop. A clear price list saves time and prevents arguments.

Always agree on the price before you start work. Tell the customer the sewing charge, what it includes, and what it does not include, such as lining, zip, or buttons if the customer must provide them. Collect a deposit before you cut the fabric. A deposit of half the sewing charge is common in Nigeria. It protects you if the customer does not return, and it helps you buy materials you need for the job.

Do not start work without a deposit for a new customer. Many tailors have sewn clothes that were never collected. A deposit shows the customer is serious. Write a receipt for the deposit, with the customer's name, phone number, style, fabric details, agreed price, deposit paid, and collection date. Give one copy to the customer and keep one for yourself.

Charge for alterations separately. Shortening a trouser, adjusting a waist, or fixing a zip is work, and it deserves payment. Many customers expect free alterations, but a clear price for common alterations helps you earn fairly for your time.

## Step 6: Manage Orders, Fittings, and Deadlines

Good tailors are organised tailors. Keep a job book for every order. Write the customer's name, phone number, measurements, style description, fabric details, agreed price, deposit, and collection date. Take a photo of the style the customer wants, with their permission, and save it with their name. This prevents confusion later.

Take measurements carefully. Measure bust, waist, hips, shoulder, sleeve length, and full length, and write them down clearly. Do not rely on memory. Ask the customer to wear the type of underwear and shoes they will wear with the outfit, so the fit is correct. If the customer is unsure of a measurement, measure twice.

Schedule fittings where needed. For a suit, a wedding dress, or a complex style, a fitting halfway through the work can catch problems early. Tell the customer when to come for fitting and keep to that time. If you notice a problem during fitting, fix it before the final pressing.

Deliver on time. This is the single most important habit in tailoring. If you promise Saturday, deliver on Saturday. If you cannot meet a date, tell the customer early and agree on a new date. Do not wait until the collection day to say the clothes are not ready. Late delivery is the most common complaint against tailors in Nigeria, and it loses more customers than bad sewing.

Do not take more orders than you can finish. It is better to turn away an order politely than to take it and disappoint the customer. If you are busy before a wedding season, tell new customers your next available date. They will respect your honesty and may wait for you.

## Step 7: Find Customers and Build Your Reputation

Your first customers are people who already know you. Tell your neighbours, church members, colleagues, and family that you sew. Sew for them at a fair price and ask them to recommend you. A happy customer wearing your outfit to an event is your best advert.

Use WhatsApp and Instagram well. Create a WhatsApp Business account with your shop name, your services, your location, and your opening hours. Post photos of outfits you have sewn, with the customer's permission. Show the fabric, the style, and the finished outfit. Use Instagram to reach new customers, and add your WhatsApp link so they can order easily. Reply quickly. A customer who messages three tailors will choose the one who replies first and politely.

Ask for referrals. After a customer collects a good outfit, ask them to tell a friend. Offer a small discount on their next order if they bring a new customer. Keep a record of your best customers and greet them by name when they return. Personal attention builds loyalty.

Work with fabric sellers and event planners. A fabric seller in the market can recommend you to customers who buy fabric and need a tailor. An event planner can recommend you for wedding and party outfits. Build those relationships by being reliable and by thanking people who send customers to you.

Register your business when you have steady sales. Register your business name with the Corporate Affairs Commission, open a business bank account, and use it for payments. A registered name and a business account make you look serious and help you work with schools, churches, and organisations that need uniforms or bulk sewing.

## Step 8: Keep Records, Manage Money, and Grow

Keep simple records every day. Write down each order, the amount charged, the deposit received, and the balance paid on collection. Write down your expenses: thread, zips, buttons, lining, transport, rent, and power. At the end of each week, add up your income and expenses. That will show you whether you are making a profit.

Pay yourself a fixed amount each week, like a salary. Do not spend all the money that comes in. Keep money aside for rent, machine repairs, and slow periods. A machine breakdown in a busy week can stop your work if you have no savings.

Plan for busy seasons. Demand rises before Christmas, Easter, weddings, and school resumption. During those periods, tailors work long hours and customers book early. Prepare by buying thread and other supplies ahead of time, servicing your machine, and setting clear booking dates. Do not take last-minute orders you cannot finish well.

Avoid common mistakes. Do not cut a customer's fabric without clear measurements and a deposit. Do not promise a style you cannot sew. Do not keep a customer's clothes for weeks after they are ready without telling them. Do not spend your deposit money before you buy the materials for the job. Each of these mistakes damages trust.

As you grow, you can train an apprentice, hire a second tailor, or add ready-to-wear pieces to your shop. You can also offer training classes for people who want to learn sewing. Teaching adds income and builds your name as an expert. You can open a second shop in another area once your first shop runs well. Grow slowly, keep your quality steady, and let your reputation do the selling.

## Final Checklist Before You Start

Before you start, check that you can sew the styles you plan to offer to a good standard, that you have a working machine and the basic tools, that you have chosen a niche and a price list, that you have a job book and receipts, that you have a WhatsApp Business account with photos of your work, and that you have a plan for fittings and delivery dates.

Start with the customers around you, sew neatly, deliver on time, and keep good records. Tailoring rewards patience, skill, and honesty. Your first months may be slow, but every well-sewn outfit is an advert for the next customer. Keep learning new styles, keep your shop tidy, and keep your promises. Over time, your small tailoring shop can become a trusted name in your area and a steady source of income.
