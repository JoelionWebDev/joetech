Mrs Adeyemi walked into a boutique in Ikeja with a dress in a nylon bag and fire in her eyes. She had paid ₦48,000 for the dress two days earlier, for her daughter's wedding on Saturday, and the zip had split the first time she tried it on. The sales girl behind the counter took one look at her face and said the worst possible thing: "Madam, are you sure you did not force it?"

That one sentence cost the shop a ₦48,000 refund, a customer who had been buying there for three years, and at least four of her friends who heard the story at the wedding. The dress was faulty. Everybody in the shop knew it. But the words came out wrong, and the words were what Mrs Adeyemi remembered.

In the moment, you need actual words, not advice to stay calm. This article gives you the exact words to say when a customer complains to your face or on the phone, from the first thirty seconds to the last handshake, with three full conversations you can learn from.

## The First Thirty Seconds Decide Everything

A complaint is not a debate. The customer is not waiting for your side of the story. In the first thirty seconds, they are checking one thing: does this person care that I am unhappy, or am I about to fight for my own money?

Say their name if you know it. Stand up if you are sitting down. Put your phone face down. Look at them, not at the product in their hand. And say something that names what happened and takes it seriously. "Good afternoon, Mrs Adeyemi. I am sorry the dress tore. That should never have happened with a new dress, and I understand why you are upset." What you must not say in those thirty seconds is an explanation. Do not say the supplier sent a bad batch. Do not say the tailor was in a hurry. Do not say other customers bought the same dress without any problem. Every explanation sounds like an excuse while the customer is still angry, even when it is true. There will be time for explanations later, once the customer has calmed down and the problem is being fixed. In the first thirty seconds, explanations are poison.

## When the Customer Is Shouting

Sooner or later, a customer will shout at you. Maybe they paid ₦120,000 for a phone accessory that stopped working in a week. Maybe a dispatch rider kept their food for three hours and it arrived cold. Maybe they have called three times already and nobody called back. The shouting is not really about you. It is about the money, the embarrassment, and the feeling of being ignored. Knowing that does not make it pleasant. It does make it survivable.

Lower your own voice. Not to a whisper, which sounds sarcastic, but a notch below normal. People match the volume they hear. If you raise your voice to meet theirs, you now have two people shouting in a shop in Lekki while other customers edge towards the door. If you drop yours, the shouting customer has to work harder to keep shouting, and most of them cannot keep it up for long.

Let them finish a full sentence without interrupting. This is harder than it sounds. When someone is saying things that are unfair, your hand itches to correct them. Do not. Wait for a pause. Then use a sentence that shows you heard them without agreeing to anything false. "I hear you. You paid ₦120,000, the item stopped working after one week, and nobody called you back after your first message. That is not acceptable, and I am sorry." Repeating their facts back proves you were listening. Customers shout louder when they think nobody is listening.

## The Apology, and What to Offer After It

"Sorry for any inconvenience" is not an apology. It is a sentence a company hides behind. Customers hear it and get angrier, because it says nothing about their problem. A real apology has a name, a problem, and an effect. "Mrs Adeyemi, I am sorry the zip split on your dress. You bought it for your daughter's wedding on Saturday, and now you are worried about what to wear. That is our fault, and I understand the stress." One honest apology, said slowly and meant, beats ten rushed ones. And once you have apologised, do not undo it with a "but". "I am sorry the food arrived cold, but traffic in Lekki was terrible" tells the customer that traffic matters more than their cold food. Keep the apology clean.

Compensation is where many small business owners freeze. They worry that giving anything will teach customers to complain for free things. The fear is understandable. The maths usually says otherwise. Take a customer who buys a ₦25,000 hair product from you every six weeks. That is roughly ₦200,000 a year. If the product irritates her scalp and you refund the ₦25,000, you have spent ₦25,000 to keep ₦200,000 a year. If you argue and lose her, you have saved ₦25,000 and lost ₦200,000 a year, plus whatever her sister and her friends would have spent.

Match the offer to the size of the problem: a wrong size gets an exchange and free delivery, a late meal gets a replacement or a refund, and a ₦120,000 item that died in a week gets a replacement or a full refund, not a 10 percent discount. Say the offer as a clear promise with a time attached. "I will send the correct size tomorrow morning by 11am, and the rider will collect the wrong one at the same time. You will not pay for delivery." Compare that with "We will sort it out." The first sentence lets the customer relax. The second leaves them wondering if anything will happen at all. ## Conversation One: The Wrong Order

Chiamaka runs a small food business in Surulere. She sells lunch packs for ₦6,500. A regular customer, Mr Bankole, ordered ten packs for his office, ₦65,000 in total, and asked for five chicken and five fish. The rider delivered ten fish. Mr Bankole called, furious, because two of his colleagues do not eat fish, and the meeting was starting in twenty minutes.

The wrong version: "Sir, maybe you did not specify. We always ask customers to confirm." Mr Bankole heard blame. He shouted louder. He told his office never to order from Chiamaka again. The ₦65,000 order was the last one.

The right version: "Good afternoon, Mr Bankole. I am sorry we sent ten fish instead of five chicken and five fish. That was our mistake, and I know your meeting is starting now. I cannot fix the five chicken packs in twenty minutes, and I will not pretend I can. Here is what I can do. I am refunding ₦32,500 for the five wrong packs to your account right now, and your next order of ten packs is free. The refund will show in your account today. I am sending the receipt to your WhatsApp now."

She named the mistake, admitted what she could not fix, and acted while he was still on the phone. Mr Bankole ordered again the next week.



## Conversation Two: The Late Delivery

Emeka sells sneakers in Abuja. A customer, Fatima, ordered a pair of trainers for ₦85,000 on Monday, for her brother's birthday on Friday. She paid in full. By Thursday evening, the shoes had not arrived. She called Emeka, and she was not shouting. She was quiet, which is worse. Quiet means a customer has already decided to leave.

The wrong version: "Madam, the courier said they will deliver tomorrow." Fatima heard a shrug. She asked for a refund. Emeka argued, because the shoes were already in transit. She went to her bank and raised a dispute. Emeka lost the ₦85,000 and the shoes.

The right version: "Fatima, good evening. I am sorry the trainers have not arrived, and I know your brother's birthday is tomorrow. You paid ₦85,000 on Monday because you trusted us to deliver by Friday, and we have let you down. I have just called the courier. The parcel is in their Kaduna hub, and they cannot promise it before Saturday. I will not ask you to wait. I am refunding your full ₦85,000 tonight, and I will also send you a ₦10,000 voucher for your next order, because I want a second chance. If the shoes still arrive tomorrow, keep them. They are yours."

The refund cost less than a bank dispute and a bad review. Fatima got her money that night, used the voucher a month later, and brought her brother, who bought two pairs.



## Conversation Three: The Faulty Product

Adaeze owns a beauty store in Lekki Phase 1. A customer, Ngozi, bought a hair dryer for ₦42,000. Three days later it stopped heating. Ngozi came to the shop with the dryer and the receipt, and she was angry, but she was also reasonable. She said, "I just want one that works."

The wrong version: "Madam, you should have tested it here. We do not refund electronics after three days." Ngozi asked for the manager. There was no manager. She left the dryer on the counter and walked out. She told the story in her estate WhatsApp group that night. Three women in the group said they would not shop there again.

The right version: "Good afternoon, Ngozi. I am sorry the dryer stopped heating after three days. A new dryer should last years, not days. Let me test it here so you can see what I am checking." Adaeze plugged it in. It blew cold air only. "You are right, the heating element has failed. I have the same model in stock. I will give you a new one now, and I will test it here with you before you leave, so you go home with one that works. I will also add a free heat protectant spray worth ₦8,000, because you have had to come back to the shop for something that was not your fault."

Ngozi told her estate group the good version of the story, and it brought two new customers in the following week.



## After the Fix: The Two Minutes That Keep the Customer

The conversation does not end when the refund is sent or the replacement is handed over. It ends two days later, with a short message or call. "Good morning, Mrs Adeyemi. I hope the replacement dress fits well. Is there anything else I can do for you?" That is the whole message. It takes thirty seconds to send, and it is the part customers remember most, because almost no business does it.

Complaints will keep coming. Every business that sells anything gets them. The shops that last are not the ones that never make a mistake. They are the ones whose customers can describe, word for word, how well they were treated when something went wrong. Learn the words. Say them slowly. Mean them. Then fix the problem and follow up. That is the whole job, and it is worth more than any advert you will ever buy.
