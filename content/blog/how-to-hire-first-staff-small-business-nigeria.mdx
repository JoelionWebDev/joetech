Hiring your first staff member is one of the biggest steps a Nigerian small business can take. Until now, you have done everything yourself: you take the orders, you reply to customers on WhatsApp, you pack the products, you deliver them, and you keep the records. It worked when you had five orders a week. Now you have twenty orders a day, customers are waiting hours for a reply, and you are working from morning until midnight.

That is the moment to hire. Not because hiring is easy, but because doing everything yourself has become the thing holding your business back. The right first hire frees up your time, improves your customer service, and lets you focus on growing the business instead of just surviving each day.

This guide walks you through hiring your first staff member in Nigeria, step by step. You will learn how to know when you are ready, what role to hire for first, what to pay, where to find good people, how to interview them, and how to manage them in the first month without the common mistakes that make first hires fail.

## How to Know You Are Ready to Hire

Many business owners hire too early, and many hire too late. Hiring too early means you pay a salary your business cannot afford. Hiring too late means you burn out, lose customers, and miss orders you could have taken.

You are probably ready to hire when three signs appear together. First, you regularly turn down work because you do not have time. A tailor in Surulere who turns away three customers a week because she cannot finish their clothes on time is losing money every week. A food vendor in Abuja who stops taking orders at noon because the kitchen cannot cope is losing money every day.

Second, you spend most of your day on tasks someone else could do. If you spend four hours a day replying to WhatsApp messages and packing orders, those are tasks a shop assistant or a sales assistant could handle. Your time is better spent finding new customers, improving your products, and planning the next step for the business.

Third, your income is steady enough to pay a salary for at least three months, even in a slow month. Do not hire on the strength of one good week. Look at your last three months. If your profit after all expenses is consistently enough to cover a salary of, say, ₦70,000 to ₦120,000 a month, plus a small buffer, you can afford to hire. If your profit is not yet steady, fix your pricing or your sales first, and hire when the numbers are ready.

Ask yourself a simple question: if I hire someone for ₦80,000 a month, will they help me earn at least ₦80,000 extra a month, either by taking more orders or by freeing me to sell more? If the answer is yes, hiring is an investment, not a cost. If the answer is no, you may need a different role, or you may need to wait.

## What Role Should You Hire First?

The first hire should remove your biggest bottleneck. Do not hire a manager first. Hire someone who does the work that takes most of your time.

For most Nigerian small businesses, the first hire is one of these roles. A sales assistant or shop assistant, who attends to customers, takes orders, and handles payments. A social media and customer service assistant, who replies to WhatsApp and Instagram messages, posts products, and follows up with customers. A production assistant, such as a tailor's assistant, a kitchen assistant, or a dispatch assistant, who helps you make or deliver the product faster. A cleaner and general assistant, who keeps the shop tidy, runs errands, and helps wherever needed.

Choose the role that matches your pain. If customers complain that you reply late, hire for customer service. If orders pile up because you cannot produce fast enough, hire for production. If your shop is busy and customers walk out because nobody attends to them, hire a shop assistant.

Write a simple job description before you start looking. It does not need to be formal. It needs to answer four questions: what will this person do every day, what skills do they need, what hours will they work, and what will you pay them? For example: "Shop assistant for a fashion store in Lekki Phase 1. Duties: attend to customers, take measurements, record sales, keep the shop tidy. Hours: Monday to Saturday, 9am to 6pm. Salary: ₦90,000 per month. Must be friendly, honest, and able to use WhatsApp."

A clear job description saves you time. It stops people who are not suitable from applying, and it gives suitable people a clear picture of the job. It also protects you later, because you and your staff member agreed on the duties and the hours from the start.

## What to Pay Your First Employee in Nigeria

Pay is the question every first-time employer worries about most. Pay too little and good people leave quickly. Pay too much and your business struggles to survive.

Start by checking what similar roles pay in your area. A shop assistant in Lekki may earn ₦80,000 to ₦120,000 a month. The same role in a smaller town may pay ₦50,000 to ₦80,000. A skilled tailor's assistant or a cook may earn more than a general shop assistant, because their skill is harder to replace. A social media assistant who can design graphics and write captions may earn ₦100,000 to ₦150,000 a month in Lagos, depending on experience.

Remember that salary is not your only cost. You may also pay for transport, lunch, or a small allowance for data and airtime if the job requires it. Some employers in Lagos add a transport allowance of ₦20,000 to ₦30,000 a month on top of the basic salary, because transport costs are high. Be clear in the job advert about what is included: is the salary ₦90,000 total, or ₦90,000 plus transport? Confusion about pay is one of the fastest ways to lose a new employee.

Nigeria has a national minimum wage, and it changes from time to time. Check the current minimum wage before you set your salary, and make sure your offer meets or exceeds it. Paying below the minimum wage is not only unfair, it can also create legal problems for your business.

Be honest about what you can afford. It is better to offer ₦80,000 and pay it on time every month than to offer ₦120,000 and pay late. Staff who are paid late lose trust quickly, and trust is hard to rebuild. Pay on a fixed date each month, such as the 28th, and stick to it. If you ever need to delay payment, tell your staff member in advance, explain why, and give a firm date.

You can also add small benefits that cost little but mean a lot. A staff lunch once a week, a bonus when the business has a good month, or a small gift at Christmas can make a good employee stay. A simple "thank you" when someone does a good job costs nothing and is often missing in small businesses.

## Where to Find Good Candidates

You do not need a recruitment agency for your first hire. Most Nigerian small businesses find their first staff member through people they already know, or through simple, low-cost adverts.

Start with referrals. Ask trusted friends, family, church members, and fellow business owners if they know someone reliable who is looking for work. A referral from someone you trust is valuable, because that person has seen how the candidate behaves. Be careful, though: do not hire a relative just because they are family. Hire them only if they can do the job well and will take instructions from you. Mixing family and business without clear rules causes problems in many Nigerian businesses.

Post the job where your ideal candidate will see it. WhatsApp status and WhatsApp groups for your area work well for shop assistants and general staff. Instagram and Facebook work well for social media assistants. Job boards like Jobberman and MyJobMag work well when you want many applications and can sort through them. A simple notice in your shop window also works: "Shop assistant wanted. Apply inside." That notice reaches people who live nearby, which matters if transport is a problem.

Write the advert clearly. State the role, the location, the working hours, the salary or salary range, and how to apply. Ask applicants to send a short message on WhatsApp with their name, age, location, experience, and why they want the job. That short message tells you a lot: can they write clearly, do they follow instructions, and do they live close enough to get to work on time?

Avoid adverts that ask for too much. Your first hire does not need a university degree for most shop and assistant roles. They need honesty, reliability, a willingness to learn, and a friendly attitude to customers. Skills can be taught. Character is much harder to teach.

## How to Interview and Choose the Right Person

Shortlist three to five candidates for an interview. Interviewing twenty people wastes your time and theirs. Read each application carefully. Look for people who live close to your shop, who have some relevant experience, and who wrote a clear, polite application. Call the shortlisted candidates and invite them for a short interview at your shop.

Keep the interview simple and practical. Ask about their previous work: what did they do, why did they leave, and what did they learn? Ask how they would handle a real situation in your business: "A customer says the dress she ordered is the wrong size. What would you do?" There is no single correct answer, but the reply shows whether the person stays calm, listens, and tries to solve the problem.

Ask about reliability: can they work on Saturdays, can they get to work by 9am, and do they have any commitments that will affect their attendance? Be honest about the hours and the workload. If the job involves standing all day or working on Saturdays, say so now. A candidate who accepts the real job will stay longer than a candidate who was promised an easy job that does not exist.

Test the skill if the job needs one. Ask a tailor's assistant to sew a straight seam. Ask a social media assistant to write a short caption for a product photo. Ask a shop assistant to attend to a customer while you watch. A five-minute practical test tells you more than thirty minutes of talking.

Check references. Call a previous employer or a person the candidate names as a referee. Ask simple questions: did they come to work on time, were they honest, and would you hire them again? Many small business owners skip this step and regret it later. A short phone call can save you months of trouble.

Trust your judgement, but do not hire on feelings alone. The friendliest candidate is not always the most reliable. Look for a balance: friendly to customers, honest about their experience, punctual to the interview, and clear about what they can do. If a candidate arrives very late to the interview without calling ahead, that is useful information about how they will behave at work.

## Offer, Contract, and Trial Period

When you choose your candidate, make a clear offer. State the salary, the working hours, the start date, and any benefits, such as a transport allowance or lunch. Put the offer in writing, even if it is a simple message on WhatsApp or a one-page letter. A written offer avoids arguments later about what was agreed.

Use a simple written contract. It does not need to be long or full of legal language. It should cover the job title, the duties, the working hours, the salary and pay date, the probation period, and how either side can end the employment. Both you and the employee should sign it, and each of you should keep a copy. If you can, ask a lawyer or a knowledgeable friend to check the contract once. A clear contract protects both sides.

Include a probation period of one to three months. Probation is a trial period where both sides can see if the job is a good fit. During probation, you can end the employment with short notice if the person is not suitable, and the employee can also leave if the job is not right for them. Explain probation clearly at the start, so the employee does not feel surprised if you raise a concern in the second week.

On the first day, explain the rules clearly. What time should they arrive? What should they wear? How should they greet customers? How do they record a sale? Where do they keep the keys? Write the main rules down and give the employee a copy. Many first hires fail not because the employee is bad, but because the owner never explained what was expected.

## Training and Managing Your First Employee in the First Month

Your first employee will not know how you like things done. That is normal. Your job in the first month is to teach them, check their work, and correct mistakes kindly.

Spend the first week working closely with them. Show them how you greet customers, how you take an order, how you pack a product, and how you record a sale. Let them watch you first, then let them try while you watch, then let them work on their own while you check in regularly. Do not leave a new employee alone on the first day and expect them to know everything.

Give clear instructions, one task at a time. Instead of saying "Handle the shop today," say: "Greet every customer, record each sale in this book, and call me if a customer asks for a discount above ₦5,000." Clear instructions prevent mistakes and make the employee feel confident.

Check in every day in the first month. Ask what went well, what was difficult, and what they need from you. Praise good work quickly and specifically: "You handled that angry customer very well today. Thank you." Correct mistakes privately and quickly: "Next time, please confirm the size with the customer before you pack the dress." Public correction embarrasses staff and damages morale.

Pay on time, every time. Nothing destroys trust faster than late pay. If you promise to pay on the 28th, pay on the 28th. Keep a record of salary payments, with the date and the amount, and give the employee a simple pay slip or a message confirming the payment.

Set a simple routine for the business. A daily opening checklist, a sales record book, and a closing checklist make the business run smoothly even when you are not there. Teach your employee to use them, and check them yourself for the first few weeks. When the routine is clear, you can step away from the shop for a few hours without worrying.

## Conclusion

Hiring your first staff member is a sign that your business is growing. It is also a skill you will get better at with practice. Start by being honest about whether you are ready: steady income, work you are turning away, and tasks that someone else could do. Hire for your biggest bottleneck, write a clear job description, and pay a fair salary on time every month.

Find candidates through referrals and simple adverts, interview a small shortlist, test the skill where it matters, and check references. Make a clear written offer, use a simple contract, and include a probation period. Train your new employee closely in the first month, give clear instructions, praise good work, and correct mistakes privately.

Your first hire will not be perfect, and neither will you as a first-time employer. That is normal. What matters is that you choose carefully, explain clearly, pay fairly, and treat your employee with respect. Do that, and your first hire can become the person who helps your business grow from a one-person hustle into a real company with a team you can rely on.
