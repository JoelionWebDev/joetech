Cash on delivery is the most loved and most feared payment method in Nigerian online selling. Customers love it because they pay only when the product is in their hands. Sellers fear it because of the stories: a rider travels two hours to Lekki, the customer refuses to pick up the call, the goods come back, and the seller has paid delivery both ways for nothing.

Both sides are right. COD builds trust with customers who have been scammed before — and in Nigeria, that is a lot of people. But unmanaged COD quietly destroys profit through failed deliveries, returned goods, fake orders and missing cash.

This guide shows you how to offer cash on delivery safely: when it makes sense, what to charge upfront, how to verify orders, how to work with riders, and what to do when a delivery fails.

## Why Nigerians Prefer Cash on Delivery

To offer COD well, understand why customers ask for it. It is rarely about having no money in the bank. It is about trust.

Many Nigerians have paid for goods online and received nothing, or received something different from what they ordered. Others have paid and waited weeks for delivery. COD removes that risk from the customer and places it on the seller — which is exactly why customers prefer it.

COD also suits how many Nigerians handle money. Cash is still common for daily spending. Some customers are paid in cash. Others share a bank account with family and prefer to pay from cash in hand. And for first-time buyers, COD is a low-risk way to test a new seller before committing to prepayment next time.

The data from sellers across Lagos, Abuja and Port Harcourt is consistent: offering COD increases orders, often by 30 to 50 percent for new customers. The question is never whether COD brings sales. It is whether you can control the costs it brings with it.

## The Real Costs of Cash on Delivery

Before you offer COD, count what it actually costs you. Many sellers only count the delivery fee and miss the rest:

- **Failed delivery costs.** A rider goes out, the customer is unavailable, and the goods return. You pay the rider for a wasted trip — often ₦2,000 to ₦5,000 within Lagos, more for inter-state — and the product was out of your shop the whole time.
- **Returned and damaged goods.** Goods that travel twice are more likely to arrive scuffed, opened or damaged. Fashion items returned after being tried on cannot always be resold as new.
- **Cash handling risk.** Cash collected by a rider can be delayed, miscounted or, in the worst cases, not remitted at all. Every handover of cash is a point of risk.
- **Your time.** Confirming orders, calling customers, tracking riders and chasing failed deliveries takes hours every week. Time spent chasing COD orders is time not spent selling.
- **Fake and prank orders.** Some orders are never real — wrong numbers, people who ordered "for fun", or competitors testing your process. Without verification, you dispatch goods to ghosts.

None of this means you should refuse COD. It means you should price it, verify it and control it like any other business cost.

## Rule 1: Always Collect a Delivery Deposit

This is the single most important rule in this guide. Never send a COD order with zero commitment from the customer.

**Charge a delivery fee upfront.** Before dispatch, the customer pays the delivery fee — ₦2,000, ₦3,500 or whatever your area costs — by bank transfer. This does three things at once: it confirms the customer is serious, it confirms their bank account works, and it means a failed delivery costs them something too, so they pick up the rider's call.

**For higher-value orders, collect a part-payment.** For orders above ₦50,000, collect 20 to 30 percent upfront, with the balance paid on delivery. A customer who pays ₦30,000 upfront for a ₦100,000 order is not going to vanish when the rider calls.

**State it clearly before the order is confirmed.** "Our cash on delivery service requires the delivery fee of ₦3,000 paid upfront to confirm your order. The product price of ₦25,000 is paid to the rider on delivery." Put this in your bio, your product posts and your order confirmation message. Customers who understand the rule rarely argue with it — and those who refuse it were often the ones who would not have received the goods anyway.

**Never waive the deposit for strangers.** You can waive it for proven repeat customers who have received five orders successfully. For a first-time buyer, the deposit is non-negotiable. Your regulars earned the trust; new customers build it one confirmed order at a time.

## Rule 2: Verify Every Order Before Dispatch

Verification takes three minutes and prevents most failed deliveries. Do it for every COD order without exception:

1. **Confirm the order by call, not just chat.** A short voice call confirms the number is real, the person is real, and they actually want the product. Ask them to confirm the item, the size or colour, the price, the delivery fee and the address. If the number does not connect after two tries at different times, do not dispatch.
2. **Confirm the full address.** Ask for a landmark: "near the Chevron toll gate", "opposite the Total filling station in Ikeja", "behind Shoprite in Lekki". Vague addresses cause failed deliveries. Save the address exactly as the customer gives it, plus the landmark.
3. **Confirm availability.** "Will you be available to receive the delivery tomorrow between 10am and 2pm?" If they are travelling or at work with no one at home, agree a better day or a pickup point instead.
4. **Confirm the amount they will pay.** State the exact cash amount, including any delivery balance. "You will pay ₦25,000 to the rider on delivery." No surprises means no arguments at the door.
5. **Send a written confirmation.** After the call, send a WhatsApp message summarising everything: product, price, delivery fee paid, balance on delivery, address, and expected delivery window. Ask the customer to reply "Confirmed". That message is your record if anything is disputed later.

For inter-state orders — Lagos to Abuja, Port Harcourt or Onitsha — verification matters even more. A failed inter-state delivery can cost ₦8,000 to ₦15,000 in transport. Consider full prepayment or a larger deposit for inter-state COD, and be honest with customers about why.

## Rule 3: Use Riders and Logistics Partners You Trust

Your rider is your business in the customer's eyes. A rude rider, a late rider or a rider who cannot find the address damages your reputation as surely as a faulty product.

**Work with a small number of reliable riders.** Two or three riders you know personally beat ten random ones from an app. You learn their strengths, they learn your customers, and accountability is clear. Agree their rates in advance for your main areas — Lekki, Ikeja, Surulere, Victoria Island, Ajah — so there is no negotiation per order.

**Consider established logistics companies.** For higher volumes, companies like GIG Logistics, Kwik Delivery, Fez Delivery or Sendbox offer tracking, insurance options and structured remittance. They cost more per delivery than an independent rider, but they reduce failed deliveries and give customers tracking they can follow. Compare two or three and test with a few orders before committing.

**Give the rider everything they need.** The customer's name, phone number, address with landmark, the exact amount to collect, and your phone number. A rider who has to call you from the customer's gate because the address is incomplete is a delivery that is already going wrong.

**Insist on proof of delivery.** The rider should confirm delivery to you by message or photo, and the customer should confirm receipt on WhatsApp. For cash collected, the rider should send you a transfer or a receipt the same day — never "tomorrow" without a reason. Keep a simple log: order, rider, amount collected, date remitted. Reconcile it weekly.

**Set a remittance rule and enforce it.** Cash collected today is transferred to you today, or at most within 24 hours. Riders who hold cash for days are a risk, however friendly they are. If a rider repeatedly delays remittance, replace them. Your money is not their float.

## Rule 4: Set Clear Rules for Returns and Rejections

Customers will sometimes reject goods on delivery. The product does not match the picture. The size is wrong. They changed their mind. Your rules decide whether a rejection costs you a little or a lot.

- **Inspect before dispatch.** Check the item, the size, the colour and the packaging before it leaves. Most "wrong item" rejections are dispatch errors, not customer dishonesty.
- **Allow inspection on delivery, within limits.** Let the customer check the item in the rider's presence before paying. This is fair and it builds trust. But state that the item must not be used, worn outside or removed from sealed packaging before payment.
- **Charge a return fee for change-of-mind rejections.** If the customer ordered correctly, the item arrived correctly, and they simply changed their mind, the delivery fee they paid upfront is non-refundable. State this in your confirmation message so it is agreed before dispatch.
- **Replace or refund genuine errors fast.** If you sent the wrong size or a faulty item, apologise, replace it at your cost, and follow up after. A fast fix turns a rejection into loyalty.
- **Track repeat rejecters.** A customer who rejects three orders in a row is not a customer — they are a cost. Politely decline future COD orders from them, or require full prepayment. You are running a business, not a viewing centre.

## Rule 5: Package for the Journey

COD goods travel more than prepaid goods — out to the customer, sometimes back again. Packaging is protection.

Use sturdy bags or boxes, not thin nylon that tears. Seal fashion items so they cannot be tried on and returned as new without evidence. Include your business card and a short thank-you note inside every package — the unboxing moment is marketing. For fragile items, add padding and mark the package clearly. A broken item delivered COD is a rejection, a return trip and a lost sale all at once.

Photograph the package before dispatch — the sealed item, the label, the rider collecting it. If a customer later claims the item arrived damaged or different, your photo is your evidence.

## Reduce COD Over Time: Convert Customers to Prepayment

COD is a trust-building tool, not a permanent way of doing business. Your goal is to move customers from COD to prepayment as trust grows.

- **After two or three successful COD orders**, offer the customer a small discount on prepayment — for example, 5 percent off, or free delivery on prepaid orders. Many will switch once they trust you.
- **Build trust publicly.** Post delivery videos, customer testimonials and unboxing photos. When new customers see hundreds of successful deliveries, they need COD less.
- **Offer secure payment links.** A Paystack or Flutterwave payment link feels safer than a direct transfer to a personal account. Give customers a proper checkout option and many will use it.
- **Reward prepaid orders.** Free delivery for prepaid orders above a set amount costs you less than the failed-delivery rate on COD, and it nudges behaviour in the right direction.

Track the ratio monthly: what percentage of your orders are COD versus prepaid? If COD falls from 80 percent to 50 percent over six months while total orders grow, your trust-building is working — and your profit per order is rising with it.

## What to Do When a Delivery Fails

Despite everything, some deliveries will fail. Handle them by the book:

1. **Call the customer immediately.** Sometimes they were in a meeting, their phone died, or the rider went to the wrong street. A quick call often saves the delivery the same day.
2. **Reschedule once, at the customer's cost or shared cost.** One free reschedule is fair. A second failed attempt means the customer pays the redelivery fee upfront or the order is cancelled.
3. **Cancel politely after two failed attempts.** "Good afternoon, we tried to deliver twice without success, so we have cancelled this order. Your delivery fee is non-refundable, but you are welcome to reorder with full prepayment." Firm, polite, final.
4. **Log every failed delivery.** Customer name, reason, cost. Patterns emerge fast: one area with bad addresses, one rider with repeated failures, one product with frequent rejections. Fix the pattern, not just the incident.
5. **Review your failed-delivery rate monthly.** If more than 10 percent of COD orders fail, something in your verification or rider process needs fixing. Below 5 percent is healthy for most Nigerian sellers.

## Set Your COD Limits

Not every order should be eligible for cash on delivery, and not every area should be covered. Setting limits protects your margins and your time.

**Set a maximum order value for COD.** Many sellers cap COD at ₦100,000 or ₦200,000. Above that, require a substantial part-payment or full prepayment. A ₦500,000 generator sent on pure COD is a risk no small business needs to take.

**Limit the areas you cover.** Start with your city — Lagos, Abuja or Port Harcourt — where your riders know the streets and a failed delivery costs a few thousand naira. Expand to inter-state COD only after your local system runs smoothly, and charge a higher upfront deposit for those orders.

**Cap the number of COD orders per day.** If you can reliably handle ten COD deliveries a day, do not accept twenty. Overloaded riders rush, make mistakes and miss remittances. It is better to deliver ten orders well than to fail at twenty.

**Review the limits monthly.** As your verification improves and your failed-delivery rate falls, you can raise the cap or extend the coverage. Limits are not permanent — they grow with your capacity.

## COD for Different Business Types

**Fashion and beauty sellers.** COD works well because customers want to see the fabric, the size and the colour before paying. Verification matters most here — confirm size and colour on the call, because wrong-size rejections are the biggest cost.

**Electronics and phone sellers.** COD is risky for high-value items. Collect a substantial deposit, verify identity, and consider releasing only after the deposit plus a guarantor for items above ₦200,000. Let the customer test the item in the rider's presence.

**Food vendors.** COD suits food because customers pay for what they can see and smell. But food cannot be returned, so verification and timing are critical — deliver while the food is fresh, and confirm the customer is available at the exact hour.

**Furniture and home items.** Large items mean expensive failed deliveries. Collect a large deposit — 40 to 50 percent — and confirm the delivery address can actually receive the item. A sofa that cannot fit through a customer's door is a costly lesson.

## Final Word

Cash on delivery is not a favour you do for customers. It is a service with a price, a process and rules. Charge the delivery fee upfront, verify every order by call, use riders you trust, insist on same-day remittance, and track every failed delivery.

Do those things, and COD becomes what it should be: a door that lets cautious first-time buyers into your business, where your products and service then convince them to stay — and eventually to pay before delivery, like the loyal customers they become.

Offer COD with confidence, but never without control. The sellers who lose money on COD are not the ones who offer it. They are the ones who offer it without rules.
