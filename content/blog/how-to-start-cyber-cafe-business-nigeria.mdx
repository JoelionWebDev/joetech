Smartphones have not killed the cyber cafe in Nigeria. Every day, students need to print assignments and apply for admissions, job seekers need to type and submit CVs, business owners need to scan documents and make photocopies, and candidates need to register for JAMB, WAEC, NYSC and other examinations online. Many of them do not own a computer or a printer, or they need a faster connection and a quiet place to work. A well-run cyber cafe — also called a business centre — still meets that need, and it can be a steady small business if it is run properly.

The modern cyber cafe is not just rows of computers for browsing. The most profitable ones earn from a mix of services: browsing, typing, printing, photocopying, scanning, laminating, passport photographs, online registrations, exam pin sales, computer training and sometimes selling accessories. The businesses that struggle are the ones that depend on browsing alone, in an area where almost everyone already has a phone with data.

This guide walks you through the business step by step: understanding the market, choosing a location, working out startup costs, buying computers and equipment, setting up internet and power, pricing your services, and attracting the customers who will keep your machines busy.

## 1. Understand How a Cyber Cafe Makes Money

A cyber cafe earns money in small amounts, many times a day. A customer pays for 30 minutes of browsing, another pays ₦200 to print five pages, a student pays ₦1,500 for typing and printing a project, a job seeker pays ₦2,000 for a CV and a passport photograph. None of these is a large amount on its own. The profit comes from volume — dozens of small transactions every day — and from offering enough services that a customer who comes in for one thing buys two or three.

The main income streams are browsing and computer use, charged per hour or per session; typing and document services, charged per page; printing in black and white and in colour, charged per page; photocopying and scanning; laminating and binding; passport photographs; and assisted online services such as JAMB and NYSC registration, job applications, admission checks, flight bookings, utility payments and exam result printing. Training is another stream: short courses in computer basics, Microsoft Word and Excel, and internet skills can bring in regular fees from students and job seekers. Selling accessories such as flash drives, earphones and printer paper adds a small but useful margin.

Your costs are rent, internet subscription, electricity and fuel for a generator, staff wages, printer ink and paper, computer maintenance, and replacement of worn-out equipment. The business is sensitive to power and internet costs in particular: a cafe that depends on a generator all day will spend a large share of its income on fuel. That is why location, power planning and pricing matter so much.

There are three common formats. A small business centre has three to six computers in a shop, focused on printing, typing and photocopying, and works well near schools, markets and offices. A standard cyber cafe has eight to 15 computers, a mix of browsing and printing services, and often a small training section. A large cafe or training centre has 20 or more computers, dedicated training rooms, and sometimes a separate printing section. Beginners should start with the small format, learn the demand in their area, and expand only when the machines are busy most of the day.

## 2. Choose the Right Location

Location is the single most important decision in this business. A cyber cafe in the wrong place will sit empty no matter how good the computers are. The best locations are close to schools, polytechnics and universities, where students need printing, typing and registration services daily; near markets and commercial areas, where traders need photocopies, scanning and online payments; near government offices, courts and banks, where people need documents typed, printed and laminated; and in busy residential areas with many young people and job seekers.

Visit your chosen area before committing. Count how many cyber cafes already exist, and observe them at different times of day. A street with two busy cafes may still have room for a third if it offers faster service, better computers or longer hours — but a street with five empty cafes is a warning. Talk to students, traders and shop owners nearby and ask what services they struggle to find. If they say they travel far for colour printing, passport photographs or laminating, that is a gap you can fill.

Check the practical details of the shop itself. You need enough space for computers with comfortable seating, a counter for printing and payments, and a small waiting area. Good ventilation matters, because computers generate heat and customers will not stay in a hot, stuffy room. The shop needs a reliable power supply or space for a generator, and it should be secure, with a good door and lock, because computers and printers are valuable and attractive to thieves. Rent varies widely: a small shop in a modest area might cost ₦250,000 to ₦800,000 per year, while a shop in a prime location in Lekki, Ikeja or central Abuja can cost several million naira per year, often with one or two years demanded upfront. Confirm the rent, agency fee and local government levies in writing before you pay anything.

## 3. Work Out Your Startup Costs

Write a simple plan listing your services, target customers, location, startup costs and prices. Then build a realistic budget. Prices change with the market, so confirm current prices from suppliers in your city before you buy.

A small starter setup with five computers might look like this. Desktop computers: five fairly used desktops at ₦150,000 to ₦250,000 each, or ₦750,000 to ₦1,250,000, or more for new machines. New computers last longer and fail less, but fairly used ones are a sensible start if you buy from a trusted seller and test each one. Printers: a black-and-white laser printer for ₦150,000 to ₦300,000, plus a colour inkjet printer for ₦120,000 to ₦250,000. A photocopier can cost ₦400,000 to ₦1 million, so many beginners start with a good printer that also copies and scans, then add a dedicated copier later. A scanner, laminating machine and binding machine together: ₦80,000 to ₦200,000. Tables, chairs, a counter and a signboard: ₦200,000 to ₦400,000. A generator or inverter system: ₦300,000 to ₦800,000 depending on size. Initial paper, ink, toner and accessories: ₦100,000 to ₦200,000. Internet setup and the first subscription: ₦50,000 to ₦150,000.

Add shop rent and deposit, business registration, and a working float for fuel, data and restocking. Altogether, a small business centre can be started for roughly ₦2 million to ₦4 million, while a standard cafe with 10 to 15 computers will more realistically need ₦5 million to ₦8 million. If your budget is smaller, start with three computers, one printer and the most essential services, then add machines from profits as demand grows. Do not spend everything on computers and leave nothing for fuel and internet — a cafe with computers but no power or connection earns nothing.

## 4. Buy Computers, Printers and Other Equipment

Buy equipment that is reliable, not just cheap. A computer that freezes during a JAMB registration or a printer that jams during a 50-page project will cost you the customer and their friends. Test every computer before paying: check that it starts properly, connects to the internet, runs Microsoft Word and a browser smoothly, and that the keyboard, mouse and monitor all work. For fairly used computers, ask about the age, the condition of the hard drive, and whether the seller offers any short warranty.

Choose desktop computers for a cafe, not laptops. Desktops are cheaper for the same performance, easier to repair, harder to steal, and their parts — keyboards, mice, monitors — can be replaced individually. A basic specification that works well is at least 4GB of RAM, a 500GB hard drive or a smaller solid-state drive, and a recent version of Windows. Install Microsoft Office, a PDF reader, a good browser, antivirus software, and the applications your customers need, such as a passport-photo tool and a typing tutor for training. Keep the computers clean, with dust covers at night, and restart them regularly.

For printing, a laser printer is best for black-and-white work: it is fast, the cost per page is low, and the quality is sharp. An inkjet printer handles colour printing and photos. Keep spare ink, toner and paper in stock at all times — running out of paper on a busy morning is an avoidable loss. A multifunction printer that prints, copies and scans saves space and money at the start, but a dedicated photocopier is worth adding once copying volume grows, because it is faster and cheaper per page.

Other useful equipment includes a laminating machine, a binding machine, a webcam or camera for passport photographs with a plain white background and good lighting, a UPS for each computer or a central inverter so work is not lost when power fails, and a router and network switch for sharing the internet. Label every computer and keep a simple inventory list with purchase dates and prices, so you know what needs replacing and when.

## 5. Set Up Internet and Solve the Power Problem

Internet is the heart of a cyber cafe. Customers will leave if pages load slowly or the connection drops during an application. Choose an internet provider with good coverage in your area — fibre is best where available, followed by a reliable 4G router with a good data plan. Compare at least two providers on speed, monthly cost and reliability, and ask neighbouring businesses which one they use. A monthly business plan of ₦20,000 to ₦50,000 is common for a small cafe, with higher plans for larger cafes. Keep a backup connection, even a small mobile data plan, so a single provider failure does not close your shop for the day.

Secure your network. Set a strong router password, limit access to your own computers, and consider cafe-management software that tracks browsing time per computer and charges automatically. This prevents arguments about time used and stops customers from accessing your admin settings. Install antivirus software and keep it updated, and consider a simple content filter, especially if students and young people use your computers. Clear browsing history and downloaded files from shared computers regularly, and remind customers not to save passwords on public machines.

Power is the biggest operational challenge. Plan for it from the start. A combination of public power supply, an inverter with batteries for short outages, and a generator for longer ones works best for most cafes. An inverter keeps computers running quietly during brief outages; a generator handles long ones but costs fuel and makes noise. Calculate your daily fuel cost honestly — a small generator can use ₦5,000 to ₦15,000 of fuel per day if it runs for many hours — and build it into your prices. Use energy-saving monitors and switch off unused computers. Where possible, negotiate with your landlord or neighbours about a shared generator arrangement, and always keep spare fuel in a safe place. Never run a generator indoors or in an enclosed space.

## 6. Price Your Services and Register the Business

Your prices must cover internet, power, rent, staff and maintenance, plus a profit, while staying fair enough that customers return. Check the going rates in your area first — prices vary between Lagos, Abuja and smaller towns — then set a clear price list and display it where every customer can see it. A visible price list prevents haggling over every page and builds trust.

Typical planning ranges are: browsing ₦200 to ₦500 per hour, depending on location and connection speed; black-and-white printing ₦50 to ₦100 per page; colour printing ₦200 to ₦500 per page; photocopying ₦30 to ₦50 per page; scanning ₦200 to ₦500 per document; typing ₦200 to ₦500 per page, with more for complex documents and tables; laminating ₦500 to ₦1,500 depending on size; binding ₦1,000 to ₦3,000; and passport photographs ₦1,000 to ₦2,500 for a set. Assisted services such as JAMB registration, NYSC registration or a job application are usually priced as a package — ₦1,500 to ₦5,000 depending on the work involved — plus any official fees, which should always be explained separately and receipted.

Offer bundles to increase the value of each visit: a student package for typing, printing and binding a project; a job-seeker package for a CV, cover letter and passport photographs; and a business package for regular photocopying and scanning. Give a small discount for bulk printing, such as 100 pages or more, but decide your lowest price in advance and do not go below it. Accept cash, bank transfer and POS, and give receipts for larger jobs.

Register the business with the CAC and open a separate bank account. A registered business can issue proper receipts, build a relationship with suppliers, and be taken seriously by schools and organisations that may want a regular printing arrangement. Keep a daily sales record — even a simple notebook — showing what came in and what went out. At the end of each week, check which services earned the most and which machines were busiest, so you know where to invest next.

## 7. Attract Customers and Keep Them Coming Back

Speed, accuracy and politeness win customers in this business. A student whose assignment is printed correctly and on time will return with friends. A job seeker whose CV is typed neatly will recommend you to others. Train your staff to greet customers, confirm the job before starting — number of pages, colour or black and white, single or double-sided — and to check the work before handing it over. Mistakes cost paper, ink and time, so a quick check saves money.

Market locally and online. Put a clear signboard outside, with your services and opening hours. Share your price list and services on WhatsApp status and in local community groups, and create a Google Business Profile so people searching for a cyber cafe or printing shop near them can find you. Visit nearby schools, churches, offices and estates with a flyer, and offer a fair introductory discount to first-time customers. Build relationships with teachers, school administrators and local business owners, who can send you regular work such as exam printing, staff ID cards and bulk photocopying.

Add services as demand grows. Computer training is a strong addition: a four- to six-week course in computer basics, Word and Excel can bring in ₦20,000 to ₦50,000 per student, and it fills your computers during quiet daytime hours. Online services — exam registrations, admission checks, flight bookings, utility payments and visa applications — earn fees and bring customers who also print and photocopy. Selling accessories such as flash drives, earphones and printer paper adds income without much extra work.

Protect the business day to day. Back up important files, keep spare keyboards, mice and cables, and clean the computers and printers regularly. Keep a small maintenance float so a faulty printer can be repaired quickly instead of sitting idle for a week. Be careful with credit: many cafes lose money by allowing regulars to use computers or print on credit. If you allow it at all, keep it small, record it in writing, and set a clear repayment time.

Start with a few reliable computers, a fast connection, a power plan and a clear price list. Serve students, job seekers and local businesses well, add training and online services as you grow, and your cyber cafe can become a trusted business centre in your area for years.
