## Why a Small Business Needs a Plan, Even a Simple One

Many Nigerian small businesses start without a plan. A tailor starts sewing for neighbours, a food vendor starts cooking for an office, a trader starts selling from a small table in the market. The business grows by word of mouth, and for a while that is enough. Then a problem arrives: rent goes up, a supplier raises prices, a competitor opens nearby, or the owner wants a loan and the bank asks for a plan.

A business plan is not a thick document for big companies. For a small business, it is a short, clear written answer to five questions: what do you sell, who buys it, how will you reach them, how much will it cost, and how much money will you make? Writing those answers down forces you to think clearly, spot problems early, and make better decisions.

This guide shows you how to write a simple business plan in one afternoon, using plain language and real Nigerian examples. You do not need a degree in business, a consultant, or expensive software. You need a notebook or a laptop, honest figures, and a few hours of focused thinking.

## What a Simple Business Plan Looks Like

A simple plan for a small business in Nigeria can fit on three to five pages. It should be clear enough that a friend, a partner, or a bank officer can read it and understand your business in ten minutes.

Your plan should have eight short sections: your business idea, your products or services, your customers, your competitors, your marketing plan, your operations, your team, and your money — costs, prices, and sales targets. Each section can be a few paragraphs or a short list. The aim is clarity, not length.

Write the plan for yourself first. If you later need it for a loan, a grant, or a partner, you can expand it and add more detail. Start with a version that helps you run the business better tomorrow, not a version that impresses someone else.

## Step 1: Describe Your Business Idea Clearly

Start with one paragraph that says what your business does, where it is based, and what problem it solves. Be specific. "We sell ready-to-wear Ankara dresses for working women in Lekki" is clear. "We sell clothes" is not.

Add a sentence about why you started the business and what you want it to become in the next year. For example: "We started in 2024 from home, and in the next twelve months we want to open a small shop in Lekki Phase 1 and sell 100 dresses a month." A clear goal helps you decide what to do next, and what to say no to.

If you have a business name, write it down, along with your location, your phone number, and your main sales channels — your shop, WhatsApp, Instagram, Jumia, or a market stall. If you are registered with the Corporate Affairs Commission, note your business name and registration details. If you are not yet registered, note that as a task to complete, not as a reason to delay the plan.

## Step 2: List Your Products or Services

Write down exactly what you sell. List each product or service, its price, and what makes it different. A caterer might list small chops at ₦5,000 per pack, a full tray at ₦45,000, and a full event service starting at ₦250,000. A phone repair shop might list screen replacement, battery replacement, and software fixes, each with a price range.

For each item, note the cost of providing it and the profit you make. That link between your plan and your pricing is essential. If you do not know the profit on your best-selling item, your plan is incomplete.

Also note what you will not sell, at least for now. A business that tries to sell everything often sells nothing well. Choosing a clear focus — for example, women's ready-to-wear, not men's, children's, and women's clothing all at once — makes your marketing clearer and your stock easier to manage.

## Step 3: Describe Your Customers

A business that tries to serve everyone serves no one well. Describe your ideal customer in plain terms: who they are, where they live or work, how much they can spend, and why they buy from you.

A good customer description looks like this: "Our customers are working women aged 25 to 45 in Lekki and Victoria Island. They earn a steady salary, shop on Instagram and WhatsApp, and want quality ready-to-wear outfits they can wear to the office and to events. They are willing to pay ₦15,000 to ₦25,000 for a dress if the quality and fit are good."

Think about how many such customers you need. If you need to sell 50 dresses a month at ₦20,000 each to reach ₦1,000,000 in sales, you need enough customers who will buy regularly, or enough new customers each month to replace those who do not return. Write down how you will find them — through Instagram, WhatsApp broadcast, referrals, a market stall, or Google Maps — and how you will keep them coming back.

## Step 4: Look Honestly at Your Competitors

Every business has competitors, even if you cannot see them. A tailor's competitor is not only other tailors, but also ready-to-wear shops and online sellers. A restaurant's competitor is not only other restaurants, but also home cooking and food delivery apps.

List three or four competitors in your area or online. For each one, note what they sell, what they charge, what they do well, and where they are weak. Perhaps one competitor is cheap but slow, another is fast but expensive, and a third has good products but poor customer service. Your plan should say how you will be different — faster delivery, better quality, clearer prices, or a better location.

Do not copy a competitor's prices or products without understanding their costs. Use the research to find a gap you can fill. If every salon in your area closes at 6pm, opening until 8pm could win you customers who work late. If every food vendor in your area sells the same menu, a new dish or a better package could set you apart.

## Step 5: Write Your Marketing and Sales Plan

Your marketing plan should answer three questions: how will customers find you, how will you convince them to buy, and how will you keep them coming back?

Start with the channels that already work for you. For many Nigerian small businesses, that is WhatsApp, Instagram, referrals, and a physical shop or market stall. Write down what you will do on each channel each week. For example: post three times a week on Instagram, send a WhatsApp broadcast twice a week, and ask every happy customer for a referral.

Set a simple monthly sales target. How many products will you sell, at what price, to reach your monthly sales goal? If your goal is ₦600,000 a month and your average sale is ₦15,000, you need 40 sales a month, or about ten a week. Breaking a big goal into weekly numbers makes it easier to track and easier to fix when you fall behind.

Write down how you will handle sales: how customers order, how they pay, how you deliver, and how you follow up. A clear sales process — order on WhatsApp, pay by transfer, delivery in 24 to 48 hours in Lagos — reduces confusion and builds trust.

## Step 6: Plan Your Daily Operations

Operations is a formal word for a simple idea: how your business runs day to day. Write down where you work, what hours you keep, where you buy your materials, how you store stock, and how you deliver to customers.

If you are a trader, note your suppliers in Balogun, Onitsha, or Aba, how often you restock, and how you transport goods. If you are a service business, note how customers book, how long each job takes, and what equipment you need. If you deliver, note whether you use a rider, a dispatch company, or your own bike, and what it costs.

Also note the licences, registrations, or permissions you need. A food business may need health and safety checks. A registered business may need a tax identification number. A shop may need a local government permit. List what you already have and what you still need to get, with a date for each one.

## Step 7: Describe Your Team

Even if you work alone, write down who does what. If you are the owner, the salesperson, the packer, and the delivery person, say so — and note which of those jobs you want help with first as the business grows.

If you have staff, list each person, their role, and what they are paid. A small shop might have an owner, a sales assistant, and a part-time rider. A catering business might have a cook, an assistant, and a driver for events. Be honest about what you can afford. Hiring too quickly can drain your cash, while doing everything yourself for too long can limit your growth.

If you work with family members or partners, write down who is responsible for money, who is responsible for customers, and who makes final decisions. Many small business disputes in Nigeria start because these roles were never written down. A clear plan prevents arguments later.

## Step 8: Work Out Your Money — Costs, Prices, and Targets

This is the section most people skip, and it is the most important. Your money section should have three parts: start-up costs, monthly running costs, and a sales forecast.

Start-up costs are what you need to begin or to grow: a shop deposit, equipment, initial stock, a signboard, or a website. List each item with its cost. If you need ₦500,000 to start, write down where that money will come from — your savings, a loan from family, a cooperative, or a bank.

Monthly running costs are what you pay every month to stay open: rent, salaries, transport, data, electricity, packaging, and marketing. Add them up. If your monthly costs are ₦250,000, you need to make at least that much in gross profit each month just to break even, before you pay yourself.

Your sales forecast is your best honest estimate of what you will sell each month for the next six to twelve months. Start with your current sales if you already trade, and grow the figure gradually as your marketing improves. Do not invent a large figure to make the plan look good. A modest, honest forecast that you can actually achieve is far more useful than an optimistic one that you miss every month.

From those three parts, work out your break-even point: how many sales you need each month to cover your costs. If your costs are ₦250,000 and your profit per sale is ₦5,000, you need 50 sales a month to break even. Every sale after that is profit. Knowing that number helps you decide whether your price, your costs, or your sales target needs to change.

## Step 9: Set Goals and Review Them Monthly

A plan that sits in a drawer is useless. Turn your plan into three simple goals for the next three months, and review them at the end of each month.

Your goals should be specific and measurable. "Increase sales" is vague. "Sell 60 dresses in March, with at least 20 sales from repeat customers" is clear. "Get more customers" is vague. "Add 100 new WhatsApp contacts who have saved our number, and get 10 reviews on Google" is clear.

At the end of each month, compare your actual sales with your forecast. If you missed your target, ask why. Was the price too high? Was your marketing inconsistent? Did a supplier fail you? Fix the cause, update the plan, and set a new target for the next month. A plan is not fixed in stone. It is a working document that should change as your business learns what works.

It also helps to write down what success looks like in a year, not just in three months. Will you have a shop, a second staff member, or a new product line? Will you be selling in another city, or supplying a larger customer? A one-year picture keeps you from chasing every small opportunity and helps you choose the work that moves you towards the bigger goal. Review that picture every three months, and change it if your business or your market has changed.

## Common Mistakes When Writing a Business Plan

The first mistake is writing a plan that is too long and too complicated. A small business does not need a 40-page document. A clear five-page plan that you actually use is better than a long plan that no one reads.

The second mistake is using figures you have not checked. If you guess your costs, your plan will be wrong. Spend time checking supplier prices, transport costs, and rent before you write the money section.

The third mistake is writing the plan for a bank or a grant, not for yourself. If you write what you think a lender wants to hear, you will end up with a plan that does not reflect your real business. Write the truth first, then adapt the format if you need to submit it.

The fourth mistake is never reviewing the plan. A plan written in January and ignored until December will not help you. Set a monthly reminder to read it, update the figures, and check your progress.

## A One-Page Business Plan Template

If you want to start today, use this one-page template. Fill in each heading in a few lines, and you will have a working plan by the end of the afternoon.

Business name and location. What you sell and who buys it. Your prices and your profit per sale. Your three main competitors and how you are different. How customers will find you. Your monthly costs and your break-even sales number. Your sales target for the next three months. The first three actions you will take this week.

Keep that page where you can see it — on your shop wall, in your notebook, or on your phone. When you face a decision, check it against the plan. If a new idea does not fit the plan, either change the plan deliberately or say no to the idea.

## Final Thoughts

A business plan does not guarantee success, but it does make success more likely. It forces you to think about your customers, your costs, and your sales before problems arrive, rather than after. It gives you a target to aim for, and a way to know whether you are on track.

Write your plan this week. Start with the one-page template, then expand it into the eight sections in this guide. Share it with a trusted friend, partner, or mentor, and ask them to point out what is unclear. Then use it. Review it monthly, update it when things change, and let it guide your decisions as your business grows in Nigeria.
