A visitor lands on your website and, within seconds, starts to judge whether your business is real, serious and worth contacting. Words matter, but photos do a lot of that judging first. A photo of your actual shop, team or work says "we exist and we do this". A generic stock photo says "we have a website". Both have a place, but using the wrong one in the wrong spot quietly costs you trust.

The question is not which type of photo is better in general. It is which type belongs where, and how to mix them so your website feels honest and professional at the same time.

## Why Photos Affect Trust So Much

People buy from businesses they trust, and trust online is built from evidence. A real photo is evidence. It shows your premises, your people and your finished work, and it gives visitors something they can picture themselves walking into. A visitor who sees the inside of a real restaurant, the hands behind a real product, or the team who will answer the phone feels closer to doing business with you.

Stock photos, on the other hand, are evidence of nothing except good taste. A smiling team in a bright office might look pleasant, but if that office is not yours and that team is not yours, a sharp visitor will sense it. Nothing damages trust faster than a website that presents borrowed images as if they were its own work, especially on pages about your team, your premises or your projects.

That does not mean stock photos are dishonest. It means they need to be used for the right job.

## When Real Photos Win

Use real photos wherever the visitor is checking whether you are genuine. These are the pages where real photos matter most:

- **Your homepage hero section.** The first image a visitor sees should feel like your business. A real photo of your premises, your product or your work sets the tone immediately.
- **About and team pages.** If you introduce people, show those people. Visitors want to know who they will deal with, and a stock team photo on a team page feels misleading.
- **Your work, projects and portfolio.** Show your own finished work, with your own photos. This is your proof, and borrowed images cannot substitute for it.
- **Testimonials and case studies.** A testimonial beside a real photo of the work described carries far more weight than one beside a generic image.
- **Location and contact pages.** A real photo of your building, entrance or shopfront helps visitors recognise you when they arrive, and reassures them that you have a real base.

Real does not mean perfect. A clear, well-lit phone photo of your actual work beats a flawless stock image of someone else's work, because honesty is part of what the visitor is buying.

## When Stock Photos Are Fine

Stock photos earn their place when the image is illustrative rather than evidential. In other words, when the photo is there to set a mood or explain an idea, not to prove something about your business.

Good uses include:

- **Blog article headers.** A blog post about time management or customer service does not need a photo of your office. A fitting stock image sets the theme without pretending to be your business.
- **Backgrounds and decorative sections.** A soft, attractive background image can make a page feel polished, as long as it is not presented as your premises or your team.
- **Concepts that are hard to photograph.** Ideas like growth, planning or security are easier to suggest with a stock image than to stage yourself.
- **Placeholders while you gather real photos.** When a website first launches, stock images can fill gaps temporarily, provided you replace the important ones, such as homepage and team photos, with real ones as soon as you can.

The rule is simple: if a visitor could reasonably think the photo shows your business, your people or your work, it must actually be yours.

## How to Mix Them Without Looking Fake

Most good business websites use both. The trick is making the mix feel deliberate rather than random. Here is how:

- **Keep a consistent style.** Choose images with similar brightness, colour and feeling, so a stock image beside a real one does not jump out. Avoid mixing dark, moody photos with bright, cheerful ones on the same page.
- **Lead with real photos.** Put your real photos in the most prominent spots, such as the homepage and the top of service pages, and use stock images further down or in supporting sections.
- **Do not use famous-looking stock images.** If a photo looks like it appears on many websites, visitors will recognise it as stock, and that recognition weakens the real photos around it. Choose less obvious images.
- **Caption honestly where it matters.** You do not need to label every decorative image, but never caption a stock photo as if it were your work, your office or your team.
- **Check your images on a phone.** Most visitors will see your website on a phone first. A photo that looks fine on a large screen can look cramped or unclear on a small one, so check the important images there.

## Practical Tips for Taking Decent Real Photos With a Phone

You do not need a professional camera to get usable real photos. A modern phone, used carefully, is enough for most website needs:

- **Use natural light.** Take photos near a window or outdoors in soft daylight, and avoid harsh direct sun or dark rooms.
- **Clean the scene first.** Remove clutter, straighten items and clear the background of anything distracting before you shoot.
- **Take more than you need.** Shoot several angles of the same subject, then pick the best. It costs nothing extra and gives you options.
- **Show people at work, not posing stiffly.** A photo of your team serving a customer or finishing a job feels more genuine than everyone lined up against a wall.
- **Keep the originals.** Save the full-size photos, not only the compressed copies you send on chat apps, so your website developer can use them at proper quality.

A small set of honest, clear real photos will do more for your website than a large library of polished images of other people's businesses.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I use stock photos on my homepage?**

You can use one as a background or supporting image, but the main image should ideally be real. The homepage is where visitors decide whether your business is genuine, so lead with something that is actually yours.

**What if my business has nothing photogenic to show?**

Almost every business does. Your workspace, your tools, your products, your packaging, your team at work, or a finished job can all be photographed well with a phone and good light. Start with what you have.

**Do I need permission to use stock photos?**

Yes. Only use stock photos from sources that allow the use you intend, and check whether credit is required. Free does not always mean free for business use, so read the terms before publishing.

**Should I replace all my stock photos with real ones eventually?**

Replace the important ones first: homepage, about, team, work and contact pages. Decorative blog headers and background images can stay stock, as long as they are not presented as your business.

## Show Visitors the Real Business

Your website should feel like a window into your business, not a catalogue of borrowed pictures. Lead with real photos where trust matters, use stock photos where they illustrate an idea, and keep the style consistent across the site.
