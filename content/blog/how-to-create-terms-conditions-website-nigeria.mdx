You have built a website for your business. It looks good, your products are listed, and customers can pay online. Then one day, a customer in Abuja orders a product, pays, and later demands a full refund because the item arrived a day late — even though your delivery page clearly said three to five working days. Or a customer in Port Harcourt claims your product damaged their property and threatens to sue. Or someone copies all your product photos and prices onto their own website.

Terms and conditions are the rules of doing business with you. They are the agreement between your business and everyone who uses your website. They tell customers what they can expect, what you promise, what you do not promise, and what happens when something goes wrong. They are not just legal decoration. For a Nigerian business selling online, they are one of the cheapest forms of protection you can have.

This guide shows you how to create terms and conditions for your business website in Nigeria, step by step. You do not need to be a lawyer to write a first version, and you do not need to spend hundreds of thousands of naira to start. You need to understand what belongs in the document, write it in plain language your customers can actually understand, and keep it updated. We will also cover the Nigerian laws that matter, the common mistakes that make terms useless, and when you should pay a lawyer to review your work.

## What Terms and Conditions Actually Are

Terms and conditions — sometimes called terms of service or terms of use — are a legal agreement between you (the business) and your website visitors and customers. When a customer buys from your website, they are entering into a contract with you. Your terms spell out the details of that contract.

Think of them like the rules printed on the back of a bus ticket, or the agreement you sign when you rent a shop. Nobody reads them for fun, but when there is a disagreement, everyone reaches for them. They answer questions like: When is payment due? Who pays for delivery? What happens if the product is damaged? Can the customer return it? How long does a refund take? What happens if the website goes down during a sale?

Without terms, those questions are answered by argument, by social media pressure, or by whoever shouts loudest. With terms, they are answered by a document the customer agreed to before paying.

## Why Your Nigerian Business Needs Terms

Many Nigerian small businesses run on WhatsApp and Instagram without any written terms at all. That works until it does not. Here are the specific reasons a website needs them.

First, terms reduce disputes. Most customer arguments are not about bad intentions; they are about mismatched expectations. A customer expects delivery in 24 hours because a competitor delivers in 24 hours. Your terms say three to five working days. If that is written clearly and the customer agreed to it at checkout, the argument ends quickly. Without it, you are negotiating from scratch every time.

Second, terms protect you from unfair liability. If a customer misuses your product and blames you, your terms can state the limits of your responsibility. They cannot remove all liability — no document can — but they can draw a reasonable line. For example, a skincare seller can state that customers should do a patch test first, and that the business is not liable for reactions when the customer ignores the instructions. That single sentence has saved many small businesses from expensive trouble.

Third, terms protect your content. Your product photos, your descriptions, your prices, your logo — all of that is your work. Your terms can state that visitors may not copy, reuse, or resell your content without permission. It will not stop a determined thief, but it gives you a clear basis to demand a takedown.

## The Nigerian Laws That Shape Your Terms

You do not need a law degree, but you do need to know which Nigerian laws your terms must respect. A term that contradicts Nigerian law is unenforceable, no matter how strongly it is worded. Here are the ones that matter most.

**The Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Act (FCCPA) 2018.** This is the main consumer protection law in Nigeria, enforced by the Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (FCCPC). It says businesses must not mislead consumers, must not sell unsafe products, and must honour reasonable consumer rights. Your terms cannot take away rights the FCCPA gives customers — for example, you cannot write a term that says "no refunds under any circumstances, ever" and expect it to hold up if you sold a faulty product. Your terms must work with consumer protection law, not against it.

**The Nigeria Data Protection Act (NDPA) 2023.** This governs how you collect, store, and use customers' personal data — names, phone numbers, addresses, payment details. If your website collects any of this, which it almost certainly does, you need a privacy policy that explains what you collect and why, and you need to handle the data responsibly. Your terms should point to your privacy policy. The NDPA is enforced by the Nigeria Data Protection Commission (NDPC).

You do not need to quote these laws in your terms. You need to write terms that respect them: honest descriptions, fair refund rules for faulty goods, proper handling of personal data, and clear identification of your business.

## Step 1: Choose How to Create Your Terms

There are four ways to get terms and conditions, and the right choice depends on your budget and your risk.

**Option 1: Write them yourself from a good structure.** This guide gives you that structure. Cost: free. Best for: small businesses selling simple products, with straightforward delivery and refund rules. Risk: you may miss something important, so a later lawyer review is still wise.

**Option 2: Use a reputable terms generator.** Several online tools generate terms from a questionnaire about your business. They are fast and cheap, sometimes free. Best for: a first version you can publish quickly. Risk: many generators are written for American or European law and may include clauses that do not fit Nigeria. Always read every clause and edit it to match your actual business. Never publish a generated document without reading it.

**Option 3: Adapt a template from a trusted source.** Some Nigerian legal and business organisations publish sample terms. These can be a good starting point because they are closer to Nigerian law. Best for: businesses that want a solid draft at low cost. Risk: a template written for a different kind of business — say, a software company — will not fit a fashion store. Adapt it carefully.

**Option 4: Hire a Nigerian lawyer.** This is the safest option, and it is less expensive than most people assume. A lawyer can draft or review terms for a small business for a fee that is often between ₦50,000 and ₦200,000 depending on complexity. Best for: businesses selling high-value items, regulated products like food, cosmetics, or medicine, or businesses that handle large volumes of customer data. If your business grows, this becomes non-negotiable.

The practical path for most small businesses is: write a solid first version yourself using this guide, publish it, and get a lawyer to review it within the next three to six months. That gives you protection now and professional quality soon.

## Step 2: Write the Introduction and Acceptance Clause

Every terms document starts by identifying the parties and explaining that using the website means agreeing to the terms. Keep it simple and clear.

Start with your business identity: "These Terms and Conditions govern your use of [your website address], operated by [your registered business name], a business registered in Nigeria." Add your contact details.

Then the acceptance clause. Something like: "By accessing this website, browsing our products, or placing an order, you agree to these Terms and Conditions. If you do not agree, please do not use this website or place an order." This matters because it establishes that the customer accepted the terms before buying.

Write this section in plain English. Your customers are not lawyers. If a customer cannot understand your terms, they cannot meaningfully agree to them, and a court may not enforce them.

## Step 3: Describe Your Products and Prices Accurately

This section states what you sell and how prices work. It prevents the most common disputes.

State that product descriptions, images, and prices on your website are as accurate as possible, but that slight differences can occur — for example, colours may look different on different phone screens, or a handmade item may vary slightly from the photo. This is honest and reasonable, and it protects you from a customer claiming a dress is a different shade of blue than the photo showed.

State your prices clearly: all prices are in Nigerian naira (₦), whether they include VAT, and whether delivery is included or charged separately. If your prices change, state that the price at the time of ordering is the price that applies, and that you will not change the price of an order after it is confirmed.

If you sell items with sizes, quantities, or variations, state that customers must check the size or specification before ordering, and that you will supply the item as described.

## Step 4: Set Out Payment Terms

Payment is where most online disputes start, so this section must be precise.

List every payment method you accept: bank transfer to your stated account, card payment through Paystack or Flutterwave, Opay, Moniepoint, or cash on delivery where available. State clearly which methods you accept and which you do not. For example, if you do not accept cash on delivery outside Lagos, say so.

State when payment is due: in full before dispatch, unless you have agreed otherwise in writing. State that orders are processed only after payment is confirmed, and that bank transfer payments may take time to confirm. This manages expectations — a customer who transfers money at 9 pm should not expect dispatch at 9:05 pm.

If you offer instalment payments or pay-on-delivery, state the rules: how much is due upfront, when the balance is due, and what happens if the balance is not paid.

## Step 5: Cover Delivery Honestly

Delivery is the most complained-about part of Nigerian e-commerce, so this section earns its length. Be specific and be honest.

State your delivery areas: which cities and states you deliver to, and any areas you do not serve. State your delivery times as ranges, not promises: "Orders within Lagos are delivered in two to three working days; orders outside Lagos take three to seven working days." Never promise same-day delivery unless you can reliably do it. Overpromising on delivery is the fastest way to fill your inbox with angry messages.

State your delivery fees, or explain that fees are calculated at checkout based on location. If delivery is free above a certain order value, say so clearly — "Free delivery within Lagos on orders above ₦100,000" — and state what happens below that threshold.

## Step 6: Write a Fair Returns and Refunds Policy

This is the section customers look for most, and the one that causes the most trouble when it is missing. Be fair, be specific, and be realistic about what your business can afford.

Start with the principle: if an item is faulty, damaged, or not as described, the customer is entitled to a return, replacement, or refund. This is not generosity — it is what Nigerian consumer protection law expects. Write it clearly so customers trust you.

Then set the rules for other returns — change of mind, wrong size, or a gift that does not fit. Decide your window: seven days is common for Nigerian online businesses; fourteen days is more generous and builds more trust. State the conditions: the item must be unused, in its original packaging, with tags attached, and the customer must contact you within the window to start the return.

State how refunds are made and how long they take: "Refunds are made to the original payment method within five to seven working days of us receiving and inspecting the returned item." Be honest about the timeline — bank transfers in Nigeria are not always instant, and payment processors take time. A customer who knows to expect seven working days is patient; a customer who was promised 24 hours is angry on day two.

State the process: the customer contacts you by email or WhatsApp, you confirm the return, the customer sends the item back, you inspect it, and then you refund or replace. Give a contact channel for returns so customers are not guessing where to send the message.

## Step 7: Limit Your Liability — Fairly and Honestly

A liability clause states the limits of what you are responsible for. This is the section where bad terms go wrong, so write it carefully.

A fair liability clause says something like: "Our total liability for any claim arising from your purchase is limited to the amount you paid for the product or service in question. We are not liable for indirect losses, such as loss of profit, that arise from your use of our products."

Be specific about what you are not responsible for: delays caused by couriers or events beyond your control, minor differences between product photos and the real item, and misuse of a product against your instructions. That is reasonable, and customers accept it when it is written plainly.

## Step 8: Explain Changes, Disputes, and Governing Law

Finish the document with the practical closing clauses.

**Changes to the terms.** State that you may update the terms from time to time, that the updated version will be posted on your website with a new date, and that the version in force when a customer places an order applies to that order. If you make a major change — for example, changing your refund window — consider notifying customers by email or WhatsApp.

**Disputes.** State how disputes will be resolved. A good approach for a Nigerian small business is: first, the customer contacts you directly and you try to resolve it; if that fails, the matter may go to mediation; and if mediation fails, the courts in Lagos (or your state) will decide, under Nigerian law. Stating this upfront prevents a customer from threatening legal action in another country or demanding an unreasonable process.

**Severability.** Add a short clause that if any part of the terms is found unenforceable, the rest of the terms still apply. This is standard and protects the whole document if one clause is struck down.

## Your Checklist

- [ ] Business name, CAC number, address, email, and phone number are stated correctly
- [ ] Products and prices are described honestly, in naira, with VAT and delivery stated
- [ ] Payment methods are listed, including the warning to pay only to your official accounts
- [ ] Delivery areas, times, and fees are stated as realistic ranges
- [ ] Returns and refunds policy is fair, specific, and states who pays return delivery
- [ ] Cancellation rules and warranty terms are written down
- [ ] Liability is limited fairly — not excluded entirely
- [ ] Terms are governed by Nigerian law, with a clear dispute process
- [ ] A lawyer reviews the document within three to six months

Terms and conditions are not a formality. They are the written record of how your business treats its customers, and how it expects to be treated in return. Write them plainly, make them fair, publish them where customers can find them, and keep them updated as your business grows. A small business with clear terms looks professional, avoids most disputes before they start, and gives itself a fair defence when a dispute does happen. That is worth far more than the few hours it takes to write them.
