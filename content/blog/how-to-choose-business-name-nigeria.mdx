Think of the businesses you remember without trying: a food vendor whose name you can say in one breath, a fashion brand whose Instagram handle you typed from memory, a logistics company whose name tells you exactly what it does. Now think of the businesses you forgot the moment you walked away. The difference is rarely the quality of the product. It is often the name.

Your business name is the first thing a customer hears, the first thing they search for, and the thing they tell their friends. In Nigeria, where most business happens by word of mouth and WhatsApp, a good name travels on its own. A bad name — hard to spell, hard to pronounce, easy to confuse with someone else — slows everything down: every advert costs more, every referral gets lost, and every customer has to ask you to repeat it.

This guide shows you how to choose a business name that customers remember, that works on a shop sign, on Instagram, and on Google, and that will not cause you legal trouble at the Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC) later.

## Why Your Business Name Matters More Than You Think

A name does three jobs at once. First, it helps people find you. When someone hears your name on the radio, sees it on a flyer, or gets it from a friend, they need to be able to search for it and find you, not ten other businesses with similar names. Second, it tells people what you do, or at least hints at it. A name like "Lagos Fresh Foods" tells a customer what to expect before they even visit. Third, it creates a feeling. A name can sound premium, friendly, modern, or traditional, and that feeling shapes what customers expect to pay and how they expect to be treated.

In Nigeria, a name must survive real-world conditions: spoken over a noisy market, shouted across a street, typed on a small phone keyboard, written by hand on a receipt. If it is hard to spell after hearing it once, you will lose customers. If it sounds like another business, you will lose customers to them. If it is long, people will shorten it themselves — and the shortened version may not be the one you want.

Your name also affects your future: it goes on your CAC certificate, bank account, website, and social media pages. Changing it later is possible, but expensive and confusing — customers who knew the old name may not recognise the new one. Spend a few days getting it right now, rather than months fixing it later.

## Step 1: Start With What You Do and Who You Serve

Before you brainstorm a single name, write down three things: what you sell, who you sell it to, and what makes you different. These three answers are the raw material for a good name.

What you sell should be clear. If you sell hair, food, clothes, or logistics services, the name should point in that direction, even if it does not spell it out. A name like "Hair by Ada" is clear. A name like "Ada Ventures" tells nobody anything. You can use a vague name if you have a big advertising budget to explain it, but most small businesses do not, so clarity is an advantage.

Who you serve matters too. A business selling luxury wigs to working women in Lekki needs a different name from a business selling affordable foodstuffs to families in Onitsha. The first might use a name that sounds premium and modern. The second might use a name that sounds warm, local, and trustworthy. Think about your customers' language, their age, and what they value. A name that appeals to university students may not appeal to their parents, and both may be your customers.

What makes you different is the third ingredient. Are you the fastest? The cheapest? The most premium? The most local? The friendliest? A name can carry that difference. "Swift Deliveries" promises speed. "Mama's Kitchen" promises home cooking. "Prime Looks" promises quality. You do not need to pack everything into the name, but one clear idea is better than none.

Write these three answers on paper. They will guide every name you consider, and they will help you reject names that sound nice but say nothing about your business.

## Step 2: Brainstorm Widely Before You Judge Anything

Good naming starts with quantity. Set aside an hour, sit with a notebook or a phone, and write down at least fifty names. Do not judge them yet. Do not stop at the first one that sounds good. The first ten names you think of are usually the most obvious ones, and the obvious ones are often already taken. The better names usually appear after the twentieth or thirtieth idea, when your brain starts combining words in new ways.

Try different approaches. Combine your own name with what you do: "Ada's Cakes", "Chidi Logistics", "Tunde Tech". Combine a place with a product: "Lekki Prints", "Abuja Fresh", "Enugu Threads". Combine a feeling with a product: "Golden Grains", "Swift Styles", "Bright Bites". Try a short invented word, or a word from Igbo, Yoruba, or Hausa with a beautiful meaning — it can stand out and feel proudly Nigerian, as long as your customers can pronounce and spell it.

Ask other people to contribute — friends, family, and early customers will think of names you never would. But do not let a group vote decide for you: groups often pick the safest, most boring name. Use their ideas as raw material, and decide yourself, based on the tests in the next steps.

Write every idea down, even the silly ones — a silly idea can lead to a good one. "Mama Put Express" might sound funny, but it could become "Mama Express", which is clear, memorable, and warm. Do not filter too early.

## Step 3: Test Every Name for Memorability

Once you have a long list, start testing. The first test is the one-second test. Say the name out loud once, then ask yourself: could a stranger repeat it back to you? If you have to spell it out letter by letter, it will fail in the real world. Short names win here. One or two words are better than three or four. "Zuri Foods" is easier to remember than "Zuri African Foods and Catering Services Limited".

The second test is the phone test. Call a friend, say the name once, and ask them to type it into their phone without you spelling it. If they type it correctly, the name passes. If they type something else, your customers will have the same problem searching for you on Google or Instagram.

The third test is the meaning test. Does the name mean something good in the languages your customers speak? Check it in Igbo, Yoruba, and Hausa if you can. A word that sounds fine in English can mean something embarrassing in another language, and a quick check with friends who speak those languages can save you an expensive mistake.

The fourth test is the feeling test. Say the name as if you are answering the phone: "Hello, this is..." Does it sound professional? Does it sound like a business you would trust with your money? Does it match the prices you want to charge? A name that sounds cheap will make it hard to charge premium prices, and a name that sounds too fancy may scare away customers looking for affordable prices.

Keep only the names that pass all four tests. You should be left with five to ten strong options. That is the right number to take into the next step.

## Step 4: Check Availability Everywhere Before You Fall in Love

A name you love is useless if someone else is already using it. Check availability in four places before you commit.

First, check the Corporate Affairs Commission. In Nigeria, a business name must be available for registration before you can use it formally. You can search the CAC public search portal online. Type your proposed name and see whether an exact match or a very similar name already exists. Be careful: a name that is only slightly different from an existing registered name may still be rejected. For example, if "Golden Foods Limited" exists, "Golden Food Limited" may be refused because it is too similar. If your preferred name is taken, do not try to force it with a small spelling change. Pick another name from your shortlist. It is far cheaper to change a name now than to fight a rejection or a legal dispute later.

Second, check the domain name. Your website address should match your business name as closely as possible. Check whether yourname.com.ng or yourname.ng is available, using any domain registrar's search tool. A .com.ng domain is common for Nigerian businesses, while .ng is shorter and increasingly popular. If the exact domain is taken, consider a small addition, like adding "ng" or your city, but avoid long or confusing addresses. If someone else owns your exact domain, customers searching for you may land on their website instead.

Third, check social media. Search Instagram, Facebook, TikTok, and X for your proposed name. Your handle should match your business name, or come very close. If @yourname is taken on Instagram, you may end up with @yourname_ng or @yournameofficial, which is acceptable, but check that the existing account is not a competitor in the same business. If it is, customers will confuse the two, and you will lose business to them.

Fourth, check Google. Search your proposed name and see what comes up. If the first results are another business in the same field, or something negative, reconsider. You want a name that, when searched, leads to you.

Do these four checks for each of your top five names. The one that passes all four is your winner. If none passes all four, go back to your long list and test more names. Do not skip the checks. A beautiful name that you cannot register, cannot find online, or cannot claim on Instagram will cost you far more than the time these checks take.

## Step 5: Understand the Legal Basics

In Nigeria, you can trade under a business name, register it as a business name with the CAC, or register a limited company. Each option has different rules, and your name must fit the option you choose.

A business name registration is the simplest and cheapest option for a small business. It registers a sole proprietorship or a partnership under a name like "Ada's Cakes" or "Chidi Logistics Services". A limited company registration is more formal. It creates a separate legal entity, and the name must end with "Limited" or "Ltd". A limited company name also faces stricter checks: the CAC will reject names that are misleading, that suggest a connection with government, or that use restricted words like "National", "Federal", "Bank", or "Trust" without approval.

There are also words the CAC does not allow in any business name without special permission. Words that suggest you are a bank, an insurance company, a university, or a government body are restricted. Words that are offensive, or that are already the name of a well-known brand, will be refused. Before you pay for a name reservation, read the CAC guidelines or ask a professional to check your name. A name reservation fee is not refunded if the name is rejected, so check first and pay second.

Keep your records straight from the beginning. Once you register a name, use it consistently on your bank account, your receipts, your website, and your social media pages. Inconsistent names — "Ada's Cakes" on Instagram, "Ada Cakes Ventures" on your bank account, "Ada's Cake" on your flyer — confuse customers and can cause problems with banks and the CAC.

## Step 6: Test the Name With Real Customers

Before you print business cards or pay for a logo, test your favourite name with real people. Show it to ten people who resemble your target customers. Do not tell them it is your idea. Just ask: "What kind of business do you think this is? Would you buy from it? How would you spell it if you heard it on the radio?"

Their answers will tell you more than any amount of thinking. If most people guess the wrong business, the name is not clear enough. If most people cannot spell it, it will fail online. If people smile and say it sounds nice but cannot say what you sell, you may need to add a short descriptor, like a tagline, under the name. "Zuri — Fresh Foods, Lekki" is clearer than "Zuri" alone, and the tagline can change later without changing the name.

Test the name on a sign too: write it on paper and hold it up from a few metres away. Can you read it quickly? Now imagine it on a small phone screen, as an Instagram handle, and as a website address. A name that is unreadable on a phone will struggle in a country where most customers find businesses on their phones.

Also test the name over time: say it every day for a week. Does it still feel right? A name you love on Monday but dislike by Friday is not the one — the right name should feel better the more you use it.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

The first mistake is copying a famous brand. A name that sounds like a big company — "Nike Stores", "Apple Foods", "Dangote This or That" — will get you attention, but the wrong kind. It can bring legal trouble, and customers will see you as a copy, not a real business. Build your own name.

The second mistake is choosing a name that limits your future. A name like "Lagos Phone Accessories" is fine if you only ever sell phone accessories in Lagos. But if you later want to sell laptops, or open in Abuja, the name becomes a cage. Pick a name that gives you room to grow. If you are not sure what you will sell in five years, choose a broader name.

The third mistake is using a name that is hard to pronounce in your market. A French or Latin word may sound classy, but if your customers in Onitsha or Kano cannot say it, they will not recommend you to their friends. Your customers should be able to say your name easily, in their own accent.

The fourth mistake is choosing a name just because the domain or handle was available. Availability matters, but it is not the only test. A name that is available everywhere because it is meaningless is not a good name. It must also pass the memory, spelling, and feeling tests.

The fifth mistake is rushing. A business name is a decision you will live with for years. Give it a few days. Sleep on your shortlist. Talk to people you trust. The right name is worth the wait.

## Your Final Checklist

Before you commit, run through this list. Can a stranger repeat the name after hearing it once? Can they spell it correctly? Does it tell people what you do, or at least hint at it? Is it available at the CAC? Is the domain available? Are the social media handles available? Does it mean something good in the languages your customers speak? Does it sound professional when you answer the phone with it? Will it still fit if your business grows or adds new products? If you can answer yes to all of these, you have found your name.

Once you choose, protect it. Register it with the CAC as soon as you can, buy the domain name even if you are not ready to build a website, and claim the Instagram, Facebook, and TikTok handles even if you will not post for a while. These steps cost little, and they stop someone else from taking the name you worked hard to choose.

Then use the name everywhere, consistently. The same spelling, the same capitalisation, the same handle, on your shop sign, your receipts, your WhatsApp profile, your Instagram bio, and your website. Consistency is what turns a name into a brand. Every time a customer sees the same name in the same form, it becomes a little more familiar, a little more trusted, and a little harder to forget.

Your business name is not just a label. It is the first promise you make to your customers, and the one they will repeat to their friends. Choose it with care, test it honestly, check it properly, and then build a business that makes the name worth remembering.
