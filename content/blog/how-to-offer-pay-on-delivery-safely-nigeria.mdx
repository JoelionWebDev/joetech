Ask any Nigerian online seller what their customers want, and pay on delivery will be near the top of the list. Many buyers have been scammed before, or they know someone who has. They have paid for goods that never arrived, or received something completely different from the photo. So when a vendor says "pay before delivery," a large number of honest customers simply walk away.

Pay on delivery — also called cash on delivery, or COD — removes that fear. The customer only pays when the product is in their hands. For the seller, it can mean far more orders, especially from first-time buyers. But it also brings real risks: customers who refuse the order when the rider arrives, fake addresses, riders who disappear with the money, and the cost of sending goods out and getting them back unsold.

This guide shows you how to offer pay on delivery safely in Nigeria: how to verify orders before dispatch, how to work with riders and logistics companies, how to handle payment, and how to set rules that protect your business without scaring away good customers.

## Why Nigerians Prefer Pay on Delivery

To offer pay on delivery well, you need to understand why customers ask for it. It is not stubbornness. It is self-protection, learned from experience.

First, trust is low. Social media is full of vendors who take payment and vanish, or who send a cheap imitation of the advertised product. A first-time customer has no reason to trust you yet, and pay on delivery is their way of managing that risk.

Second, many Nigerians are uncomfortable with online payments. Some do not have a debit card they trust for online use. Others have had a failed transfer or a debited-but-not-delivered experience with a bank app. Paying cash or transferring on the spot, after seeing the product, feels safer.

Third, it is simply how many people are used to buying. In a physical market in Lagos or Onitsha, you inspect the goods, agree the price, and pay. Pay on delivery recreates that familiar experience online.

The opportunity for sellers is clear. If your competitors insist on payment before delivery and you offer a safe pay-on-delivery option, you will win customers they lose. The challenge is doing it without losing money on failed deliveries. ## The Real Costs and Risks

Before you offer pay on delivery, count the costs honestly. Every failed delivery costs you money, even though no sale happened.

A typical failed delivery in Lagos might cost you ₦2,000 to ₦4,000 in dispatch fees for the round trip. If the product is a ₦25,000 dress and the customer refuses it, you have lost the dispatch fee, the rider's time, and the hours you spent packing and confirming the order. If it happens ten times in a month, you have lost ₦20,000 to ₦40,000 — money that could have gone into stock or adverts.

The main risks are:

- **Order refusal.** The customer is not at home, changes their mind, or says the product is not what they expected.
- **Unreachable customers.** The phone number does not go through, the address is incomplete, or the customer stops replying after placing the order.
- **Fake orders.** Competitors or pranksters place orders with no intention of paying, wasting your dispatch money.
- **Rider problems.** A rider who collects cash and delays remitting it, or who damages the product in transit.
- **Returns and exchanges.** The customer pays, then wants to return the item, and you must collect it and refund them.

None of these risks means you should avoid pay on delivery. They mean you need rules, verification steps, and the right logistics partners — which is exactly what follows.

## Step 1: Set Clear Rules and Publish Them

Your pay-on-delivery policy should be written down and visible before a customer orders — on your Instagram highlights, your WhatsApp Business catalogue, and your website. When the rules are clear from the start, disputes become rare.

Your policy should state:

1. **Where you deliver.** List the areas you cover for pay on delivery — for example, Lagos mainland and island, Abuja, and Port Harcourt — and say clearly where you do not offer it.
2. **The delivery fee.** State the fee upfront, and whether the customer pays it to the rider or it is added to the product price. Never surprise a customer with a fee at the door.
3. **How long delivery takes.** Give a realistic range, such as 24 to 48 hours in Lagos and 2 to 4 days outside Lagos, and explain what happens if there is a delay.
4. **Inspection rules.** Can the customer open the package before paying? Most sellers allow a quick visual check but not wearing, testing, or using the product before payment. State your rule clearly.
5. **What happens if they refuse.** State your refusal policy in plain language — for example, that a customer who refuses an order without a valid reason may need to pay a delivery commitment fee on their next order, or pay before delivery next time.
6. **Returns.** Explain how returns work: the time window, the condition the product must be in, and who pays the return delivery fee.

Write the policy in simple English, not legal language. A customer who understands the rules is far more likely to follow them.

## Step 2: Verify Every Order Before You Dispatch

Verification is the single most effective way to cut failed deliveries. Never send out a pay-on-delivery order that you have not confirmed. It takes five minutes, and it can save you thousands of naira.

Confirm every order with these steps:

1. **Call the customer.** Do not rely on WhatsApp messages alone. A short phone call confirms the number is real, the person is serious, and the address is correct. If the number does not go through after two attempts at different times of day, do not dispatch.
2. **Confirm the full address.** Ask for the house number, street, area, and a nearby landmark. "Lekki" is not an address. "14B Admiralty Way, Lekki Phase 1, near the Chicken Republic junction" is an address. Ask for the local government area too, so your rider can plan the route.
3. **Confirm the product, size, colour, and price.** Read the order back to the customer, including the total amount they will pay the rider. Many refusals happen because the customer expected a different size or a lower price.
4. **Confirm availability.** Tell the customer the exact time window for delivery, and ask whether someone will be available to receive it. If the customer works in an office in Victoria Island and is only home after 7pm, schedule accordingly.
5. **Send a written confirmation.** After the call, send a WhatsApp message summarising the order, the total amount, the delivery fee, and the expected delivery day. Ask the customer to reply "Confirmed." That message is your record if there is a dispute later.

For high-value orders — say, above ₦50,000 — consider asking for a small commitment fee, such as ₦2,000 or ₦5,000, paid in advance by transfer. Explain honestly that it covers the dispatch cost if the customer refuses the order, and that it is deducted from the total on delivery. Many serious buyers accept this readily, and it filters out almost all fake orders. If a customer refuses to pay any commitment fee on a high-value order, that is useful information — proceed with caution, or ask for full payment before delivery instead.

## Step 3: Choose Reliable Riders and Logistics Partners

Your delivery partner is the face of your business at the customer's door. A rude rider, a late delivery, or a damaged parcel can cost you a customer and a bad review. Choose partners carefully.

For deliveries within Lagos and Abuja, you have three main options:

- **Your own rider.** Hiring a rider full-time or part-time gives you control, but it comes with salary, fuel, and bike maintenance costs. It makes sense once your daily order volume is steady, typically 10 or more deliveries a day.
- **Dispatch companies.** Companies like GIG Logistics, Kwik Delivery, and other Lagos-based dispatch services handle pickup and delivery for a fee, and many support pay-on-delivery collection. Compare their rates, their coverage areas, and how quickly they remit collected cash to you.
- **Bike-hailing and courier apps.** Apps that connect you with riders on demand are flexible for small volumes. Check the rider's rating before confirming, and share the tracking details with your customer.

Whichever option you choose, agree these points in advance:

- How much the delivery costs per area, and who pays for a failed delivery.
- How and when collected cash is remitted to you — same day, next day, or weekly — and into which account.
- What proof of delivery you receive: a photo, a signature, or a delivery confirmation message.
- What happens if a product is damaged in transit, and who bears the cost.
- How the rider identifies themselves to the customer, so the customer knows the delivery is genuine.

Start with one or two partners and build the relationship. A reliable rider who knows your products and your customers is worth far more than the cheapest quote from a stranger.

## Step 4: Handle the Money Safely

The moment cash changes hands is the moment your business is most exposed. Set up a clear system for it.

First, decide how the customer pays the rider. Cash is common, but it creates the most risk — the rider carries money, and you must trust the rider to remit it. Bank transfer to your account, done by the customer in front of the rider, is often safer: the rider confirms the transfer alert with you by phone before leaving, and the money goes straight to you. POS machines carried by riders are another option, though network failures can cause delays at the door.

Whatever method you use, the rule is simple: the rider does not leave until payment is confirmed. Give your rider a direct line to you, so they can call and confirm a transfer alert in seconds.

Second, keep records. For every pay-on-delivery order, record the customer's name, phone number, address, product, amount, rider, and payment status in a simple sheet or notebook. When a customer claims they paid and you have no record, you need to be able to check quickly.

Third, reconcile daily. At the end of each day, match the deliveries made against the money received. If a rider collected ₦80,000 across four deliveries, your account should show ₦80,000. Do this every day, not at the end of the week — small gaps become big losses when they are left unchecked.

Finally, keep your business money separate from your personal money. Use a dedicated business account for all collections. It makes reconciliation easier, and it gives you a clean record of what the business actually earns.

## Step 5: Reduce Refusals With Honest Product Information

Most refusals are not caused by dishonest customers. They are caused by a gap between what the customer expected and what arrived. Close that gap before the order is placed.

Show honest photos and videos of the actual product, not a stock photo from the internet. If you sell fabrics, show the real colour in natural light. If you sell shoes, show the sole, the inside, and the true size. Record a short video of the product in your hand, so the customer can judge the size and quality.

State measurements clearly: length, width, size in centimetres, and the equivalent Nigerian or UK size. State what is included and what is not. If a phone comes without a charger, say so. If a dress is a free size that fits up to a certain measurement, say so.

Be honest about delivery time. If an item will take four days to reach Enugu, say four days. A customer who waits four days knowingly is patient. A customer promised "tomorrow" who waits four days is angry — and angry customers refuse deliveries.

Answer questions before the order is confirmed. If a customer asks whether a wig is human hair or synthetic, answer honestly. A lost sale today is cheaper than a refused delivery tomorrow, and honesty is what turns a one-time buyer into a repeat customer.

## Step 6: Handle Refusals, Returns, and Difficult Customers

Even with perfect verification, some orders will be refused. How you handle those moments matters more than the refusal itself.

When a rider reports a refusal, call the customer yourself, calmly. Ask what went wrong. Sometimes the reason is fixable: the customer was not at home, the rider went to the wrong address, or the customer misunderstood the price. Offer to redeliver at a better time, or clarify the confusion. A calm conversation saves many sales.

If the product is genuinely wrong — the wrong size, colour, or a damaged item — accept the return gracefully and offer a replacement or a refund. That customer may still buy from you again, and they may tell friends that you handled the problem well.

If a customer refuses repeatedly without a valid reason, or places orders and never answers the phone, keep a simple record and apply your policy: their next order requires a commitment fee or full payment before delivery. You do not need to argue or blacklist them publicly. A quiet, consistent policy is enough.

For returns after payment, follow the policy you published. Inspect the returned product before refunding, keep the customer informed at each step, and process the refund promptly. A slow refund is one of the fastest ways to lose a customer permanently and earn a damaging review.

## Step 7: Know When Pay on Delivery Is Not Worth It

Pay on delivery is not right for every product or every location. Knowing when to say no protects your business.

Consider not offering it when:

- **The order value is very high.** For items above ₦150,000 to ₦200,000, such as laptops or premium phones, the risk of refusal or rider theft is high. Ask for a bank transfer or a substantial deposit instead.
- **The delivery location is far or hard to reach.** If the address is in a remote area with poor roads and no reliable rider coverage, the delivery cost may exceed your profit. Offer payment before delivery, or ask the customer to pick up from a nearby pickup point.
- **The product is custom-made or perishable.** A custom cake, a tailored outfit, or fresh food cannot easily be resold if the customer refuses. For these, a deposit or full payment in advance is standard practice, and most customers understand it.
- **The customer has a history of failed orders.** Your records will show you who the repeat offenders are. It is good business to require payment upfront from them.

Saying no to pay on delivery in these cases is not bad customer service. It is honest business, and most serious customers will accept a clear explanation and an alternative, such as a secure payment link through Paystack or Flutterwave, or a bank transfer with a receipt.

## Getting Started This Week

You do not need to rebuild your business to offer pay on delivery safely. Start with these five actions this week:

1. Write down your pay-on-delivery policy, including the areas you cover, the fees, and the refusal rules. Publish it on your WhatsApp Business profile and your Instagram highlights.
2. Create a simple order confirmation checklist — call, confirm address, confirm price, send a written summary — and use it for every pay-on-delivery order.
3. Talk to two dispatch companies or riders, compare their rates and remittance times, and pick one to start with.
4. Set up a daily reconciliation routine: every evening, match deliveries against money received.
5. Review your product photos and descriptions, and fix the ones that could mislead a customer.

After a month, check your numbers: how many pay-on-delivery orders you received, how many were delivered successfully, how many were refused, and how much the failed ones cost you. If your success rate is above 85 percent, the system is working. If it is lower, look at your verification step first — that is where most failures begin.

Pay on delivery is a trust-building tool. Used well, it turns sceptical first-time buyers into loyal customers who eventually pay in advance without hesitation, because they have learned they can trust you. Offer it carefully, verify every order, work with riders you trust, and keep your records clean — and it will grow your sales instead of draining them.
