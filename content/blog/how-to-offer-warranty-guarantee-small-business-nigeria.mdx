# How to Offer a Warranty or Guarantee That Builds Trust and Wins Sales

A customer in Lekki is looking at two identical phone accessories on Instagram. One seller says "DM to buy." The other says "6-month warranty. If it stops working, bring it back and we replace it free." Which one gets the order? Almost every time, it is the second seller — even if the price is slightly higher.

That is the power of a warranty or guarantee. In Nigeria, where many buyers have been disappointed by a product that stopped working a week after payment, or a service that never matched the advert, trust is the scarcest currency in business. A clear promise that you will stand behind what you sell removes the biggest objection in a customer's mind: "What if this does not work?"

Yet many small business owners in Nigeria avoid offering guarantees. They worry that customers will abuse them, that refunds will eat their profit, or that a warranty will create arguments they cannot win. Those fears are real, but they are manageable. A badly designed guarantee can hurt you. A well-designed one is one of the cheapest marketing tools you will ever use, because it turns hesitant browsers into buyers and buyers into people who recommend you.

This guide shows you how to design a warranty or money-back guarantee that fits a small Nigerian business, how to price it into your products, how to write it in plain language, and how to handle claims without drama.

## Why a Guarantee Works So Well in Nigeria

Nigerian customers are careful with their money, and for good reason. Many have bought a "new" phone that turned out to be refurbished, a blender that burnt out in a month, or a wig that shed after two washes. Online shopping adds another layer of doubt: the buyer cannot touch the product before paying, and stories of vendors who disappear after receiving payment are common.

A guarantee changes the risk equation. Without one, the customer carries all the risk. With one, you share it. That single shift makes people more willing to try a new brand, pay a fair price, and order a larger quantity.

Consider a small electronics shop in Computer Village, Ikeja. Two shops sell the same power bank for ₦25,000. Shop A offers no warranty. Shop B offers a three-month replacement warranty and writes it on the receipt. Shop B will sell more units, even at ₦27,000, because customers are not really buying a power bank — they are buying the confidence that their ₦27,000 will not be wasted. The extra ₦2,000 is cheap insurance in the customer's mind, and the shop's actual cost of honouring the occasional faulty unit is far less than the extra sales the promise brings in.

## Warranty vs Guarantee: Know the Difference

People use the two words interchangeably, but they are not the same thing, and your customers deserve to know exactly what you are promising.

A **warranty** is a promise about the product itself. It says the item will work as described for a stated period, and if it fails through no fault of the buyer, you will repair it, replace it, or refund it. Warranties are common for electronics, appliances, furniture, phones, laptops, solar systems, generators, and vehicles.

A **guarantee** is broader. It is a promise about the outcome or the customer's satisfaction. A money-back guarantee says: if you are not satisfied within a stated period, you can return the product and get your money back. A service guarantee says: if the work is not done to the agreed standard, we will fix it free or refund you. Guarantees are common for food, cosmetics, clothing, training, consulting, cleaning, and digital products such as courses and templates.

The key is to name what you are offering clearly. Do not write "guaranteed" on a flyer without saying guaranteed against what, for how long, and what happens if it fails. Vague promises create arguments. Specific promises create trust.

## The Main Types of Guarantee You Can Offer

You do not need to offer everything. Pick the type that fits your product, your margin, and your ability to deliver.

**Money-back guarantee.** The customer gets a full refund if they return the product within a stated period, usually 7, 14, or 30 days. This is the strongest trust builder, and the most feared by sellers. In practice, return rates for honest businesses are low — typically 2 to 5 percent — because most customers who buy in good faith keep products that work as described. Money-back guarantees work best for cosmetics, skincare, supplements, food products, clothing, and digital products such as courses and templates. Set a clear condition: the product must be returned in usable condition, or with a stated percentage unused. For a skincare seller in Surulere, a fair rule is "return within 14 days with at least 70 percent of the product remaining."

**Replacement warranty.** If the product fails within the warranty period, you replace it with the same item or one of equal value. This is the most common warranty for electronics, phone accessories, small appliances, and gadgets in Nigeria. Customers love it because it is simple: faulty item in, working item out. Replacement warranties are easy to manage if you keep a small stock of replacements. If you sell 100 Bluetooth speakers a month and 3 percent fail, you need only 3 replacement units in reserve. Build that cost into your price, as explained below.

**Repair warranty.** Instead of replacing the item, you repair it free of charge within the warranty period. This suits laptops, generators, inverters, air conditioners, and furniture. Be clear about turnaround time. "We will repair it within 5 working days" is a promise you can keep. "We will fix it soon" is not. If you cannot repair in-house, partner with a technician in your area and agree on a fixed repair fee in advance so a warranty claim does not become a loss.

**Service guarantee.** For services — cleaning, catering, photography, web design, tailoring, consulting, training — the guarantee is that the work will meet the agreed standard. If it does not, you will redo it free, or refund a stated portion. A cleaning company in Lekki might promise: "If you are not satisfied with any room, tell us within 24 hours and we will re-clean it free." A web designer might promise two rounds of free revisions and a fix for any bug within 30 days of delivery. Service guarantees should always be tied to a written scope agreed before work begins.

## How to Design a Guarantee You Can Afford

The number one mistake small businesses make is copying a big company's guarantee without doing the maths. A multinational can absorb a 10 percent return rate. A small business in Onitsha cannot. Your guarantee must be generous enough to build trust and careful enough to protect your profit. Follow these five steps.

**Step 1: Know your failure rate.** Look at your past sales. Out of every 100 units sold, how many came back faulty or drew a complaint? If you do not have records, start tracking today. A simple notebook or spreadsheet with date, product, customer, fault, and outcome is enough. Typical failure rates for honest sellers are low: 1 to 3 percent for quality electronics, 2 to 5 percent for fashion items with sizing issues, under 2 percent for furniture. If your failure rate is above 10 percent, the problem is not your guarantee — it is your supplier or your quality control. Fix the product first.

**Step 2: Price the guarantee into your product.** A guarantee is not free. Build its cost into your selling price, quietly and honestly. The formula is simple: expected warranty cost per unit equals your failure rate multiplied by your cost of honouring a claim. For example, you sell a blender that costs you ₦18,000 and sells for ₦28,000. Your failure rate is 3 percent, and a replacement costs you ₦18,000 plus ₦2,000 delivery. Expected warranty cost per unit is 3 percent of ₦20,000, which is ₦600. Adding ₦600 to your price is invisible to the customer, but it means the guarantee pays for itself. Even adding ₦1,000 for safety leaves you with a healthy margin and a promise you can keep.

**Step 3: Set a fair time limit.** Longer is not always better. A warranty should cover the period when genuine faults show up, not the entire life of the product. For most electronics, faults appear in the first 30 to 90 days. A 3-month warranty therefore covers the real risk. Match the period to the product's price and expected life. A ₦150,000 inverter deserves a longer warranty than a ₦6,000 phone charger. Customers understand this instinctively. What they do not forgive is a warranty so short it feels like a trick — "7-day warranty" on a television, for instance, tells the buyer you do not trust your own product.

**Step 4: Define what is covered and what is not.** This is where most warranty arguments start. Write down, in plain language, what is covered — manufacturing defects, faulty parts, failure under normal use, stitching that comes undone, a zip that breaks, a battery that will not hold charge — and what is not — physical damage from dropping or water, misuse, unauthorised repairs, normal wear and tear, or damage from power surges where a stabiliser was not used. Be specific about power issues, because they matter in Nigeria. If you sell electronics, state whether the warranty covers damage from voltage fluctuation. Many sellers exclude it, and that is fair — but say so upfront, and recommend a stabiliser or surge protector at the point of sale.

**Step 5: Decide the remedy in advance.** For each type of fault, decide in advance what happens: repair first, replace if repair fails, refund if replacement is impossible. Write this into your policy. When a claim arrives, you follow the policy instead of negotiating under pressure. A good default order is repair, then replace, then refund. This protects you from customers who demand an instant refund for a fault that a 20-minute repair would fix, while still giving the customer a fair outcome.

## Writing Your Warranty Policy in Plain Language

Your warranty policy is a document your customers will actually read only when something goes wrong. That is exactly when it must be clear. Write it the way you would explain it to a customer standing in front of you.

A good policy answers six questions: What product or service does it cover? How long does the cover last, and when does it start — from the date of purchase or delivery? What exactly is covered, and what is excluded? What will you do if something goes wrong — repair, replace, or refund? How does the customer make a claim — bring the item to the shop, send a WhatsApp message, fill a form, call a number? What does the customer need — receipt, order number, photos of the fault?

Put this policy where customers can find it before they buy: on your website's product pages, on the receipt, on a card inside the packaging, and as a pinned post or highlight on Instagram and WhatsApp Business. A warranty that only appears after a complaint feels like an excuse. A warranty that is visible before purchase is a selling point.

Here is a short example you can adapt:

> **Our Warranty:** Every item in this shop carries a 3-month replacement warranty from the date of purchase. If your item develops a manufacturing fault under normal use, bring it back with your receipt and we will replace it free of charge. The warranty does not cover physical damage, water damage, or damage from power surges. To make a claim, send a WhatsApp message to 0800 000 0000 with a photo of the fault and your receipt.

That is about 80 words, and it answers all six questions. Keep the tone friendly. A policy that reads like a threat — full of "we are not liable" and "the customer must" — undoes the trust the guarantee was meant to build. Use "we will" more than "you must."

## Handling Claims Without Losing the Customer — or Your Profit

The moment a customer returns with a faulty product is the moment your guarantee is tested. Handle it well and you gain a loyal customer who tells friends about you. Handle it badly and you lose that customer and everyone they warn.

**Listen first, judge later.** When a customer brings a complaint, let them finish speaking before you respond. Do not start with "Did you drop it?" or "That is not covered." Start with "I am sorry this happened. Let me look at it." A customer who feels heard is far more reasonable than one who feels accused. Ask simple questions: When did it stop working? What happened just before it failed? Did anything unusual occur — a fall, water, a power cut?

**Inspect and decide quickly.** Inspect the item in front of the customer where possible, or within 24 hours if it needs testing. A slow decision feels like a delay tactic. If the fault is clearly a manufacturing defect — a phone that will not charge from day one, a shoe whose sole separates after two weeks — approve the claim on the spot. If the fault looks like physical damage, explain what you can see, calmly and with evidence. Show the customer the crack, the water mark, or the bent pin. Most reasonable people accept a fair explanation when they can see the evidence themselves.

**Honour the promise you made.** If the claim is covered, do what your policy says, promptly and without argument. Replace the item, repair it within the stated time, or refund the money. Do not offer a lesser remedy than the one you advertised. Keep a small reserve of replacement stock for your fastest-moving products so a valid claim never waits for your next supplier trip. For services, keep a slot in your weekly schedule for rework so a guarantee job does not displace paying customers.

**Record every claim.** Write down every claim, valid or not: date, customer, product, fault, decision, and cost. This record is gold. After three months, you will see patterns — a particular supplier's chargers failing, a particular shoe size tearing, a particular service step being missed. Fix the pattern and your claims fall. The record also protects you. If the same customer returns the same product three times with a different story each time, your records show the pattern and support a polite refusal.

## A Simple Plan to Launch Your Guarantee This Week

You can put a guarantee in place in seven days without spending much money. Day 1: check your failure rate from your last three months of sales and complaints. Day 2: choose your guarantee type and period, starting with your best-selling product. Day 3: do the maths, price the expected cost into your product, and confirm your margin still works. Day 4: write the policy in plain language, answering the six questions above, in under 200 words. Day 5: print it on your receipts, add it to your product pages and your WhatsApp Business catalogue, and brief your staff. Day 6: tell your existing customers by posting it on your WhatsApp status, Instagram, and Facebook page. Day 7: start tracking claims in your notebook or spreadsheet, and review them monthly.

Start with your best-selling product only. Once that guarantee runs smoothly for a month, extend it to the rest of your range. A small, well-kept promise beats a grand promise that collapses.

## Final Word

A warranty is not a cost. It is a statement of confidence in your product and respect for your customer. In a market where trust is hard to earn and easy to lose, that statement is worth more than a discount. Start small, price it honestly, and honour it promptly. Your customers will notice. They will buy more, complain less, and tell their friends. That is how a small business in Lagos, Abuja, Port Harcourt, or Onitsha grows — one kept promise at a time.
