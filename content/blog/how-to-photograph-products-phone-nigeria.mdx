Your product photo is your shop window. On Instagram, WhatsApp, Jumia, or your own website, a customer cannot touch your dress, taste your food, or test your phone. The photo is all they have. If it is dark, blurry, or cluttered, they scroll past — even if your product is better and cheaper than the next seller's. If it is clear, bright, and honest, they stop, they zoom in, and they ask for the price.

The good news is that you do not need a professional camera or a studio to take photos that sell. The phone in your pocket — even a mid-range Tecno, Infinix, Samsung, or second-hand iPhone — is good enough, if you use light, background, and angles properly. This guide shows you how, with Nigerian examples, low-cost setups you can build at home in Lekki, Abuja, or Onitsha, and free editing apps that work on MTN or Airtel data.

## Start with Light: The One Thing That Matters Most

If you remember only one thing from this guide, remember this: light matters more than your phone. A ₦500,000 camera in a dark room takes worse photos than a ₦150,000 phone beside a bright window. Most bad product photos in Nigeria are not bad because of the phone. They are bad because they were taken at night under a yellow bulb, or in a dark shop with mixed light.

Natural daylight is your best and cheapest light source. Photograph near a large window, on a balcony, or outside in open shade — for example, under a veranda or a tree where the sun is bright but not shining directly on your product. Direct midday sun in Lagos is harsh: it creates hard shadows, washes out colours, and makes white products look blown out. Morning light between 8am and 10am, and late afternoon light between 4pm and 5:30pm, are softer and warmer, and they make most products look their best.

If you must shoot indoors, turn off the yellow ceiling bulb and use daylight from the window only. Mixed light — yellow from the bulb plus blue-white from the window — makes colours look wrong, and your red dress will look orange in the photo. If your room has no good window, take the product outside. A clean table on a balcony, a white sheet spread on the ground in open shade, or even the bonnet of a clean car can work as a shooting spot.

When you need artificial light, a simple ring light is the best first investment: a basic 10-inch one costs roughly ₦15,000 to ₦35,000 in Computer Village or on Jumia, and a larger 18-inch one roughly ₦40,000 to ₦80,000. Buy one with adjustable brightness and colour temperature. If that is out of reach, two white LED bulbs in desk lamps, placed on either side of your product, will do a decent job.

Always clean your lens before you shoot. Wipe it gently with a soft cloth or the inside of your shirt. A fingerprint on the lens is the most common reason phone photos look hazy, and it takes two seconds to fix.

## Set Up a Clean Background That Makes Your Product Stand Out

A busy background kills a good product photo. Customers should see your product first, not your bed, your shop clutter, or the tiles on your floor. A clean background also makes your photos look consistent, so your Instagram page or WhatsApp catalogue looks like a real brand, not a random collection of snapshots.

The cheapest good background is a plain white or light-grey surface. A large sheet of white cardboard, called a Bristol board, costs a few hundred naira from a stationery shop in Lagos or Abuja, and it is enough for shoes, bags, cosmetics, and small electronics. Place it on a table against a wall, curve it gently so there is no hard line where the table meets the wall, and put your product on it. That curve — photographers call it a sweep — makes the background look endless and professional.

For larger products such as clothes on a hanger, a plain wall works well. Find a clean white or light-coloured wall, remove anything hanging on it, and hang your product at eye level. For food, a wooden table, a clean tray, or a plain linen cloth can look warm and appetising, as long as the surface is clean and the colours do not fight with the food. A plate of jollof rice looks better on a plain white plate than on a busy patterned one.

Choose a background colour that contrasts with your product. A black bag disappears on a black background. A white shoe disappears on a white background unless you add a shadow or a coloured surface underneath. If your product is dark, use a light background. If it is light, use a mid-grey or soft pastel background. Avoid bright red or neon backgrounds, which distract from the product and make colours look wrong.

Keep props minimal. One relevant prop can add context — a sprig of leaves next to a skincare bottle, a cup of coffee next to a pastry, a watch next to a shirt. Five props become clutter. The product must always be the biggest, sharpest thing in the frame. If a customer has to search for the product in your photo, the photo has failed.

## Phone Settings: Get the Basics Right Before You Tap

You do not need to understand manual camera settings to take good product photos, but three quick settings will improve almost every shot.

First, turn on the grid lines in your camera app. On most Android phones, open the camera, tap Settings, and turn on "Grid lines" or "Composition lines". On iPhone, go to Settings, then Camera, and turn on Grid. The grid divides your screen into nine squares. Place your product on one of the crossing points, not dead centre, and your photos will look more balanced and professional. This is the rule of thirds, and it works for almost every product.

Second, tap the product on your screen to focus on it, then slide the brightness control down slightly if the photo looks too bright. Most phone cameras overexpose white products and light skin tones. Tapping to focus also tells the phone what to expose for, so the product is sharp and correctly lit, even if the background is slightly darker.

Third, use the rear camera, not the selfie camera. The rear camera is sharper, has better colour, and handles low light better. Use a small phone tripod or a stack of books to hold the phone steady, and use the timer — two or three seconds — so you are not shaking the phone when you tap the shutter. A tripod costs roughly ₦5,000 to ₦15,000, and it is worth it if you photograph products every day. If you do not have one, rest your elbows on the table and hold your breath as you tap.

Shoot in the highest quality your phone allows, and do not use digital zoom — walk closer instead, or crop later in an editing app. Use normal photo mode, not portrait mode, unless you are photographing a person wearing the product, as portrait mode can blur the edges of your product.

Take many photos. Professional photographers take dozens of shots to get five good ones. Take at least ten photos of each product, from different angles and distances, and pick the best three. Storage is cheap. A missed sale because you only took one blurry photo is expensive.

## Angles and Shots: Show Customers What They Need to See

Customers buy with their eyes, but they also buy to avoid disappointment. A single front photo is not enough. They want to see the product from the angles that answer their questions: What does it look like from the side? How big is it really? What is the texture? What is inside?

For most products, take these five shots as a minimum. First, a straight-on hero shot: the product centred, well lit, on a clean background. This is the photo you use as your main listing image. Second, a 45-degree angle shot, which shows the front and the side at once and gives a sense of depth. Third, a close-up detail shot: the stitching on a bag, the label on a bottle, the texture of a fabric, the screen of a phone switched on. Fourth, a scale shot: the product next to a common object, such as a hand, a phone, or a bottle of water, so customers can judge the size. Fifth, a lifestyle or in-use shot: the dress worn by a model, the food served on a table, the phone in a hand. The lifestyle shot is the one that makes a customer imagine owning the product.

For clothes, photograph the item flat or on a hanger first, then on a person if you can — a hanger shows the shape, a person shows the fit. Ask a friend or use a mannequin if you cannot hire a model, keep the background clean, and never photograph clothes on a bed or floor, which makes even an expensive item look second-hand.

For food, photograph at 45 degrees, garnish lightly, and shoot as soon as it is ready, while it is still steaming or glossy. Cold, dull food photographs badly, no matter how good it tastes.

For jewellery and small items, get close: a ring photographed from a metre away looks like a dot, while a close-up in good light shows the stone and the metal. Use tap-to-focus, hold the phone steady, and take several shots.

## Editing on Your Phone: Make Good Photos Look Great

Editing is not cheating. It is the digital version of good lighting. A light edit — brighter, sharper, truer colours — makes a good photo look professional. A heavy edit — oversaturated colours, fake backgrounds, smoothed skin — makes a product look different from what the customer will receive, and that leads to complaints and returns.

Start with the free editing tools already on your phone. Google Photos and the iPhone Photos app both let you adjust brightness, contrast, warmth, and sharpness. Increase brightness slightly if the photo is dark, increase contrast slightly if it looks flat, and increase sharpness slightly if the details are soft. Do not push any slider to the maximum. Small changes are enough.

For more control, try a free app such as Snapseed, which is free on Android and iPhone and works offline once downloaded. Snapseed lets you adjust specific areas, whiten a background, and crop precisely. Canva, also free, is useful for adding your price, your business name, or a clean border to a photo for Instagram or WhatsApp Status — but keep text minimal. One line with the price is enough. A photo covered in text looks like an advert, not a product.

Follow a consistent editing style for all your products. Use the same brightness, the same background, and the same crop size, so your catalogue looks like it belongs to one brand. Save your favourite settings as a preset if your app allows it, or write down the numbers — for example, brightness plus 10, contrast plus 5, warmth minus 5 — and apply the same values to every photo. Consistency builds trust, because customers learn that what they see is what they get.

Never edit a photo to hide a flaw. Do not smooth over a scratch on a phone, whiten a stain on a dress, or change the colour of a fabric. If the product has a defect, photograph it honestly and mention it in the description, with a lower price if appropriate. An honest photo that sells a used item at the right price is better than a perfect photo that brings an angry customer to your door.

## Organising Your Photos and Posting Them Where They Sell

Good photos are an asset. Treat them like one. Create a folder on your phone for each product, with the original photos and the edited versions saved separately. Name your files clearly, such as "ankara-dress-red-front.jpg", so you can find them again in a month when a customer asks for the same item. Back up your best photos to Google Photos or Google Drive, so you do not lose them if your phone is stolen or damaged.

When you post, match the photo to the platform. On Instagram, use square or portrait photos that fill the screen, and post your best hero shot first — the first photo in a carousel is the one that makes someone stop scrolling. On WhatsApp, use clear photos in your catalogue, with the price and a short description under each one. On Jumia or Konga, follow the platform's photo rules: usually a white background, a minimum size, and no text or watermarks on the main image. A photo that works on Instagram may be rejected on a marketplace, so check the rules before you upload.

Write a caption that helps the photo sell: product name, price in naira, sizes or colours, delivery fee to Lagos and Abuja, one line about the material or benefit, and a clear call to action such as "Send us a DM to order". Customers want the facts, then the order button.

Post at times when your customers are online — early morning, lunchtime, and evening — and post consistently. One good product photo a day is better than twenty rushed photos once a month. Over time, your page becomes a catalogue that customers browse, share, and return to.

## Common Mistakes That Make Product Photos Look Cheap

The first mistake is shooting in a dark room at night. Yellow light, shadows, and noise make even a new product look used. Shoot in daylight whenever you can, and if you must shoot at night, use a ring light, not a ceiling bulb.

The second mistake is a cluttered background. A product photographed on a messy bed, next to a pile of clothes, or on a cracked tile floor tells the customer the seller is careless. A clean white board costs less than a bottle of soft drink, and it changes the whole photo.

The third mistake is using another seller's photos. Customers in Nigeria have been scammed by sellers who used downloaded photos and delivered a different product. If your photo is not your product, do not use it. Use your own photos, even if they are simpler. Honesty is a competitive advantage.

The fourth mistake is over-editing. Filters that change the colour of a dress, smooth the texture of a bag, or blur the background until the product looks cut out will disappoint the customer when the real item arrives. Edit lightly, keep colours true, and let the product speak for itself.

The fifth mistake is taking only one photo. One photo cannot show the front, the back, the size, and the detail. Take at least five shots of every product, and let the customer choose with confidence. A customer who can see the product clearly is a customer who is ready to pay.

## A Simple Setup You Can Build This Weekend for Under ₦50,000

You do not need to spend a lot to start. Here is a practical starter kit, with rough prices you can check in Computer Village, a stationery shop, or on Jumia. A white Bristol board, a few hundred naira. A small phone tripod, roughly ₦5,000 to ₦15,000. A 10-inch ring light, roughly ₦15,000 to ₦35,000. A soft cloth for cleaning your lens, a few hundred naira. A free editing app, Snapseed or Google Photos, no cost. Total: well under ₦50,000, and most of it is a one-time purchase that lasts for months.

Set up a permanent corner in your shop or at home, near a window, with your board, your tripod, and your ring light always ready. When a new product arrives, you can photograph it in ten minutes, edit it in five, and post it the same day. That speed matters: a product photographed and posted the day it arrives sells faster than one that sits in a carton for a week waiting for a "proper" photoshoot.

Practise with one product this week: photograph it in daylight, on a white board, from five angles, and edit it lightly. Compare the result with your old photos. Show both to a friend and ask which one they would buy. The answer will tell you everything you need to know. Then do the same for your next ten products, and watch your enquiries change. Good photos make customers trust you faster, ask fewer questions, and pay with more confidence. That is what a good product photo is really for.
