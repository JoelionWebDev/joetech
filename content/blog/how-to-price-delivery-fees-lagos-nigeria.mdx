Delivery is where many Lagos businesses quietly lose money. You sell a dress for N25,000, the customer is in Ajah, and by the time you have paid a rider and handled a failed delivery attempt, your profit has disappeared. Or you charge so much that customers buy from a seller closer to them.

Pricing delivery well is not guesswork. It is a simple calculation: know what delivery truly costs you, add a small margin for the unexpected, and present the fee in a way customers understand and accept. This guide shows you how to do that in Lagos, with real naira examples, zone pricing, rider options, and rules for free delivery that do not eat your profit.

## Why Delivery Pricing Matters More Than You Think

For a customer in Lagos, the delivery fee is often the deciding factor. A buyer in Lekki who sees a N3,000 delivery fee on a N8,000 order will hesitate. The same buyer will happily pay N3,000 delivery on a N40,000 order, because the fee feels small compared with the purchase. The fee has not changed. The context has.

Delivery also shapes how customers judge your business. A fee that is clear before they order builds trust. A fee that appears only at the last step, or changes after they have paid, destroys trust. A fee that is too low may win you the order and lose you money on every trip. Many sellers in Lagos absorb delivery costs to win sales, then discover at the end of the month that their "busy" business made almost nothing.

Think of delivery as a separate product with its own costs and its own price. Your food, clothes, or cosmetics have a price. Moving them from your shop in Yaba to a customer in Ikeja also has a price. When you separate the two in your mind, you stop subsidising one with the other without realising it.

Lagos adds its own challenges. Traffic on the Lekki-Epe Expressway, the Third Mainland Bridge, or the Apapa road can turn a one-hour trip into three hours. Rain can delay a rider. A customer may not be at home when the rider arrives. Fuel prices move. All of these are real costs, and your pricing must leave room for them.

## Step 1: Work Out What Delivery Really Costs You

Before you set a fee, list every cost involved in getting an order to a customer. Most sellers count only the rider's charge and forget the rest. That is why their maths never adds up.

Start with the rider or driver. If you use a dispatch rider, note what you pay per trip. Within a neighbourhood, a bike rider in Lagos often charges between N1,500 and N3,000, depending on distance. Between distant areas — say, from Lekki to Ikeja — the charge is often N4,000 to N7,000. A car or van for bulky items costs more, often N8,000 to N15,000 for a cross-Lagos trip. These are guide ranges, not fixed prices: ask two or three riders for quotes on your regular routes and write down what they actually charge.

Next, add fuel and transport if you deliver yourself. A trip from Surulere to Ajah and back can use a noticeable amount of fuel, and your time has value too. If you spend two hours in traffic delivering one order, that is two hours you are not packing orders, answering customers, or making new stock. Price your own time, even roughly, or you will keep working for free.

Add packaging — a sturdy bag, box, bubble wrap, or food container — and a small allowance for failed deliveries, when a customer is unreachable or gives the wrong address and the rider must return. If you use a payment link, note the provider's small percentage too.

Write all of this down for your three most common routes. For example, a Lekki seller delivering to Lekki Phase 1 might spend N2,000 on a rider, N300 on packaging, and N200 as an allowance for delays. The true cost is N2,500, not N2,000. That difference is the difference between profit and loss over a month of orders.

## Step 2: Choose a Pricing Model That Fits Your Business

There are four common ways Lagos businesses price delivery. Each suits a different kind of business. Choose one, and be consistent.

Flat-rate delivery is the simplest: one fee for everywhere in Lagos, for example N3,000 per order. Customers love it because it is easy to understand. It works best if most of your orders go to similar distances, or if your product price is high enough to absorb the difference. It fails when one customer is five minutes away and another is two hours away — you will overcharge the near customer and lose money on the far one.

Zone pricing is the most common in Lagos, and usually the fairest. Divide Lagos into zones based on distance from you. For a seller in Lekki, Zone 1 might be Lekki and Ajah, Zone 2 might be Victoria Island, Ikoyi, and Yaba, and Zone 3 might be Ikeja, Surulere, and the Mainland. Charge a different fee for each zone. Customers understand zones quickly if you name them clearly, and you stop losing money on long trips.

Distance-based pricing charges by kilometre, often through a logistics app. This is precise, but it can confuse customers who do not know how many kilometres away they are. It works well for bulky or heavy items where distance really drives the cost.

Free delivery above a minimum order is the fourth model, and it is powerful when used carefully. For example: "Free delivery within Lekki for orders above N50,000." The customer spends more to qualify, and you protect your margin because the order is large. The mistake is offering free delivery on small orders, where the delivery cost is bigger than your profit on the item.

Many businesses combine models: zone pricing for most orders, plus free delivery above a minimum order in the nearest zone. Start simple. One clear model, explained in one short message, beats a clever model nobody understands.

## Step 3: Build Your Lagos Zone Map

Zone pricing only works if your zones are honest. Do not copy another seller's zones — their shop is in a different place, so their distances are different. Build your own map around your location.

Start by writing down where your last twenty or thirty orders went. Group them by area: Lekki, Ajah, Victoria Island, Ikoyi, Yaba, Surulere, Ikeja, Ikorodu, Epe, and so on. You will quickly see that most orders come from a handful of areas. Those areas are your core zones. Areas with only one or two orders can share a zone.

Next, ask your rider what they charge for each area. A rider who knows Lagos will give you a realistic price per area, including traffic. Write those prices down. If a rider quotes N2,000 to Lekki Phase 1 and N5,000 to Ikeja, those are your starting costs. Add your packaging and delay allowance, then round to a clean number customers can remember — N2,500 and N5,500, for example.

Publish your zones where customers can see them — your WhatsApp catalogue, Instagram highlights, website, and a pinned group message. A customer in Surulere should find her fee in ten seconds. Review your zones every few months: fuel prices change, and a zone that was fair six months ago may be losing you money now.

## Step 4: Choose Between a Rider, a Logistics Company, and Delivering Yourself

How you deliver affects what you can charge. Each option has a cost and a trade-off.

A freelance dispatch rider is the most common choice for small Lagos businesses. Riders are flexible, they know the roads, and you can negotiate a regular rate if you give them steady work. The risk is reliability: a rider may be late, may not answer the phone, or may cancel on a rainy day. Keep the numbers of two or three trusted riders, not just one, so a single cancellation does not stop your business. Agree the price before the trip, confirm who pays if the customer is not available, and always share the rider's number with the customer so they can talk directly.

A logistics company — such as a courier service or a delivery platform — is more formal. You book through an app, you get a tracking number, and the company handles problems. The price is usually higher than a freelance rider, and the company may take longer because it batches orders. This option suits businesses with many orders a day, or sellers of high-value items who need proof of delivery. Compare two or three companies on your busiest route before you commit to one.

Delivering yourself works when you have few orders, when the customer is nearby, or when the item is fragile and you do not trust anyone else with it. A cake maker in Ikeja delivering to a customer in Ikeja herself is sensible. The same cake maker driving to Epe for one order is not — the fuel and time cost more than the profit. Set a rule for yourself: deliver personally only within your nearest zone, and use a rider for everything further away.

Whichever option you choose, tell the customer when the rider leaves and ask the rider to confirm delivery. Customers who know where their order is complain far less than customers left guessing.

## Step 5: A Simple Formula and a Worked Example

Here is a formula you can use for every order:

Delivery fee = rider cost + packaging + delay allowance + small margin

The margin is your buffer. It does not need to be large — a few hundred naira is enough — but it must be there. Without it, every small problem comes straight out of your profit.

Worked example: you sell skincare from a shop in Yaba. A customer in Ikeja orders products worth N18,000. Your rider charges N3,500 from Yaba to Ikeja. Packaging costs N400. You add N300 as a delay allowance and N300 as a margin. Your delivery fee is N4,500. You tell the customer the total before she pays: "Products N18,000 plus delivery N4,500, total N22,500." She can decide with full information, and you keep your profit even if traffic is bad.

Regular routes are where negotiation pays: a food vendor delivering lunch packs to the same office daily can agree a weekly rider rate and pass part of the saving to the customer.

Do this calculation for your three busiest routes, write the fees down, and stick to them. When fuel prices rise, recalculate rather than quietly absorbing the increase until it hurts.

## Step 6: Free Delivery Without Losing Money

Free delivery is a strong sales tool, but only when the maths works. The rule is simple: free delivery must be paid for by a bigger order, not by your profit.

Set a minimum order that covers the delivery cost twice over. If delivery to your nearest zone costs you N2,500, and your profit margin on products is about a third of the price, a free-delivery minimum of N25,000 to N30,000 is reasonable. A customer who spends N30,000 gives you enough profit to cover the N2,500 delivery and still earn well. A minimum of N10,000 would not.

Limit free delivery to your nearest zone, or to certain days. "Free delivery within Lekki on orders above N50,000, every Friday" is clear and affordable. "Free delivery everywhere, always" is not, unless your prices are high enough to absorb it.

Be honest about what free delivery means. It does not mean the rider works for free — it means you have included the cost in a larger order. If a customer asks why free delivery does not apply to her small order, explain kindly: "Free delivery starts at N50,000, because delivery to your area costs us N4,000. Your order is N12,000, so the delivery fee is N4,000, making N16,000 in total." Most customers understand when the numbers are clear.

Test free delivery for a month before making it permanent. Track how many orders qualified and how much delivery cost you. If profit rose, keep it. If not, raise the minimum or limit the zone.

## Step 7: Tell Customers the Fee Early and Clearly

The most common delivery complaint in Lagos is not the price — it is surprise. A customer who is told the fee at the start rarely argues. A customer who discovers it at the end feels cheated, even if the fee is fair.

State the delivery fee in your product post, your catalogue, and your first reply to a customer. When a customer asks "How much is this dress?", your answer should be: "The dress is N25,000. Delivery to Lekki is N2,500, to Victoria Island N3,500, and to Ikeja N5,000. Where are you based?" That one message answers the next three questions before they are asked.

Use the same fee for everyone in the same zone. Charging one customer N3,000 and another N5,000 for the same trip, because one of them negotiated harder, will be discovered — customers talk — and it will damage your reputation. If you want to offer a discount, offer it openly to everyone, for a stated reason and a stated period.

Confirm the total before payment. "Your total is N22,500 — products N18,000 plus delivery N4,500. Please send payment to this account." A customer who has seen the total in writing is far less likely to dispute it later.

If a fee must change — because fuel rose or a rider increased his rate — tell customers in advance. A short status or group message is enough: "From Monday, our delivery fee to Ikeja moves from N4,500 to N5,000 because rider rates have increased. Thank you for understanding." Customers accept honest changes. They do not accept silent ones.

## Step 8: Handle Failed Deliveries, Returns, and Bulky Items

Failed deliveries are part of Lagos business. The customer is not at home, the phone is off, the address is wrong, or the estate gate will not let the rider in. Decide your policy before it happens, and tell customers.

A fair policy is: the first delivery attempt is included in the fee. If the customer is unavailable and asks for a second attempt, the customer pays for the second trip, or you split it. State this at the point of order: "Please make sure someone is available to receive your order. If the rider cannot deliver and has to return, a second delivery will cost N2,500." Customers who know this will make sure someone is home.

For returns, decide who pays. If the item is wrong or damaged, you should pay for the return and the replacement — that is your fault, and fixing it well builds loyalty. If the customer simply changed her mind, she should pay the return delivery. Write this down and share it with customers before they order, not after a problem arises.

Bulky and fragile items need special pricing. A large cake, a standing mirror, a set of chairs, or a washing machine cannot go on a bike. It needs a car or van, careful packing, and sometimes two people. Price it separately, and quote the customer before you accept the order. Never promise a standard bike fee for an item that needs a van — you will either lose money or damage the item trying to save money.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

The first mistake is charging the same fee everywhere in Lagos. Lekki to Ajah and Lekki to Ikorodu are not the same journey, and pricing them the same guarantees you lose money on one of them.

The second mistake is hiding the fee until the end. Customers who feel tricked do not come back, and they tell their friends.

The third mistake is absorbing delivery costs to win every sale. A busy month with no profit is worse than a quiet month with profit. If an order does not pay after delivery costs, it is better to let it go, or to raise your product price honestly.

The fourth mistake is using only one rider with no backup. When that rider is sick or his bike breaks down, your deliveries stop. Keep two or three trusted contacts.

The fifth mistake is never reviewing your fees. Fuel, rider rates, and traffic change. A fee that was fair six months ago may be losing you money today. Review your three busiest routes every few months, ask riders for fresh quotes, and adjust.

Price delivery like the real cost it is: calculate it honestly, charge it openly, and review it regularly. Customers respect a clear fee far more than a cheap one that keeps changing.
