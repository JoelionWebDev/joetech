A beautiful product photo can stop someone from scrolling, but it is the caption that decides whether they buy. On Instagram, Facebook and TikTok, the image gets the attention and the words do the selling. Yet many Nigerian small businesses treat the caption as an afterthought. They post a lovely photo of a dress, a cake or a wig, and write only "Available. DM to order." The customer has no idea what it costs, what sizes are in stock, whether it delivers to their area, or why they should choose this seller over the next one. So they keep scrolling, and the sale goes to a vendor whose caption answered their questions.

Writing captions that sell is a skill, and like any skill it can be learned. You do not need to be a professional writer, and you do not need big grammar. You need to understand your customer, say clearly what you are selling and why it is worth the price, and tell the reader exactly what to do next. This guide explains how to do that, with simple formulas, examples you can adapt for your own business, and common mistakes to avoid. It works for fashion vendors, food sellers, bakers, skincare brands, hair vendors, phone sellers and service businesses across Nigeria.

## Start With the Customer, Not the Product

The first rule of a selling caption is to write about the customer, not about yourself. A caption that says "We sell quality dresses" is about you. A caption that says "A dress that fits comfortably all day, from the office in Ikeja to a wedding in Lekki" is about the customer and her life. Before you write, ask yourself three questions. Who is this product for? What problem does it solve for them? What would stop them from buying it? A busy mother buying children's clothes cares about durability, easy washing and fair prices. A bride choosing aso-ebi cares about colour, fabric quality and delivery before the wedding date. A young professional buying a laptop bag cares about size, protection and style.

Use the words your customers use. If your customers say "owambe" and "aso-ebi", use those words. Read the messages your customers send you on WhatsApp and the comments they leave on your posts. The phrases they repeat are the phrases your captions should use, because those are the words that will make a new customer feel understood. Avoid jargon and long, complicated sentences. Short sentences are easier to read on a phone, especially for a customer scrolling quickly on a bus in Lagos.

Think about the moment your customer sees your post. They may be comparing three sellers at once, they may be shopping late at night, or they may be buying a gift in a hurry. Your caption should respect their time. Put the most important information first: what it is, who it is for, and why it is good. Save the background story for later in the caption, after you have earned their attention. A customer who reads the first two lines and understands the offer is far more likely to keep reading than a customer who has to wade through a long introduction to find the price.

## Hook Them in the First Line, Then Keep Them Reading

On Instagram and Facebook, only the first one or two lines of your caption are visible before the reader has to tap "more". That first line is your hook, and it decides whether anyone reads the rest. A weak hook is "Good morning my lovers, trust you are doing well." A strong hook speaks directly to a need: "Your next owambe outfit is here, and it is ready in your size." "Tired of wigs that tangle after two weeks?" "Small chops for your party, delivered hot to your door in Lekki." Each of these tells the reader, in one line, that this post is for them.

There are several kinds of hooks that work well for Nigerian small businesses. A question hook asks about a problem: "Do you struggle to find shoes in size 44?" A benefit hook promises a result: "Look elegant all day without breaking the bank." A news hook announces something new: "New stock just landed, and this colour will sell out first." A story hook starts with a real moment: "A customer ordered this dress on Monday and wore it to her sister's wedding on Saturday." Choose the hook that fits the product and the day, and vary them, so your posts do not all start the same way. Test different hooks and watch which posts get the most saves, comments and messages, then use more of what works.

After the hook, keep the caption easy to read. Use short paragraphs, with a blank line between them, so the text does not look like one heavy block. Use simple line breaks to separate the product details, the price, and the ordering instructions. You can use a few emojis to break up the text and draw attention to key points, such as a price or a delivery note, but do not overdo it. A caption full of emojis and no clear information looks unprofessional, and it is hard to read. Aim for a caption that takes less than a minute to read, and read it aloud before you post. If it sounds awkward or confusing when spoken, rewrite it until it sounds like something you would say to a customer face to face.

## Sell the Benefit, Not Just the Features

Customers buy benefits, not features. A feature is a fact about your product: it is made of cotton, it has three compartments, it lasts for six months. A benefit is what that fact means for the customer: cotton keeps you cool in Lagos heat, three compartments keep your laptop, charger and documents organised, six months of use means you will not need to buy another one soon. Many Nigerian sellers list features and forget the benefits, and their captions read like a stock list instead of a sales message. For every feature you mention, add the benefit in the same sentence, so the customer understands why it matters.

Use specific details, because vague claims do not convince anyone. "High quality" means nothing on its own, because every seller says it. "Stitched with strong thread, so it survives machine washing" is specific and believable. "Long-lasting cream" is vague. "One bottle lasts about six weeks with daily use" is specific and helps the customer judge the value. "Fast delivery" is vague. "Delivery within Lekki in 24 hours, and nationwide in two to three working days" is specific and useful. The more concrete your caption is, the more the customer trusts you, and the less they need to message you with follow-up questions.

Connect the benefit to the customer's life in Nigeria. A dress that does not need ironing saves time for a working mother. A food pack that serves ten people makes planning a small party easier. A phone accessory that protects against cracked screens saves the cost of a repair in Computer Village. A cleaning service that comes on weekends means a busy professional returns to a clean flat. When you show the customer how your product fits into their real life, the price feels more reasonable, because they are buying the result, not just the item. End the benefit section with a short line that sums up the value, such as "Comfort, quality and a price that makes sense", and then move on to the practical details.

## Always Include the Price, the Options and the Next Step

The single biggest mistake in Nigerian social media captions is leaving out the price. Sellers often do this because they fear competitors will copy their prices, or because they want to force customers into their inbox. In practice, hiding the price loses far more sales than it protects. Most customers will not send a message just to ask for a price, and those who do will ask the same question you could have answered in the caption. Put the price in the caption, clearly, in Naira, with the options that affect it. If a dress costs one price in sizes 8 to 14 and more in larger sizes, say so. If a cake costs one price for a small size and more for a large one, list both.

Include the options a buyer needs to decide: sizes, colours, flavours, quantities, and what is currently in stock. If only three pieces are left in a particular colour, say so. If a product is available for pre-order with a delivery date, state the date. If delivery is included, say so. If it is not included, state the delivery fee for the main areas you serve, or say clearly that delivery is calculated by location and give a typical example, such as delivery within Lekki or within Ikeja. The more questions your caption answers, the fewer messages you have to answer, and the faster a ready buyer can order.

Every caption needs one clear call to action, telling the reader exactly what to do next. "Send us a WhatsApp message on 0803 000 0000 to order" is clear. "Tap the link in our bio to order on WhatsApp" is clear. "Comment ORDER and we will send you the payment details" is clear. "DM to order" on its own is weak, because it does not say what to write or what happens next. Give the reader the exact words to use, such as "Send us the word DRESS plus your size", so they do not have to think about what to say. Put the call to action at the end of the caption, and repeat it once in the first comment if your platform allows it. A customer who knows what to do is a customer who will do it.

## Use Stories, Reviews and Social Proof in Your Captions

A caption that tells a short, true story is more memorable than a caption that only lists facts. You do not need a long story. Two or three sentences are enough. "A customer bought this bag for her mother, and her mother has carried it every day since." "We baked forty boxes of small chops for a birthday in Surulere last Saturday, and the celebrant ordered again for Sunday." "This is the third time this customer has ordered the same cream, and she says her skin has never felt better." These small stories show that real people buy your products and come back for more, and they make your caption feel human instead of like an advert.

Customer reviews belong in your captions as well as in your Highlights. A short quote from a happy customer, with their first name and location where they agree, adds proof to your claims. "I love it, the dress fits perfectly. Thank you. Adaeze, Abuja." A review like that, placed after your product description, answers the doubt in a new customer's mind: will this seller deliver what they promise? Use reviews honestly. Never invent a review, never copy a review from another business, and never use a customer's words without their permission. One genuine review is worth more than ten invented ones, because customers can tell the difference, and a discovered fake will cost you trust.

Social proof can also be simple numbers, used honestly. "We have delivered to customers in Lagos, Abuja and Port Harcourt this month" tells a new buyer that you serve people across Nigeria. Avoid vague claims about being the best or the cheapest, because every seller says that and nobody believes it. Show your proof instead: a photo of parcels ready for dispatch, or a screenshot of a thank-you message with private details removed. Let the evidence do the talking, and keep your own claims modest and specific.

## Write Captions Faster With Simple Formulas You Can Reuse

You do not need to write every caption from scratch. A few simple formulas will cover most of your posts, and you can reuse them with different products. One useful formula is Hook, Benefit, Details, Action. Start with a hook that speaks to the customer, follow with one or two benefits, list the price and options, and end with a clear call to action. Another formula is Problem, Solution, Proof. Name the problem your customer has, explain how your product solves it, and add a short review or result as proof. A third formula is Story, Product, Offer. Tell a two-sentence story, introduce the product, and end with an offer or a reminder that stock is limited.

Keep a small note on your phone with your best captions, so you can adapt them instead of starting from a blank screen each time. Save the captions that brought you the most messages, sales or saves, and note what made them work: was it the hook, the price, the review, or the clear call to action? When you launch a new product, take a winning caption, change the product details, and post it. This is not laziness. It is smart business, because it lets you post consistently without spending an hour on every caption.

Batch your caption writing to save time. Once a week, sit down for an hour, choose the products you will post that week, and write all the captions in one go. During the week, you only need to copy, paste and post, which takes minutes instead of an hour. Keep your tone consistent across all your captions, and check every caption for mistakes before you post. A wrong price or phone number can cost you sales for days before you notice it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How long should a social media caption be for a Nigerian small business?**

Long enough to answer the customer's questions, and short enough to read in under a minute. For most products, five to ten short lines is enough: a hook, one or two benefits, the price and options, and a clear call to action. Longer captions work for stories, guides and launches, but only if the first two lines earn the reader's attention.

**Should I always put the price in my caption?**

Yes, in most cases. Stating the price saves you and the customer time, and it stops you losing buyers who will not message you just to ask. If your price varies by size, location or custom work, give the starting price in the caption and explain what affects the final price.

**How many hashtags should I use in a caption?**

Use a small number of relevant hashtags, around five to ten, and place them at the end of the caption so they do not interrupt the reading. Choose hashtags your customers actually search for, such as a product, a location or an occasion, rather than a long list of generic tags.

**Can I use the same caption for Instagram and Facebook?**

Yes, with small changes. The core message can stay the same, but adjust the call to action for each platform, because Instagram users tap a bio link while Facebook users may click a link in the post or comment. Check that phone numbers, prices and links are correct on each platform before you post.

**How do I know if my captions are working?**

Watch the numbers that matter: how many people save the post, comment on it, send you a message, or place an order after seeing it. A caption that gets many likes but no messages is entertaining, not selling. Keep the captions that bring enquiries and sales, and rewrite the ones that do not.
