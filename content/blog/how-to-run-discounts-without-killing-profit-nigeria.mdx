Every Nigerian business owner knows the temptation. Sales are slow, a competitor is running a promo, and the obvious answer seems to be a discount: cut your prices, watch the orders come in, and worry about the profit later. Sometimes it works beautifully. A well-planned promo can clear old stock, bring in new customers and create a buzz that lasts for weeks. But a badly planned discount does the opposite. It fills your day with orders that earn you nothing, trains your customers to wait for the next price cut, and leaves you exhausted with less money than you started with.

The difference is not whether you discount, but how. A discount is a tool, and like any tool, it needs a plan: a clear reason, a price worked out from your real costs, a time limit, and a way to measure whether it actually made you money. This guide walks through how to run discounts and promos that grow your business instead of quietly shrinking it — how to work out what you can afford, which types of promo work best, how to announce them, and how to stop them before they become a habit your customers expect.

## First, Know Your Numbers Before You Cut Any Price

No discount should ever be decided by feeling. Before you cut a price, you need to know three numbers for the product in question: what it costs you, what you sell it for, and how much profit is left after every cost. That profit — the margin — is what a discount eats into, and if you do not know how big it is, you cannot know how big a discount you can afford.

Work it out simply. Take the price you sell a product for and subtract everything it costs you to get that product into a customer's hands: what you paid your supplier, packaging, delivery or transport, and any marketplace or payment fees. What remains is your profit on one sale. Now apply your planned discount to the selling price and work out the new profit. If a product sells for ₦10,000, costs you ₦7,000 in total, and you offer 20 percent off, the new price is ₦8,000 and your profit falls from ₦3,000 to ₦1,000. That promo needs three times as many sales just to earn the same money. If the discount is 30 percent, the price becomes ₦7,000 and there is no profit at all — you are working for free. Many business owners discover this arithmetic only after the promo is over, when they count the money and realise the busy week earned them almost nothing.

Set a floor for yourself: a minimum profit per sale that you will not go below, and a maximum discount you will offer on any product. A useful rule for most small businesses is to keep discounts modest — often between 5 and 15 percent — and to reserve bigger cuts for stock you genuinely need to clear. Write these limits down before the promo starts, because in the excitement of a busy sales day it is easy to agree to a bigger discount on the spot and regret it later. If a customer asks for a discount you cannot afford, it is better to say no politely, or to offer something else of value, than to make a sale that loses you money.

## Choose the Right Kind of Promo for Your Goal

Not all promos are discounts, and the right choice depends on what you are trying to achieve. A straight percentage discount is the simplest and the most familiar, and it works well for a short, clear event such as a weekend sale or a holiday promo. But it is also the most dangerous, because it directly reduces what you earn on every sale and teaches customers that your normal price is negotiable. Use it sparingly, for short periods, and always with a clear end date.

A bundle is often a smarter choice. Instead of cutting the price of one item, offer two or three items together for a price that is better than buying them separately but still profitable for you. A hair vendor might bundle shampoo, conditioner and hair oil; a food seller might bundle a party pack; a fashion seller might bundle a dress with a matching scarf. Bundles raise the value of each order, move more stock at once, and feel generous to the customer without slashing your margin on a single item. They also introduce customers to products they might not have tried alone, which can create repeat sales later.

Buy-one-get-one offers, free gifts and free delivery work in a similar way. A free gift — a small extra item with every order above a certain amount — costs you little but makes the customer feel rewarded, and it does not touch your headline price at all. Free delivery above a minimum order value encourages customers to buy more to qualify, which raises your average order size. A loyalty reward, such as a small discount or a free item after a set number of purchases, brings customers back instead of attracting one-time bargain hunters. Each of these promos has a cost, so work it out in advance just as you would a discount — but their cost is usually smaller, and their effect on your pricing reputation is far gentler.

## Give Every Promo a Reason, a Limit and an End Date

A promo without a reason looks desperate, and a promo without an end date becomes your new price. The most effective promos are tied to something real: a new product launch, your business anniversary, a festive season such as Christmas or Sallah, a clearance of old stock before new stock arrives, or a thank-you to your customers. A reason gives customers a story and gives you a natural moment to stop. "Christmas Promo — ends 24 December" is clear and believable. "Discount" with no end date is not a promo at all; it is simply a lower price that your customers will expect forever.

Set limits before you announce anything. Decide how long the promo will run — a weekend, a week, never more than a few weeks — and how much stock it covers. Decide whether it applies to everything or only to selected products, and say so clearly. A promo on selected items protects your best sellers: you can discount the slow-moving stock that needs clearing while your most popular products keep their full price. Decide the maximum number of orders you can handle, too. A promo that brings in more orders than you can pack and deliver on time creates late deliveries, angry customers and bad reviews that undo all the good the promo did.

Write the terms down in plain language and share them wherever you announce the promo. State the discounted price, what is included, when it ends, and any conditions, such as payment before delivery or no discount on already-discounted items. Clear terms prevent arguments: a customer who knows the promo ends on Sunday will not demand the discount price on Wednesday, and a customer who knows delivery is not included will not be surprised by the delivery fee. When the end date arrives, end the promo. Restore your normal prices promptly, thank the customers who took part, and resist the urge to extend it by "just one more day" — extensions train customers to ignore your deadlines, and the next promo will be believed even less.

## Announce Your Promo Where Your Customers Already Are

A good promo that nobody hears about is a wasted opportunity. Announce it where your customers already spend their time, starting with WhatsApp Status, which is free and reaches the people most likely to buy from you. Post about the promo several times during the period it runs — at the start, in the middle, and a final reminder before it ends — because a single post is easily missed in a busy Status feed. Use clear, attractive photos of the products on offer, and put the price, the discount and the end date in the image or the caption so nobody has to ask.

Use your other channels in the same way: Instagram and Facebook posts and stories, a broadcast message to your regular customers, and a short announcement on your marketplace listings if you sell on Jumia or Konga. Keep the message short and specific. "20% off all Ankara dresses this weekend only — ends Sunday 8 p.m." will sell far more than a long paragraph about your business. Show the old price next to the new price so customers can see the saving, and show the products clearly so they know exactly what they are getting. If you have customer reviews or photos of people using the product, share them alongside the promo, because they give new buyers confidence.

Do not forget your existing customers. A short personal message to your regulars — "As one of my loyal customers, you get early access to this weekend's promo" — costs nothing and makes them feel valued. They are also the people most likely to share your promo with friends and family, which is free advertising of the best kind. Ask them to share, and make sharing easy by giving them a clear image and a short message they can forward. And track where your orders come from during the promo, even roughly: ask new customers how they heard about you. That simple question tells you which channel actually brings sales, so you can spend your effort there next time.

## Watch Out for the Traps That Turn Promos Into Losses

Even a well-planned promo can go wrong if you fall into one of the common traps. The first is discounting too often. If you run a promo every week, your customers will stop buying at full price and simply wait for the next discount. Your normal price stops being your price and becomes the price nobody pays. Keep promos occasional — a few times a year for big events, and a small clearance when you genuinely need to move stock — so that full price remains normal and a discount remains special.

The second trap is discounting your best sellers. Your most popular products sell well at full price; cutting their price gives away profit you were already earning. Discount the products that need help — the slow sellers, the old stock, the sizes or colours that are not moving — and let your best sellers earn their full margin. A related trap is the hidden cost trap: a promo that looks profitable until you count the extra packaging, the extra delivery trips, the extra airtime and the extra hours. During a busy promo, count those costs too, or the promo that felt successful will quietly cost you money.

The third trap is competing only on price. There will always be a seller willing to go lower than you, and if price is the only reason customers choose you, you will lose the moment someone cheaper appears. Use promos to attract attention, but keep customers with everything else: quality, honest descriptions, fast replies, careful packaging, reliable delivery and friendly service. A customer who buys during a promo and then receives excellent service is likely to return at full price. A customer who buys only because you were cheapest will leave the moment someone else is cheaper. Your promo should be the introduction, not the whole relationship.

## Measure the Result — Did the Promo Actually Make Money?

A promo is not finished when it ends. It is finished when you have measured whether it worked, and that measurement takes less than an hour. Sit down with your records and answer four questions. How many orders did the promo bring in, and how does that compare with a normal week? How much money came in, and how much profit is left after all costs, including the discount itself? How many of the promo customers were new, and how many were existing customers who would have bought anyway? And what did the promo cost you in extra time, packaging and delivery?

Compare the profit, not just the sales. A promo that doubled your sales but halved your profit has not helped your business — it has simply given you more work for the same money. A promo that brought in fewer sales but attracted new customers who came back the following week at full price has done something valuable, even if the promo week itself was modest. Write the answers down in a note, with the dates and the numbers, so that next time you plan a promo you are deciding from evidence instead of from memory. Over a year, these notes become a simple playbook: which promos worked, which did not, and what to repeat.

If the promo worked, think about what to do with the new customers it brought in. Save their contact details, with their permission, and keep in touch: a thank-you message, a note when new stock arrives, an early invitation to your next event. The real profit from a promo is rarely in the promo week itself; it is in the customers who stay. If the promo did not work, do not simply repeat it and hope. Change one thing — the product, the discount size, the timing, or the channel — and test again. Promos are experiments, and every experiment that teaches you something is worth the effort, as long as you actually learn from it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How big a discount should a small business offer?**

There is no single right number, because it depends on your profit margin. Work out your cost and your profit first, then choose a discount that leaves you a profit you can live with. For many small businesses, modest discounts of 5 to 15 percent are sustainable, while bigger cuts should be reserved for clearing old stock.

**How often should I run promos?**

Occasionally. A few well-planned promos a year — around festive seasons, your anniversary, or a product launch — keep discounts special. Promos every week train customers to wait for discounts and destroy your full-price sales.

**Are bundles better than percentage discounts?**

Often, yes. A bundle raises the value of each order and moves more stock, while a percentage discount simply cuts what you earn on each sale. Bundles also introduce customers to products they might not have tried, which can lead to repeat sales.

**What is the biggest mistake with discounts?**

Discounting without knowing your numbers. Many sellers set a discount that looks attractive, then discover afterwards that it wiped out their profit or even sold at a loss. Always work out the discounted profit per sale before you announce anything.

**Should I discount my best-selling products?**

Usually not. Best sellers already sell well at full price, so discounting them gives away profit you were already earning. Discount slow-moving stock and old items instead, and let your popular products earn their full margin.

## What to Do Next

Do these three things before your next promo. First, pick one product and work out its real numbers — total cost, selling price, profit — and decide the biggest discount you can afford on it without going below your minimum profit. Second, plan your next promo properly: give it a real reason, choose the promo type that fits your goal, set a firm end date, and write the terms in plain language. Third, after the promo ends, spend an hour measuring it: orders, profit, new customers, and lessons for next time — and keep in touch with the new customers it brought you.
