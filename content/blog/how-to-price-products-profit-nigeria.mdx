Funke sells skincare from a small shop in Ikeja and from her WhatsApp catalogue. In January she bought a popular body cream from her supplier at ₦6,500 a jar and sold it for ₦10,000. By March the supplier's price was ₦7,800, and she kept selling at ₦10,000 because she did not want to upset her customers. By June the supplier wanted ₦9,200. Her delivery rider had also raised his fee, from ₦2,000 to ₦3,000 for Lekki runs, because fuel had gone up again. She sat down one Sunday, added everything up, and found she was making less than ₦500 on a jar that took her a week to sell. She had been busy all year and her savings had gone backwards.

Funke's problem is the problem of almost every Nigerian seller right now. Prices do not rise once and settle. They rise in steps, a little at a time, month after month, driven by the naira moving against the dollar, by fuel, by transport, by a supplier who imports and passes every change straight down the line. The price you set in January is wrong by June, and the price you set in June is wrong by October. The sellers who survive this are not the ones with the cheapest prices. They are the ones with a routine for repricing, a way of deciding each time whether to absorb a rise or pass it on, and a way of telling their customers that does not sound like an apology or a quarrel.

## Prices move in steps, so check them on a schedule

The first mistake is waiting until the money runs out before looking at your prices. By then you have sold a month of stock at a loss, and no single price rise can fix it without scaring your customers. The second mistake is checking every day and changing prices every week, which makes customers feel they are being charged whatever you feel like that morning.

Pick a routine and keep it. The first Saturday of every month, sit with your notebook for one hour and check your five best-selling products. For each one, write down what your supplier charges now, what transport and delivery cost now, and what you sell it for now. If the supplier's price has moved, you have a decision to make. If it has not moved, you close the book and get on with your week. One hour a month is enough, because most suppliers do not raise prices every week. They raise them every few weeks, and a monthly check catches each rise while it is still small.

## Absorb or pass on: decide with numbers, not feelings

Every time a cost rises, you have three choices, and only three. Absorb it and keep your price, pass it on and raise your price, or change the product so it costs less to sell. The right choice depends on how much profit is left after the rise.

Take Funke's body cream. In January the jar cost ₦6,500 from the supplier, packaging and a share of delivery came to ₦800, so her true cost was ₦7,300. She sold at ₦10,000, keeping ₦2,700, a margin of 27%. In March the supplier raised the price to ₦7,800. Her true cost became ₦8,600 and her profit fell to ₦1,400, a margin of 14%. That is thin, but the cream was her best seller, and she decided to absorb the rise for one month while she watched what happened.

In June the supplier went to ₦9,200 and the rider went up too, pushing her true cost to about ₦10,300. Her selling price was still ₦10,000. She was now losing ₦300 on every jar, plus her time. Absorbing was no longer a choice. It was a slow way of closing the shop. She raised the price to ₦12,500. Her profit became ₦2,200 a jar, a margin of about 18%, and she kept most of her customers because the rise was explained, the product was the same, and every other seller of that cream had raised their price too.

The rule is simple to state and hard to keep. If a rise leaves you with at least 20% margin, you can absorb it for a month while you decide. If it leaves you below 20%, pass it on, or change the product. If it leaves you at zero or below, pass it on immediately, because every sale is now taking money out of your pocket. Funke's error was not absorbing the March rise. Her error was absorbing it without a date in mind, so one month of patience quietly became four months of losses.

## One product, three price rises: watch the maths hold

The fear that stops most sellers from raising prices is the fear that customers will leave. Sometimes they will. But a customer who leaves over a ₦2,000 rise on a product she loves is rarer than sellers think, and a seller who keeps absorbing rises until she cannot pay her supplier loses every customer at once when the shop closes.

Follow one product through three rises, because this is what actually happens in Nigeria. Amara sells Ankara fabric in Balogun market, six yards at a time. In February she bought a bale at a price that worked out to ₦9,000 per six yards, sold at ₦14,000, and kept ₦5,000 after transport and market levies. In April the supplier's price went up and her cost per six yards became ₦10,500. She raised her selling price to ₦15,500, kept ₦5,000, and told her regulars the week before the new price started. Two customers grumbled and one went to try another stall. She came back a fortnight later, because the other stall's fabric was thinner.

In July the naira slipped again and Amara's cost reached ₦12,000. She raised the price to ₦17,500 and kept ₦5,500. This time she also started offering a smaller cut, three yards at ₦9,000, for customers who could not afford six yards at once.

In October her cost hit ₦13,500. She raised the price to ₦19,500 and kept ₦6,000. Three rises, three price increases, and her profit per sale actually grew slightly, because each time she raised the price by a little more than the cost rise.

That is the pattern to copy. Small rises, often, with notice. One big jump of ₦6,000 after six months of silence feels like a shock and loses customers. Three rises of ₦2,000, each explained, feel like the market, and customers accept the market.

## Smaller pack or higher price: choose honestly

When a cost rises, some sellers quietly make the product smaller and keep the price the same. A food seller who used to sell a 500g pack of chin chin for ₦2,500 starts selling 400g for ₦2,500. A cream seller moves from a 500ml jar to a 400ml jar. Customers notice. They always notice, usually at the second purchase, and the feeling it leaves is worse than a price rise, because it feels like a trick.

There is an honest way to shrink: say so, and give the customer a real choice. Keep the full-size jar at the new higher price, and add a smaller jar at the old price. "The 500ml jar is now ₦12,500. If that is too much this month, the 300ml jar is ₦7,500." The customer decides. She may buy the small jar this month and the big one next month, when her salary lands. What she will not do is feel deceived, and a customer who feels deceived does not come back to argue. She simply never returns, and you never learn why.

## How to tell your regulars

Most sellers raise prices the wrong way: they say nothing, and the customer finds out at the till. That single moment does more damage than the rise itself, because the customer feels ambushed. Tell people before the new price starts, and tell them in the right order.

Tell your regulars first, personally, a week before the change. A short WhatsApp message is enough: "Good evening, Mrs. Adeyemi. From Monday next week, the body cream will be ₦12,500 instead of ₦10,000. My supplier has raised the price twice since March, and I have kept my price the same until now. The cream is the same, and the 300ml jar stays at ₦7,500 if that suits you better. Thank you for understanding." That message does three things. It gives notice. It gives a reason that is true. And it gives the customer an option, which is what stops a price rise from feeling like a locked door.

Tell everyone else on your status the same week, and update your catalogue and your price list on the same day the new price starts. Nothing confuses customers faster than a status that says ₦12,500 and a catalogue that still says ₦10,000. Update both, together, on the day.

## Drop the products that cannot pay

Not every product deserves a price rise. Some are worth keeping cheap because they bring people in, and they buy something else once they arrive. A hair vendor may keep her cheapest attachment at a low margin because it brings new customers who later buy the expensive wigs. But that only works if you know which product is the magnet and which one is the earner. Check your monthly numbers and mark each product honestly: if it earns less than 20% after three rises and it is not bringing in customers for anything else, stop selling it.

Funke did this in August with a small imported jar whose supplier price had climbed from ₦4,000 to ₦6,800 in five months: she stopped stocking it and used the money to buy more of the body cream that still earned her ₦2,200 a jar.

## Your monthly repricing routine

Put it on a calendar, because a routine you do not schedule is a routine you will skip. First Saturday of the month, one hour, five products, three questions each. What does it cost me now? What do I sell it for now? What is my margin now? If the margin is above 20%, leave the price alone. If it is between 10% and 20%, decide this month whether to raise the price or shrink the pack, and tell your regulars this week. If it is below 10%, raise the price this week or drop the product. Write the decision in the same notebook, with the date, so next month you can see what you decided and why.

Keep a price history for each product, one line a month. It takes five minutes. When a customer says "but it was ₦10,000 last month," you can answer from the book instead of from memory. When a supplier raises the price for the third time in a quarter, you can see the pattern and order more stock before the next rise, if you have the cash.
