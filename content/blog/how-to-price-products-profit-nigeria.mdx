You sold fifty items this week. Your WhatsApp was busy, your delivery rider was busy, your phone never stopped. Then you sat down on Sunday night, counted the money, paid your suppliers, paid for transport and data, and realised there was almost nothing left. You were busy, but you were not profitable.

This is one of the most common problems for Nigerian small businesses. The price feels right because customers are buying, but the price is wrong because it does not cover the real cost of doing business. Pricing is not guessing, and it is not copying the woman in the next shop. Pricing is maths, plus a clear understanding of what your customers will pay for.

This guide walks you through pricing your products for profit, step by step, with naira examples you can copy. Whether you sell clothes in Lekki, food in Yaba, hair in Surulere, or services online, the same principles apply.


## Step 1: Calculate Your True Cost Per Item

Before you set any price, work out the true cost of one unit. This is the foundation of everything else. Take a single product and list every cost that goes into getting it to the customer.

**Direct costs — the obvious ones:**

- Cost of the product itself from your supplier, divided by the number of units.
- Import or shipping cost, if you buy from outside Lagos or outside Nigeria, divided per unit.
- Customs, clearing, or agent fees, if any, divided per unit.

**Selling costs — the ones sellers forget:**

- Transport to the market or to your supplier, divided per unit.
- Delivery cost — rider, dispatch, or fuel — per order.
- Packaging — nylon, box, bag, ribbon, sticker — per unit.
- Payment charges — bank transfer fees, POS percentage, Paystack or Flutterwave fees per transaction.
- Data and airtime used to sell that product — a share of your monthly data bundle.

**Business costs — a fair share per unit:**

- Rent for your shop or storage, divided by the number of units you sell in a month.
- Light — NEPA bills, generator fuel, or inverter costs — divided per month and then per unit.
- Staff wages, if you pay anyone, even part-time.
- Your own time — this is the hardest one, but it is real. If you spend two hours a day answering enquiries and packing orders, that time has a value.

Here is a worked example. A seller in Lekki buys a dress for ₦12,000 from a supplier. Transport to collect it costs ₦1,000, packaging costs ₦500, delivery to the customer costs ₦2,000, payment charges are ₦200, and a share of rent and light is ₦800. The true cost is ₦12,000 + ₦1,000 + ₦500 + ₦2,000 + ₦200 + ₦800 = ₦16,500. If she sells that dress for ₦18,000, her profit is only ₦1,500 — not the ₦6,000 she thought she was making when she looked only at the supplier price. If she sells it for ₦15,000 because a competitor does, she loses ₦1,500 on every sale.

Do this calculation for your five best-selling products this week. Use a notebook or a Google Sheet. The numbers may surprise you, and some may be uncomfortable. That discomfort is useful: it tells you exactly which products are making money and which ones are quietly losing it.

## Step 2: Decide Your Profit Margin — And Be Honest About It

Once you know your true cost, you need to add profit. Profit is not greed. Profit is what pays for growth, for emergencies, for a bad month, and for your own salary. A business without profit is a hobby that eats your savings.

A simple way to think about margin is as a percentage on top of cost. For many Nigerian retail businesses, a healthy gross margin is 30% to 50% on top of true cost. For food businesses, where waste and spoilage are real risks, 40% to 60% is common. For services like hair, photography, or cleaning, where the main cost is your time and skill, the margin should be higher still, because you are selling expertise, not just a physical item.

Use this formula: Selling Price = True Cost ÷ (1 − Desired Margin). If your true cost is ₦16,500 and you want a 40% margin, your price is ₦16,500 ÷ 0.60 = ₦27,500. If that price feels too high for your market, you have two honest choices: reduce your costs, or accept a lower margin and sell more volume. What you must not do is pretend the margin exists when it does not.

Be careful with the word "margin" versus "mark-up". A 50% mark-up on a ₦10,000 cost gives a price of ₦15,000. A 50% margin on the same cost gives a price of ₦20,000, because margin is calculated on the selling price, not the cost. Many sellers confuse the two and end up with half the profit they intended. Decide which one you mean, write it down, and use it consistently.

Also decide your minimum acceptable margin. For most small businesses, anything below 20% is dangerous, because one price increase from a supplier, one bad delivery, or one refund can wipe it out entirely. If a product cannot earn at least 20% after all costs, consider dropping it, raising its price, or finding a cheaper supplier. Selling a product at a loss to attract customers only works if those customers then buy profitable products — and in practice, they often do not.

## Step 3: Check What Customers Will Actually Pay

Cost tells you the lowest price you can afford. Customers tell you the highest price they will pay. Your final price must sit between those two numbers, and finding that spot takes research, not guessing.

Start by listening. What do customers say when you quote a price? Do they buy immediately, do they negotiate hard, or do they disappear? If everyone buys without negotiating, your price is probably too low. If everyone negotiates and most walk away, your price is probably too high — or your value is not clear enough. If about half negotiate and most still buy, you are probably close to the right range.

Look at your competitors, but look properly. Visit three competitors who serve the same kind of customer you do. Note their prices for similar products, and note what is included: delivery, packaging, warranty, after-sales support, or a return policy. A competitor who charges ₦20,000 with free delivery and a return policy is not the same as a competitor who charges ₦18,000 with no delivery and no returns. Compare the full offer, not just the number.

Talk to your customers directly. Ask five regular customers on WhatsApp: "What would you expect to pay for this, and what would make it worth more to you?" You will learn whether they value speed, quality, packaging, or after-sales care. A customer who values quality will pay more for a product that lasts. A customer who values speed will pay more for same-day delivery in Lagos. Price is never just a number — it is a number attached to a promise.

## Step 4: Choose a Pricing Strategy That Fits Your Business

There is no single correct pricing strategy. There are several, and the right one depends on your product, your customers, and your goals.

**Cost-plus pricing.** Add a fixed margin to your true cost. Simple, honest, and easy to explain. Best for most small retailers and food sellers starting out. The weakness is that it ignores what customers will pay and what competitors charge, so review it regularly.

**Competitor-based pricing.** Price close to your competitors, slightly above or below depending on your quality. This works when products are very similar, like phone accessories or basic groceries. The danger is a race to the bottom, where everyone cuts prices until nobody makes a profit. Use it only when you genuinely offer the same thing, and never as your only strategy.

**Value-based pricing.** Price based on what the product is worth to the customer, not just what it cost you. A custom-made dress for a wedding, a professional logo for a business, or a well-made wig that lasts a year can be priced higher than the cost-plus formula suggests, because the customer is buying confidence, beauty, or a business result. This is the most profitable strategy, but it requires you to communicate the value clearly — through good photos, testimonials, and a professional presentation.

**Penetration pricing.** Start low to win customers quickly, then raise prices once you have a reputation. This can work for a new business entering a crowded market, but it is risky: customers who joined for the low price may leave when the price rises, and you may train the market to expect cheap prices from you. If you use it, set a clear date for the price rise and tell customers it is an introductory price.

**Bundle pricing.** Sell two or three items together for a price that is lower than buying them separately, but higher than the cost of the bundle. A skincare set, a lunch pack with drink, or a phone plus case plus screen guard. Bundles increase the average order value and help you clear slow-moving stock. Make sure the bundle price still leaves a healthy margin after all costs.

Most Nigerian small businesses should start with cost-plus, then move toward value-based pricing as their reputation grows. The goal is to earn more from the same customers by offering more value, not by charging more for the same thing.

## Step 5: Price for Delivery, Discounts, and Hidden Charges

Delivery is where many Nigerian pricing plans fall apart. A seller quotes ₦20,000 for a product, the customer agrees, and then the delivery rider charges ₦3,500 to Lekki or ₦5,000 to Ikeja, and the seller absorbs it to avoid losing the sale. Do that ten times a month and you have given away ₦35,000 or more.

Decide your delivery policy before you quote prices, and state it clearly:

- **Free delivery above a threshold.** For example, free delivery in Lagos for orders above ₦50,000. This encourages bigger orders and covers your delivery cost from the margin on a larger sale.
- **Flat delivery fee by area.** Lekki ₦2,000, Ikeja ₦3,000, Abuja ₦5,000, Port Harcourt ₦6,000. Publish the fees so customers are not surprised.
- **Customer pays delivery.** State it upfront: "Price is ₦20,000 plus delivery." This is fair and common, as long as it is said before the customer pays, not after.
- **Delivery included in the price.** Add the average delivery cost to your price and advertise "free delivery". This feels generous to customers, but only works if your average delivery cost is stable. If delivery costs vary widely by location, this strategy will lose you money on faraway orders.

Discounts need the same discipline. A 10% discount sounds small, but on a product with a 30% margin, it removes one-third of your profit. A 20% discount on the same product removes two-thirds. Before you offer a discount, work out what it costs you. A discount should be a tool with a purpose — to clear old stock, to reward a loyal customer, to win a bulk order — not a habit you offer every time a customer asks.

Also watch payment charges. If a customer pays by transfer, the bank may charge you a fee. If they pay by POS or through Paystack, a percentage is deducted. If they pay cash on delivery, there is a risk of a failed delivery and a wasted rider fee. Build a small allowance for payment costs into your price, or state clearly which payment methods are free and which attract a charge. Many sellers now prefer transfers because they are cheaper than card payments, and they say so openly.

## Step 6: Test Your Prices — Small Changes, Real Data

Pricing is not a one-time decision. It is a test you run continuously. The best way to test is to change one price, by a small amount, and watch what happens for two weeks.

Raise the price of one product by 5% or 10% — for example, from ₦20,000 to ₦22,000 — and track how many units you sell, how many enquiries you get, and how many customers complain. If sales stay roughly the same, your new price is better, and you have just increased your profit on every sale. If sales drop sharply, you have learned something valuable: that product is price-sensitive, and you need to either lower the price again or increase the perceived value with better photos, better packaging, or a stronger guarantee.

Test in the other direction too. If a product is not selling at all, a price cut of 10% may be less costly than holding dead stock for three months. Old stock that does not sell is money sitting on a shelf. A small discount that moves it is better than a full price that nobody pays.

Keep a simple price test log in your notebook or spreadsheet: product, old price, new price, date changed, units sold before, units sold after. After a month, you will have real data instead of feelings. Data is what turns pricing from guesswork into a skill.

Do not change all your prices at once. Change one or two, watch the result, then decide. Changing everything at the same time tells you nothing about which change worked, and it confuses regular customers who notice that everything went up overnight.



## A 7-Day Plan to Fix Your Pricing

- **Day 1:** List your five best-selling products and write down their current selling prices.
- **Day 2:** Calculate the true cost of each one, including transport, delivery, packaging, payment charges, and a share of rent and light.
- **Day 3:** Work out your real profit and margin on each product. Mark any product earning less than 20% margin.
- **Day 4:** Check three competitors for each product and note their prices and what is included.
- **Day 5:** Decide new prices for the products that are under-priced. Use the cost-plus formula and check them against competitor prices and customer value.
- **Day 6:** Tell your regular customers about any price changes, with notice and a brief explanation. Update your WhatsApp catalogue, your Instagram highlights, and your price list.
- **Day 7:** Start a simple price log. Record units sold, enquiries, and customer reactions for the next two weeks, then review and adjust one price at a time.

After thirty days, review the numbers. You should see higher profit per sale, even if the number of sales is similar. That is the goal: not necessarily more sales, but more profit from the sales you already make.

## Final Word

Pricing is not about charging the highest price you can get away with. It is about charging a fair price that covers your true costs, rewards your work, and reflects the value your customers receive. A price that is too low starves your business. A price that is too high without value drives customers away. The right price sits in the middle, and it is found with maths, research, and testing — not with guessing.

Do the true-cost calculation this week. You may find that one small price change, on one product, adds thousands of naira to your profit every month. That is the power of pricing done properly: the same work, the same customers, and a business that finally pays you what the work is worth.
