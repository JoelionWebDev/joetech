Facebook Marketplace has become one of the busiest places to buy and sell in Nigeria. From used iPhones in Ikeja Computer Village to sofas in Lekki, from fairly used cars in Abuja to baby clothes in Surulere, millions of Nigerians open the Marketplace tab every day looking for a bargain. The problem is that the same features that make it convenient — no listing fee, instant messaging, and no middleman — also make it a favourite hunting ground for fraudsters. Every week, Nigerians lose money to Marketplace scams, from ₦5,000 for a pair of shoes to several million naira for a car. The good news is that almost all of these scams follow the same patterns, and once you know the patterns, they are easy to spot. This guide explains the most common Facebook Marketplace scams in Nigeria, how to protect yourself as a buyer, how to protect yourself as a seller, and what to do if you have already been scammed.

## Why Facebook Marketplace Is a Scam Hotspot in Nigeria

Facebook Marketplace works differently from Jumia or Konga. There is no escrow, no buyer protection, and no verified seller badge that means very much. Anyone with a Facebook account can list anything in minutes, and Facebook does very little checking. A scammer can create a fresh account in the morning, list a stolen photo of an iPhone 15 Pro Max for ₦450,000, collect deposits from ten people by evening, and delete the account before anyone reports it. The second reason is trust: many people move the conversation off Facebook to WhatsApp quickly, where there is no record, and scammers exploit this by sending fake bank alerts and fake waybills there.

## The Most Common Marketplace Scams in Nigeria

The first and most common is the too-good-to-be-true listing. An iPhone 14 Pro Max for ₦280,000 when the same phone sells for ₦750,000 in Computer Village. A 2020 Toyota Camry for ₦2.5 million when the market price is ₦9 million. A brand-new 65-inch Samsung TV for ₦150,000. The photos are usually stolen from a real listing abroad or from Jumia, and the seller will have a story for the low price: "I am relocating to Canada next week" or "I need urgent money for my mother's surgery". The price is the bait. Once you are hooked, the scam moves to the next stage: the deposit.

The deposit scam is the workhorse of Marketplace fraud. The seller tells you that other buyers are interested, so you must pay a "commitment fee" or "booking fee" — usually ₦10,000 to ₦100,000 — to hold the item. Sometimes they ask for the full payment upfront because they are "not in Lagos" and will send the item by dispatch rider or waybill. Once the money lands in their account, they block you. Some scammers will send a fake dispatch tracking number or a fake waybill to keep you waiting while they collect more deposits from other victims.

The fake payment alert scam targets sellers. A buyer agrees to your price, comes to inspect the item, and then "transfers" the money. You receive an SMS that looks exactly like a bank alert, or the buyer shows you a screenshot of a successful transfer on their phone. The money never actually arrives. The SMS is fake, sent from a bulk SMS service, and the screenshot is edited. This scam works best in a hurry — the buyer is in a rush, the network is "slow", and they pressure you to release the item before the alert is confirmed.

The cloned listing scam is more subtle. A scammer copies a genuine seller's photos and description, then lists the same item at a lower price. If you ever find two identical listings with the same photos but different prices and different sellers, one of them is fake.

The overpayment scam usually targets sellers of expensive items. A buyer "mistakenly" sends more than the agreed price and asks you to refund the difference quickly, often to a different account. The original payment was made with a stolen card or a fake transfer that will be reversed, but your refund is real money that leaves your account permanently.

Finally, there is the physical danger scam. A buyer or seller arranges a meeting in a quiet location — an empty street or an uncompleted building — and the victim is robbed. This is rare compared with deposit fraud, but it is the most dangerous, and it is the reason the meeting-location rules in this guide are not optional.

## Red Flags to Check Before You Reply to Any Listing

The first check is the price. Search the same item on Jumia, Konga, and a second Marketplace listing. If the price is less than half the going rate for no credible reason, walk away. Genuine sellers in Nigeria know the market value of what they own. A person selling a working iPhone at a third of its value is either lying about the item or lying about the price — either way, you should not be the one to find out which.

The second check is the seller's profile. Tap the seller's name and look at their profile. How old is the account? A profile created last month with one photo and no friends is a warning sign. Does the person have normal activity — posts, comments, photos of real life? Scammers' profiles are usually empty, locked, or filled with only Marketplace listings. Also check whether the same account has listed dozens of unrelated high-value items in different cities, all at bargain prices. That is a scammer running a catalogue of fake listings.

The third check is the photos. Look closely. Are they all the same quality and style, or is one a professional product shot from the manufacturer's website and the next a blurry photo taken in a bedroom? Mixed photo styles usually mean stolen images. You can also do a reverse image search: save the photo and search it on Google Images. If the same photo appears on a listing in the United States or on a Jumia product page, the seller does not own the item.

The fourth check is the description. Scam listings often have very short descriptions or copy-pasted text. Genuine sellers usually describe the condition, the reason for selling, and what is included. Watch for phrases like "serious buyers only, no time wasters" and "first to pay gets it" — these are pressure language, not information.

## How to Buy Safely on Facebook Marketplace: The Rules

Rule one is the most important: never pay before you see the item. Not a deposit, not a booking fee, not a "commitment" payment, not transport money. A genuine seller who wants to sell will let you inspect the item. If they insist on payment first, that is your answer — walk away. This single rule would prevent the majority of Marketplace losses in Nigeria.

Rule two: inspect before you pay, and inspect properly. For a phone, check the IMEI number by dialling *#06# and compare it with the number on the box. Check that the phone is not iCloud-locked or Google-locked by asking the seller to sign out in front of you and reset the phone. Test the camera, the speakers, the charging port, and the battery health. For a laptop, check the battery, keyboard, screen, and ports, and ask for the receipt if they have it. For a car, never buy without a physical inspection and a mechanic you trust, and confirm the engine and chassis numbers match.

Rule three: meet in a safe public place. A busy mall, a bank premises, a police station, or a well-known market during trading hours. Meet during the day, never at night. Bring a friend or family member, and tell someone where you are going. If the seller insists on a quiet location or a specific lonely spot, cancel the meeting.

Rule four: pay in a way you can trace. Pay by bank transfer to an account in the seller's own name, and check that the account name matches the name on their Facebook profile and their ID. Ask to see the seller's National Identity Card, driver's licence, or voter's card. Avoid paying cash where possible, because cash leaves no trail. And never pay into a third-party account — "my brother's account" or "my wife's account" — unless you have verified that person too.

Rule five: confirm the money before you hand over the item or leave. If you are the buyer, the seller should confirm receipt in their own bank app, not from a screenshot you show them. If you are the seller, log into your own bank app and confirm the credit yourself, and wait for it to show as available, not pending. Never accept a screenshot of a transfer or an SMS alert as proof — both can be faked in minutes. If the network is slow, wait. A genuine buyer will wait with you.

Rule six: use a video call before you travel. If the item is in another part of Lagos or in a different city, ask the seller to do a live video call showing the item, with a piece of paper with your name on it in the frame. A recorded video can be faked; a live call with a specific request is much harder to fake. If the seller refuses a video call, they probably do not have the item.

## How to Sell Safely on Facebook Marketplace

First, set a clear price and state your terms in the listing: "Payment by transfer only, confirmed in my bank app before collection. No deposit refunds. Inspection welcome at [public location]." Clear terms filter out many scammers before they message you.

Second, never release an item on the strength of a screenshot or an SMS. Log into your own bank app and confirm the credit yourself. Check the amount, the sender's name, and the time. Fake alerts often arrive as SMS from a regular phone number rather than the bank's sender name, or as a screenshot with a slightly wrong font or logo. If the buyer says "the alert will come, network is slow", wait until it comes.

Third, be careful with "buyers" who want you to ship the item before payment. Unless you are using a proper escrow or a payment-on-delivery arrangement with a rider you trust, do not send an item to a stranger on a promise. If a buyer is in another city, insist on full payment confirmed in your bank app before you hand the item to any dispatch rider, and share the rider's details and the waybill number with the buyer.

Fourth, protect your personal information. Do not share your home address in the listing or in early messages. Use a public meeting place. Do not share your BVN, your card details, or your bank login with anyone — no real buyer ever needs those. Be careful with buyers who ask for your WhatsApp number immediately and try to move the whole conversation off Facebook. You can use WhatsApp, but keep the key facts — the price, the condition, the meeting place — in the Facebook chat as well, so there is a record.

Fifth, watch out for the overpayment trick. If a buyer sends more than the agreed price and asks for a refund of the difference, stop. Do not refund anything until you have confirmed with your bank that the original payment is genuine and cleared. A real overpayment can be reversed through the bank; a scammer's "overpayment" is a trap.

## Safe Payment Methods in Nigeria

Bank transfer to an account in the person's own name is the safest common method, because it leaves a trace and the account name can be checked. Before you transfer a large amount, do a test transfer of ₦100 first and confirm the account name matches the person's real name. Paystack and Flutterwave payment links are also useful for sellers, because the buyer pays through a secure page and the seller receives the money through a verified business account.

Payment on delivery is a good option for smaller items when you use a rider service you trust. Escrow services exist for high-value items — a trusted third party holds the money until the buyer confirms the item is as described — but make sure the escrow service is real and well-known, not a website the "seller" recommended, because fake escrow sites are a scam of their own.

Avoid these payment methods entirely: gift cards, cryptocurrency sent to a stranger, airtime transfers as payment for goods, and transfers to a POS agent or a betting wallet. None of these can be traced or reversed, and they are the payment methods scammers ask for most often.

## What to Do If You Have Already Been Scammed

If you paid a scammer, act fast. The first few hours matter. First, call your bank immediately and report the fraud. Ask them to place a hold on the transaction and to trace the receiving account. Banks in Nigeria have fraud desks, and while recovery is not guaranteed, early reports have a better chance. Keep the transaction reference, the amount, the date, and the receiving account details.

Second, report the seller and the listing to Facebook. Open the listing or the conversation, tap the three dots, and report it as a scam. Facebook may not refund you, but reports help get the account removed.

Third, report to the authorities. You can report online fraud to the Economic and Financial Crimes Commission (EFCC) through their website or by visiting their Lagos or Abuja office, and to the Nigeria Police Force's cybercrime unit. Bring screenshots of the conversation, the listing, the payment receipt, and the scammer's account details. Many victims do not report because the amount feels small, but every report builds a pattern that helps investigators link accounts and phone numbers.

Fourth, warn others. Post in the community groups where you found the listing, with screenshots and the scammer's details, so other buyers are warned. Scammers rely on silence and embarrassment.

Fifth, protect yourself from repeat scams. Scammers sometimes contact their victims again, pretending to be a "recovery agent" or a bank official who can get the money back for a fee. That is a second scam. No genuine recovery process asks you to pay upfront.

## Selling on Facebook Marketplace as a Real Business

If you run a small business in Nigeria and you sell on Marketplace regularly, treat it like a real sales channel, not a side hustle. Take your own clear photos in good light, from several angles. Write honest descriptions that include the condition, the size, the colour, the location, and what is included. Reply to messages quickly — most buyers message three sellers at once and buy from whoever replies first. Use WhatsApp Business for follow-ups, with a catalogue and quick replies, but keep the first contact on Facebook so there is a record.

Price your items fairly and state whether the price is fixed or negotiable. Listings with clear prices get more genuine enquiries than listings that say "DM for price". Keep a simple record of what you sold, to whom, and for how much — even a notebook or a spreadsheet — so you can track your best-selling items and your real profit after delivery costs.

Finally, build your reputation. Ask happy buyers to leave a rating on your Marketplace profile, and keep your response rate high. A seller with ratings and a history of completed sales is trusted far more than a new account with no history — and that trust is worth money, because it lets you charge a fair price instead of competing with scammers on price alone.

## Your Safety Checklist Before Every Marketplace Deal

Before you send any money or hand over any item, run through this list. Is the price realistic? Have you checked the seller's profile and seen a real person with history? Have you seen the item on a live video call or in person? Are you meeting in a busy public place during the day? Are you paying into an account in the seller's own name? Will you confirm the payment in your own bank app before the item changes hands? If the answer to any of these is no, pause the deal. A genuine bargain will still be there after you do your checks. A scam will not survive them.

Facebook Marketplace can be a great place to find a bargain or grow a small business in Nigeria. The deals are real, and so are the risks. The difference between a good deal and a painful lesson is usually two minutes of checking and the discipline to never pay before you see the item. Use these rules every time, and Marketplace becomes what it was meant to be: a market, not a trap.
