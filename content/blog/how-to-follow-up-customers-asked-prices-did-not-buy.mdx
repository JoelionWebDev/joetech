Every Nigerian seller knows this pattern well. A customer sends a message: "How much is this?" You reply with the price, maybe with a photo and some extra details. The customer reads it. Then nothing. No reply, no order, no goodbye. You check the chat the next day. Still nothing. You wonder whether your price was too high, whether they found a cheaper seller, or whether they simply forgot.

The truth is that most of these customers are not rejecting you. They are comparing prices, checking their budget, waiting for salary day, or asking their spouse. Some of them are still deciding between you and another seller. Some of them meant to come back and got distracted by life. In Nigeria, where many purchases are planned around payday and where customers routinely ask three or four sellers for the same product before deciding, a customer who asked for your price is a warm lead. They have already shown interest. The sale is not lost. It is waiting.

That is why follow-up matters so much. The businesses that sell consistently are rarely the ones with the lowest prices. They are the ones that stay in touch politely, remind the customer at the right time, and make it easy to say yes. This guide shows you how to follow up with customers who asked for prices but did not buy, without sounding desperate, without annoying them, and without wasting your own time.

## Understand Why Customers Go Quiet

Before you write a single follow-up message, it helps to understand what is happening on the customer's side. Most of the time, silence does not mean rejection. It means the customer is not ready yet, and your job is to be there when they are.

The most common reason is price comparison. A customer who wants to buy a wig, a phone, a cake, or a pair of shoes will often message several sellers in the same afternoon. They are collecting prices and weighing their options. If you are the first to reply, they may still be waiting for the other sellers to answer. If you are the last, they may already be deciding. Either way, a polite follow-up a day or two later puts you back at the top of their mind at the moment they are making their decision.

The second reason is money timing. Many Nigerians shop around payday, or after a business deal lands, or when a particular bill has been paid. A customer who asks for a price on the 20th of the month may be planning to buy on the 28th when salary arrives. If you write them off after two days of silence, you lose a sale that was simply waiting for the right date.

The third reason is that the customer needs permission or agreement. They may need to check with a spouse, a parent, or a business partner. They may be buying for someone else and need to confirm the size, the colour, or the budget. These conversations take time, and the customer may forget to come back to you while they sort it out.

The fourth reason is distraction. Life in Nigeria is busy. A customer may have been chatting with you on a bus, then arrived at their destination and forgotten the conversation entirely. A gentle reminder is often all it takes.

The final reason, and the one sellers fear most, is that the price was genuinely too high for that customer. Even then, the customer is not lost forever. They may come back when they have saved more, or they may be interested in a cheaper alternative you can offer. A follow-up that offers a lower-priced option, or a payment plan, can save a sale that seemed dead.

Understanding these reasons changes how you follow up. You stop seeing silence as rejection, and you start seeing it as a normal part of how Nigerians buy. Your follow-up becomes a service, not a chase.

## When to Follow Up

Timing is the difference between a welcome reminder and an annoyance. Follow up too soon and you look desperate. Follow up too late and the customer has already bought from someone else.

A good rhythm for most small businesses is this. Send the first follow-up the next day, or one full day after the customer asked for the price. If they asked on Monday afternoon, message them on Tuesday afternoon or Wednesday morning. That gap is long enough to feel polite, and short enough that they still remember the conversation.

If they do not reply to the first follow-up, wait three to four days and send a second one. This second message should add something new, not just repeat the first. Tell them about a new colour, a restock, a delivery option, or a small discount. Give them a reason to reply beyond your asking.

If they still do not reply, send one final message after another week. This one should be short, warm, and easy to answer. Something like: "No pressure at all. If you still need the item later, I am here to help." That message leaves the door open without chasing the customer.

After that, stop. Three follow-ups are enough. Continuing to message a person who has not replied will not win the sale, and it will damage your reputation. The customer may block you, or they may remember you as the seller who would not stop messaging. Either way, you lose more than the sale. The customer may come back in a month, ready to buy, but only if your last message to them was polite.

There are two exceptions to this rhythm. If the product is limited, such as a single piece of an item, a seasonal product, or a service with a fixed date, you can follow up sooner and say so honestly. For example, "Just to let you know, we only have two pieces of this left." That is useful information, not pressure, as long as it is true. Never invent scarcity. Customers talk to each other, and a false claim about stock will be found out.

The second exception is when the customer told you a specific date. If they said "I will get back to you on Friday when I get paid," follow up on Friday, not before. Keeping your word about timing builds trust, and trust is what turns a one-time buyer into a regular customer.

## What to Say in a Follow-Up Message

The words you use matter as much as the timing. A good follow-up message has three qualities: it is short, it is helpful, and it makes replying easy.

Start by greeting the customer by name if you know it. A message that begins "Good morning, Ada" feels personal. A message that begins "Hello" feels like a broadcast. If you do not know their name, a simple "Good morning" is fine, but try to learn names. Customers notice when you remember them.

Next, remind them gently what they asked about. Do not assume they remember. Say something like, "You asked about the black handbag yesterday." That single sentence reconnects the conversation and saves the customer from scrolling back through the chat.

Then add something useful. This is the part most sellers skip, and it is the part that makes a follow-up work. Useful can mean many things: confirming that the item is still in stock, mentioning a new colour, explaining delivery to their area, offering a cheaper alternative, or sharing a short video of the product being used. The point is that your message gives the customer something, instead of only asking them for a decision.

Finally, end with a simple question that is easy to answer. "Would you like me to reserve one for you?" is easy. "Do you have any questions I can help with?" is easy. "Have you decided?" is not easy, because it puts the customer on the spot. Make the next step small and low-pressure.

Here are a few examples you can adapt. For a general check-in: "Good morning, Tunde. You asked about the sneakers yesterday. They are still available in your size, and delivery to Ikeja is available this week. Would you like me to hold a pair for you?" For a customer who said the price was high: "Good afternoon. I was thinking about our chat, and I have a similar style at a lower price that you might like. Would you like me to send you photos?" For a customer who said they would come back later: "Hello, I hope you are well. You mentioned you would check back this week about the cake order. I am here whenever you are ready, and I am happy to answer any questions."

Notice what these messages have in common. They are short. They are polite. They offer something. They do not beg, they do not guilt-trip the customer, and they do not ask "Why haven't you replied?" That question, even when asked politely, makes the customer feel judged, and judged customers do not buy.

## Keep a Simple Follow-Up System

Follow-up fails most often not because sellers do not know what to say, but because they forget who to follow up with. When you are chatting with twenty customers a day, it is impossible to remember who asked for a price on Monday and who is waiting for payday on Friday. You need a simple system.

A notebook is enough. At the end of each day, write down the name or number of each customer who asked for a price but did not buy, what they asked about, and the date you should follow up. A small table with four columns — name, product, price quoted, follow-up date — will do. If you prefer your phone, a simple spreadsheet or a notes app works the same way.

Each morning, check the list and send the follow-ups that are due. Do this before you start posting new content or answering new enquiries. Follow-up is the highest-value work you can do in the morning, because these are customers who have already shown interest. A new post may bring new people, but a follow-up brings back people who are already close to buying.

If you have a team, make follow-up someone's specific job. When follow-up is everyone's job, it becomes nobody's job. Assign it to one person, give them the list, and check at the end of the day how many follow-ups went out and how many turned into orders.

Over time, your list will teach you useful things. You will notice which products get many enquiries but few sales, which may mean the price is too high or the product photos are not convincing. You will notice which days customers reply most, and which follow-up messages get the best response. Use what you learn to improve your pricing, your photos, and your messages.

## Offer Alternatives Instead of Discounts

When a customer does not buy after asking for the price, the temptation is to immediately offer a discount. Sometimes a discount is the right move, but it should not be your first move. Constant discounting trains customers to wait for a lower price, and it eats into your profit.

Before offering a discount, try offering an alternative. If the customer wanted a premium product, show them a similar one at a lower price. If they wanted a large quantity, offer a smaller pack. If they wanted a full service, offer a basic version. Many customers are not looking for the cheapest option. They are looking for an option that fits their budget. An alternative gives them a way to buy from you without you cutting your price.

If you do offer a discount, make it small, specific, and time-bound. For example, "I can offer you a small discount if you order before Friday." That is better than an open-ended price cut, because it gives the customer a reason to decide now, and it protects your margin. Never offer a discount in your first follow-up message. Offer it in the second or third, after you have tried other approaches.

Payment plans are another powerful alternative in Nigeria. Many customers can afford a product if they can pay in two parts. Offering to split the payment, with a small deposit to reserve the item and the balance on delivery, can turn a "too expensive" into a sale. Be clear about the terms, put them in writing, and only offer instalments to customers you trust or who have paid a deposit.

Bundles work well too. A customer who hesitates at the price of a single item may say yes to a bundle that includes something extra at a small additional cost. For example, a hair seller might offer a wig plus a free bonnet, or a skincare seller might offer a set of three products at a slightly better total price than buying them separately. The bundle feels like value, and it increases the size of the sale.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many times should I follow up with a customer who asked for a price?

Three times is a good rule. The first follow-up comes one day after the enquiry, the second comes three to four days later with something new to offer, and the third comes about a week after that as a final friendly message. After three follow-ups with no reply, stop messaging that customer about that product. You can still send them general updates through your broadcast list or channel, but do not keep chasing them directly.

### What if the customer says my price is too high?

Do not argue and do not panic. Thank them for telling you, explain briefly what makes your product worth the price, and offer an alternative, such as a similar product at a lower price, a smaller quantity, or a payment plan. If you can offer a small discount, do it as a second step, not the first. And remember that some customers simply cannot afford your product right now. That is not a rejection of you. They may return later when their budget allows.

### Should I follow up by text or by voice note?

Both work, and voice notes often work better because they sound warmer and more personal. Use text for the first follow-up if you have never spoken to the customer by voice, and switch to voice notes once the conversation is flowing. Always send prices, account numbers, and order details as text, even if you discuss them by voice, so the customer has a written record they can refer back to.

### What if a customer reads my message and does not reply?

That happens often, and it does not mean they are angry with you. They may be busy, still deciding, or waiting for payday. Do not send another message immediately, and never ask why they read your message without replying. Wait for your next scheduled follow-up and add something new, such as a restock, a new colour, or a delivery option. If they do not reply after three follow-ups, leave them alone politely.

### Is it okay to offer a discount in a follow-up message?

Yes, but use discounts carefully. Offer a small, specific discount with a clear time limit, rather than an open-ended price cut. Try offering an alternative product or a payment plan first, and use the discount as a later step. If you discount too often, customers will learn to wait for a discount instead of buying at your normal price, and your profit will shrink.

## What to Do Next

1. **Build your follow-up list today.** Go through your WhatsApp chats from the last seven days and write down every customer who asked for a price but did not buy. Record their name, the product, the price you quoted, and the date. This list is your most valuable sales tool for the week.

2. **Send your first round of follow-ups tomorrow morning.** Message each person on the list with a short, polite note that reminds them of the product, adds something useful, and ends with an easy question. Do this before you post new content or answer new enquiries.

3. **Set up a simple daily routine.** Every morning, spend fifteen minutes checking who is due for a follow-up and sending those messages. Track how many replies you get and how many turn into orders. After two weeks, you will see clearly which follow-up messages work best, and you can use that knowledge to sell more from the enquiries you already have.
