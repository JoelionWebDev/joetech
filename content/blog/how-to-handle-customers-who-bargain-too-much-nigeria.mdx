If you sell anything in Nigeria, you know this moment well. A customer picks up your product, asks the price, and immediately says, "Ah, that is too much. Give me half price." You quote ₦15,000 for a dress. They offer ₦6,000. You quote ₦8,000 for a pair of shoes. They say, "My last price is ₦3,000." Sometimes they smile. Sometimes they threaten to walk away. Sometimes they bring a friend to help them bargain harder.

Bargaining is part of Nigerian buying culture. In markets from Balogun to Onitsha Main Market, from Wuse to Dugbe, customers expect to negotiate. Many customers enjoy it. For some, bargaining is a habit they learned from childhood. For others, it is a way to test whether your price is fair. It does not always mean they cannot afford your product. It often means they want to feel they got a good deal.

The problem is not bargaining itself. The problem is when bargaining eats your profit, wastes your time, or leaves you angry and frustrated. A business cannot survive if every sale is made at a loss. This guide will help you handle customers who bargain too much with calm, respect, and firmness. You will learn why customers bargain, how to set prices that leave room to negotiate, what to say in the moment, when to walk away, and how to keep the customer even when you do not agree on price.

## Why Nigerian Customers Bargain So Hard

To handle bargaining well, you need to understand why it happens. The first reason is culture and habit. In open markets, prices are rarely fixed. A trader quotes a high price, the customer offers a low price, and they meet in the middle. Customers carry this habit into shops, Instagram stores, and WhatsApp businesses, even when prices are written down. They bargain because that is how they have always bought things.

The second reason is fear of being cheated. Many customers have been overcharged before. They have paid ₦20,000 for an item they later saw for ₦12,000 in another shop. They have bought a fake product at the price of an original. Bargaining is their way of protecting themselves. When they push your price down, they are trying to make sure they are not paying too much.

The third reason is real money pressure. Times are hard for many Nigerians. Fuel, food, school fees, and rent have all risen. A customer may genuinely have only ₦5,000 in their pocket for an item you sell for ₦7,500. They are not trying to insult you. They are trying to fit the purchase into a tight budget. Understanding this helps you stay patient, even when the offer is very low.

The fourth reason is the joy of the deal. Some customers feel proud when they negotiate a lower price. They will tell their friends, "I got it for ₦4,000, not ₦7,000." That story matters to them. If you give a small discount with a smile, you give them that story, and they will remember your shop fondly.

## Set Your Prices Before the Customer Arrives

The best way to handle bargaining is to prepare before the customer starts. A business without a clear pricing plan will always struggle, because every negotiation becomes a guess.

First, know your true cost. For each product, add the cost of buying it, transport, packaging, data, rent, and your time. If a dress costs you ₦8,000 to buy and ₦1,000 to bring to your shop, your total cost is ₦9,000. If you sell it for ₦9,500, your profit is only ₦500. That is too small to survive on, especially if you later give a discount. You need to know this number before you quote any price.

Second, set a floor price. This is the lowest price you can accept without losing money or working for nothing. Using the dress example, if your cost is ₦9,000, your floor price might be ₦11,500. That leaves a small profit and room for a discount. Never go below your floor price, no matter how hard the customer pushes. If you do, you are paying the customer to take your product.

Third, set a starting price that leaves room to negotiate. In Nigeria, many sellers quote a price slightly above the price they truly want, because they know the customer will bargain. If you want ₦12,000 for the dress, you might quote ₦14,000. The customer bargains down to ₦12,000, feels happy, and you still earn your profit. This is not cheating, as long as your starting price is fair and your final price is honest. It becomes cheating only when you inflate the price far beyond the product's value.

Fourth, write your prices down. Keep a price list in your shop, on your WhatsApp Business catalogue, or in a notebook. When prices are written, customers bargain less aggressively, because they see the price is not made up on the spot. A written price also helps your staff charge the same amount when you are away.

Fifth, decide your discount rules in advance. Will you give 5% off for cash payment? Will you give free delivery for orders above ₦20,000? Will you give a small discount to a customer who buys three items? Decide these rules before the rush, and apply them fairly. When you have rules, you do not have to decide under pressure.

## What to Say When a Customer Bargains Too Hard

Words matter. The same refusal can sound rude or respectful, depending on how you say it. Here are practical phrases you can use, with examples.

When a customer offers far too little, stay calm and smile. Say, "Thank you, ma. I understand you want a good price. The lowest I can do for this one is ₦12,000, because the quality is high and my cost is already close to that." This is firm, polite, and gives a reason. It does not insult the customer, and it does not leave room for endless argument.

When a customer says, "Another shop sells it for half this price," do not argue. Say, "You are right, prices differ from shop to shop, sir. Our own is original, and we give you a guarantee. If you buy from us, we will attend to you if there is any problem." This shifts the conversation from price to value. You are not saying the other shop is lying. You are explaining why your price is different.

When a customer says, "I am your regular customer, give me a better price," thank them for their loyalty. Say, "Thank you for always coming to us, ma. Because you are our regular, I can do ₦11,500 for you today, but I cannot go lower than that." This rewards loyalty without destroying your profit. Keep a small loyalty discount for regulars, but do not give it to everyone who claims to be a regular.

When a customer threatens to leave, let them go politely. Say, "No problem, sir. Thank you for coming. If you change your mind, we are here." Many customers who walk away will return in ten minutes, or the next day, once they compare prices elsewhere. If you chase them, beg them, or reduce your price in panic, they will learn that threatening works, and they will use it every time.

When a customer bargains for a long time, set a gentle limit. Say, "Ma, I have given you my best price now. I cannot go lower, but I can add a small gift for you," or "I can give you free delivery within Lekki today." An extra, such as a small gift, free delivery, or a longer warranty, can close the sale without cutting your price further. Customers often accept an extra happily, because it feels like a win.

Use the customer's name when you can, keep your voice calm, and never raise your voice. Never say, "You cannot afford it," or "Go and buy from the other shop." Those words end the relationship. Even if the customer does not buy today, they may buy tomorrow, or they may send someone else. Leave every conversation with respect intact.

## Use Value, Not Only Price, to Win the Sale

Customers who focus only on price often forget value. Your job is to remind them, gently, what they are paying for.

Explain quality in simple words. Instead of saying "This is quality," show it. Let the customer touch the fabric, check the stitching, test the zip, or see the sole. Say, "This leather will last you two years if you care for it. The cheaper one may tear in three months." A customer who understands the difference will often accept a higher price.

Explain your service. If you offer a guarantee, free adjustment, delivery, or after-sales support, say so. For example, a tailor who offers free adjustment within one week is offering more than a tailor who does not. A phone accessories seller who tests the charger before the customer leaves is offering more than a seller who does not. These services have value, and they justify your price.

Explain the cost honestly when it helps. You do not need to show your supplier invoice, but you can say, "The price of this fabric has gone up in the market, ma. I bought it at a higher price this month." Most Nigerian customers understand rising costs, because they face the same costs in their own lives. Honesty about cost can reduce pressure.

Offer choices instead of a single price. If a customer cannot afford the ₦15,000 dress, show them a similar dress for ₦10,000, or a smaller size, or a different colour on offer. Giving a choice keeps the customer in your shop. Saying only "Take it or leave it" pushes them out. Choices respect their budget while protecting your profit.

## How to Handle Different Types of Hard Bargainers

Not all hard bargainers are the same. Different types need different responses.

The friendly joker bargains with laughter and stories. They may say, "My sister, you want to kill me with this price." Laugh with them, stay warm, and hold your price. Say, "I will not kill you, sir. I will give you my best price, ₦12,000, and you will look good." Humour can keep the conversation light, but do not let laughter push you below your floor price.

The silent bargainer says little. They pick up the item, look at it, ask the price, and then go quiet or shake their head. Do not panic and drop your price immediately. Give them space. Say, "Take your time, ma. Check it well." Silence is often a tactic. If you wait calmly, they will often speak first and make a reasonable offer.

The comparer always mentions another shop. "In Computer Village, they sell it for ₦5,000." Respond calmly and focus on what you offer. Say, "Thank you for telling me. Our price includes a test before you leave and a one-week exchange if it has a fault. That is why it is ₦7,000 here." If their claim is true and your price is truly too high, review your pricing after they leave. If it is not true, stay firm.

The bulk buyer wants a large discount because they are buying many items. This can be a good customer, if you handle it well. Offer a small discount per item, not a large one. For example, if they buy ten items, offer 5% off the total, or one free small item. Calculate the total profit before you agree. A bulk sale at a loss is worse than no sale.

## When to Walk Away, and When to Give a Small Discount

Knowing when to stop negotiating is a skill. If you keep negotiating forever, you will waste time you could use to serve other customers. If you stop too early, you may lose a sale you could have closed.

Walk away politely when the customer's offer is below your cost, when they have made the same low offer three times without moving, or when they are rude or abusive. Say, "Thank you for coming, ma. I cannot sell at that price, but you are welcome anytime." Do not feel guilty. A sale at a loss harms your business and teaches the customer to expect that loss-making price again.

Give a small discount when the customer is close to your price, when they are a loyal regular, when they are buying several items, when they pay in cash and save you transfer charges or POS delay, or when you need to clear old stock. A discount of 5% to 10% is usually enough. A discount of 40% or 50% is rarely wise, unless the item is old stock you must clear.

Never give a discount in anger or in panic. If you feel pressured, take a breath, smile, and repeat your best price. If the customer is still pushing, offer an extra instead of a lower price. An extra costs you less than a deep discount and often satisfies the customer more.

## Protect Your Profit Without Losing Customers

Profit is not greed. It is what keeps your business alive, pays your rent, buys new stock, and feeds your family. A business that sells at a loss will close, and then it cannot serve any customer.

Set a daily profit target, not only a sales target. For example, if you sell ₦50,000 worth of goods in a day, but your cost was ₦42,000, your profit is ₦8,000. That is the number that matters. Track it daily in a notebook or a simple sheet on your phone. When you see your profit clearly, you will bargain less carelessly, because you will know what each discount costs you.

Do not compete only on price. If another seller nearby sells cheaper, do not try to beat them by selling at a loss. Compete on quality, service, cleanliness, speed, and trust. Customers who buy only because you are cheapest will leave the moment someone else is cheaper. Customers who buy because you are reliable will stay.

Train your staff to handle bargaining the same way you do. Many businesses lose money because a staff member, under pressure, gives a deep discount to end a difficult conversation. Teach your staff your floor prices, your discount rules, and the phrases to use. Give them permission to call you when a customer pushes hard. A staff member who knows the rules will not panic.

Review your prices every month. Costs change in Nigeria. Transport, data, and supplier prices rise. If your costs rise and your prices stay the same, your profit will disappear quietly. Check your suppliers, check your competitors, and adjust your prices fairly. Tell regular customers in advance when a price must rise, and explain why. Most will understand.

## A Simple 30-Day Plan to Bargain Better

You can improve how you handle bargaining in one month, without changing your products or your shop.

In week one, calculate your costs and set floor prices for your ten best-selling products. Write them in a notebook and keep it near your till. Tell your staff the floor prices and the rule: never sell below the floor.

In week two, practise your responses. Write down five phrases you will use when customers bargain hard, such as "My best price is..." and "I can add a small gift instead." Practise saying them calmly with a smile. Role-play with a friend or staff member if you can. The more you practise, the easier it becomes in a real sale.

In week three, track every discount you give. Write the product, the starting price, the final price, and the reason for the discount. At the end of the week, add up the total discounts. Ask yourself whether each discount was worth it. Keep the good discounts, and stop the ones that were given in panic.

In week four, review your results. Did your profit improve? Did you lose fewer sales? Did customers still return? Ask a few regular customers whether they feel your prices are fair. Use their answers to adjust your prices and your approach. Then continue the habit into the next month.

Bargaining will always be part of selling in Nigeria. You cannot stop customers from trying, and you should not try to. Your job is to handle it with confidence, respect, and a clear knowledge of your costs. Set your floor price, explain your value, stay calm, offer a small extra when it helps, and walk away politely when the price is too low. Do this consistently, and you will protect your profit, keep your customers, and build a business that lasts.
