Many Nigerian small business owners work hard every day but still do not know if they made a profit at the end of the month. Money comes in from sales, money goes out for stock, transport, fuel, and food, and by month end the cash is gone. Without a budget, you cannot tell whether the business is growing, struggling, or quietly losing money.

A budget is not a complicated accounting document. It is a simple plan that says how much money you expect to receive, how much you plan to spend, and how much you want to keep as profit or savings. It helps you make better decisions, such as when to buy stock, when to raise prices, and when to cut an expense that is eating your profit.

This guide shows you how to create a monthly budget for your small business in Nigeria, step by step. You will learn how to list your income, group your expenses, set aside money for rent, power, and emergencies, and review your budget at month end. The examples cover a food vendor in Surulere, a fashion seller in Lekki, a barbing salon in Ikeja, and a POS agent in Abuja. The same steps work for traders, service providers, and online sellers.

## Why a Budget Matters More in Nigeria

Prices in Nigeria change often. The cost of fuel, food items, fabric, and imported goods can rise within weeks. A product you bought for one price last month may cost more this month. Without a budget, those price rises eat your profit quietly, and you only notice when you cannot afford to restock. A budget forces you to check your costs regularly and adjust your prices before it is too late.

Income is often irregular for Nigerian small businesses. A caterer may earn a lot in December during wedding season and very little in January. A fashion seller may sell well at month end when salaries are paid, and slowly in the middle of the month. A budget helps you plan for slow weeks by saving from good weeks, instead of spending everything when sales are high and struggling when sales drop.

Power costs are another Nigerian reality. Many businesses pay for electricity, fuel for a generator, and sometimes solar charging. These costs change with fuel prices and with how often power supply fails. A budget gives these costs a proper place, so you do not treat fuel as a small, forgettable expense when it is actually one of your biggest monthly costs.

A budget also protects you from mixing business money with personal money. Many small business owners pay school fees, buy food, and handle family expenses from the same cash as the business. That makes it impossible to know if the business is profitable. A budget separates the two by giving the owner a fixed monthly payment from the business, just like a salary, instead of taking cash whenever it is needed.

Finally, a budget helps you plan for growth. If you want to buy a new freezer, rent a bigger shop, or stock more goods for December, you need to know how much you can save each month. A budget turns a vague wish into a clear plan with a monthly savings amount and a date when you can afford it.

## Step 1: Work Out Your Real Monthly Income

Start your budget with income, because you cannot plan spending until you know what comes in. Look at your sales for the last three months, not just last week. Add the three months together and divide by three. That gives you an average monthly income that is more reliable than one good week or one bad week.

Be honest about your income. Count only money you actually received, not money customers promised to pay later. If a customer bought on credit and has not paid, do not count that money in your budget until it arrives. Many businesses look profitable on paper because they count unpaid credit sales, but the cash is not there to buy stock or pay rent.

List your income by source if you have more than one. A food vendor might earn from daily sales, weekend catering, and drinks. A fashion seller might earn from shop sales, WhatsApp orders, and Instagram orders. Listing each source separately shows you which part of the business brings in the most money, and which part needs more attention.

If your business is new and you have no sales history, start with a careful estimate. Look at what similar businesses in your area earn, and start with a low estimate rather than a hopeful one. It is better to plan for less income and be pleasantly surprised than to plan for high income and run out of cash. Review your estimate after your first month and adjust it with real figures.

Do not forget irregular income. If you earn a commission, a seasonal bonus, or money from a side service once in a while, note it separately. Do not build your regular monthly spending around money that may not come. Use irregular income for savings, debt repayment, or a one-off purchase, not for rent or food that must be paid every month.

## Step 2: List Every Business Expense

Next, list every expense your business pays in a month. Start with fixed expenses, which stay roughly the same each month. These include shop rent, salaries for staff, internet data, and any regular subscription. Even if you pay rent yearly, divide the yearly rent by twelve and include that monthly amount in your budget, so you are saving towards the next payment all year.

Then list variable expenses, which change from month to month. These include stock or raw materials, transport and delivery, fuel for your generator, packaging, and market levies. Look at your receipts, bank alerts, and notebook records for the last three months. Add them up and work out a monthly average for each item, just as you did for income.

Do not forget small expenses that add up. A POS charge here, a small transport fare there, a bottle of water for a customer, or a phone recharge for business calls can total a large amount by month end. Write them down for one full month and you may be surprised. Small leaks sink a budget faster than one big expense, because they are easy to ignore.

Include a line for your own pay. As the owner, you should pay yourself a fixed amount each month, even if the business is small. This amount should cover your personal needs, and it should be realistic for what the business can afford. Paying yourself a fixed amount stops you from taking cash randomly, and it makes your personal spending easier to control.

Finally, add a line for savings and emergencies. Set aside a small amount each month, even if it is only a few thousand naira. This money covers broken equipment, a sudden price rise, or a slow sales week. A business without emergency savings must borrow or close when a problem appears. A small monthly saving, kept in a separate account or a locked savings app, gives you room to handle surprises without panic.

## Step 3: Put the Budget Together on Paper or a Sheet

You do not need expensive software to make a budget. A notebook, a Google Sheet on your phone, or a simple table in a notes app is enough. The best tool is the one you will actually use every week. Choose a method that feels easy, because a budget you never open is worse than no budget at all.

Draw a simple table with three columns: item, planned amount, and actual amount. Under income, list each income source and the total. Under expenses, list each expense group and the total. At the bottom, subtract total expenses from total income. The result is your expected profit or your expected shortfall. If the result is negative, your planned spending is higher than your expected income, and you need to cut something before the month starts.

Use clear groups for expenses so you can see where money goes. Useful groups for Nigerian small businesses are stock and materials, rent, power and fuel, transport and delivery, staff pay, marketing and data, owner pay, and savings. You can add or remove groups to fit your business, but keep the list short enough to review in a few minutes.

Write your budget before the month starts, ideally in the last week of the previous month. A budget made after spending has already happened is only a record, not a plan. Sit down with your notebook, your bank alerts, and your sales records, and decide how much you will spend on each group next month. That decision is the heart of budgeting.

Keep the budget where you can see it. Stick a copy on your shop wall, keep it in your phone notes, or save it in Google Sheets where you can open it on any device. A budget hidden in a drawer will be forgotten. A budget you see every day will guide your spending choices, such as whether to buy extra stock or wait until next week.

## Step 4: Track Spending Every Week

A budget only works if you track what you actually spend. At the end of each week, write down what you spent in each group and compare it with the planned amount. This weekly check takes less than thirty minutes, and it catches problems early, while you still have time to fix them.

Record spending on the same day it happens when you can. Keep a small notebook by your till, or use a notes app on your phone. Write the date, the item, and the amount. A short note like "Fuel, 5 litres" is enough. Waiting until month end to remember everything leads to missing items and a budget that does not match reality.

Use bank alerts and transfer history to check your records. Your bank app shows transfers, POS payments, and withdrawals. Compare those with your notebook once a week. If you find spending you forgot to record, add it. The goal is not perfection, but a clear picture of where money actually goes.

When a group goes over budget, do not panic. Find out why. Did stock prices rise? Did you buy extra goods for a coming event? Did fuel cost more because power supply was poor? Understanding the reason helps you decide what to do: cut spending in another group, adjust next month's budget, or raise prices if costs have risen permanently.

Celebrate when you stay within budget. If you planned to spend a certain amount on transport and you spent less, move the saving to your emergency fund or your growth savings. Small wins build the habit of budgeting, and the habit matters more than any single month's figures. A business owner who reviews their budget weekly will make better decisions than one who only looks at it once a year.

## Step 5: Review at Month End and Adjust for Next Month

At the end of each month, sit down and compare your planned budget with your actual income and spending. Write the actual amounts next to the planned amounts. Note the groups where you spent more, the groups where you spent less, and whether your income met your expectation. This review is the most valuable part of budgeting, because it teaches you how your business really behaves.

Ask yourself three questions. First, did I earn what I expected, and if not, why? Second, which expenses were higher than planned, and were they necessary? Third, what will I change next month? Write the answers down. A short note such as "Fuel was high because of power cuts, so I will increase the fuel budget and reduce marketing next month" is a useful plan, not just a complaint.

Adjust next month's budget based on what you learned. If stock prices have risen, increase the stock budget and consider raising your selling prices. If a slow season is coming, reduce your income estimate and cut non-essential spending early. If you saved money in one group, decide whether to save it, use it to buy more stock, or pay off a debt. Every month teaches you something, and a good budget gets more accurate over time.

Look at your profit over three months, not just one month. One good month can hide a weak business, and one bad month can hide a growing one. A three-month view shows the real trend. If profit is growing slowly, your budget is working. If profit is falling, you need to act: cut costs, raise prices, or find more customers before the business runs into trouble.

Keep your old budgets. A file of past monthly budgets, even in a simple notebook, becomes a valuable record. It shows how your business has grown, what seasons are strong, and what your real costs are. When you apply for a loan, talk to a supplier, or plan to expand, those records prove that you understand your business and manage money carefully.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much should a small business save each month?

Start with what you can afford, even if it is a small amount. Many advisers suggest saving a tenth of your income, but for a small Nigerian business, a realistic start is any fixed amount you can set aside every month without fail. The habit matters more than the amount. Increase the saving as income grows, and keep the savings in a separate account so you are not tempted to spend it.

### Do I need an accountant to make a budget?

No, you can make a simple monthly budget yourself with a notebook or a Google Sheet. An accountant is useful for tax, loans, and larger businesses, but a small business budget is about knowing your income and expenses and planning your spending. If your business grows large or your records become complex, then consider hiring an accountant to help you review them.

### What if my income changes every month?

That is normal for many Nigerian small businesses. Use the average of the last three months as your planned income, and plan your essential spending, such as rent and stock, around your lowest recent month, not your best month. In good months, save the extra. In slow months, use the savings to cover essential costs. This way, irregular income does not cause a crisis.

### Should I include my salary in the business budget?

Yes. Pay yourself a fixed amount each month and list it as an expense, just like staff pay. This stops you from taking cash whenever you need it, and it shows you the true cost of running the business. If the business cannot afford to pay you a reasonable amount, that is important information. It means prices, costs, or sales need to change.

### How do I handle debt in my budget?

List loan repayments and money owed to suppliers as fixed expenses, and pay them on time. Do not hide debt by leaving it out of the budget. If debt payments take a large part of your income, focus on paying them down before spending on new stock or expansion. A clear budget shows you how much debt you can afford and when it will be finished.

## What to Do Next

1. Gather your sales records, bank alerts, and notebook for the last three months.
2. Work out your average monthly income and write it at the top of a new budget page.
3. List your fixed expenses, your variable expenses, your owner pay, and a small amount for savings.
4. Subtract total expenses from total income and check whether the result is a profit or a shortfall.
5. If the result is a shortfall, cut a non-essential expense or plan a price review before the month starts.
6. Track every expense for one week, compare it with your plan, and adjust your spending while there is still time.
7. At month end, compare planned and actual figures, note what you learned, and write next month's budget using those lessons.
