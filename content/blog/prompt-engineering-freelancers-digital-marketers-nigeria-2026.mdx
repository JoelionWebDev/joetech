Two freelancers get the same message on a Tuesday morning. A grill house in Lekki Phase 1 wants a month of Instagram content: twelve posts, captions included, plus a flyer for a Friday suya night. The budget is ₦150,000. The deadline is Friday.

The first freelancer opens ChatGPT and types, "Write 12 Instagram captions for a restaurant." By noon she has twelve captions. They are smooth, they are generic, and they could advertise any grill house in any city. She sends them on Thursday. The client replies on Friday morning: "These don't sound like us. Can you redo?"

The second freelancer spends the first hour doing something that looks like nothing. She reads the client's past posts, notes how the owner talks, and asks for the menu and the prices. Then she builds her prompts around that brief. Her twelve captions mention the ₦4,500 suya platter, the Friday crowd that spills onto the pavement, and the pepper mix the owner is quietly proud of. The client approves them on the first pass.

The difference between the two is not talent. It is where the prompting starts. The first freelancer prompted the deliverable. The second prompted the whole job.

## A client job has three acts

Most freelancers treat prompting as a writing trick. The stronger way to think about it is as a job in three acts, and each act needs a different kind of prompt.

Act one is the brief. This is where most jobs are won or lost, and it is the act most people skip. A client says "make it pop" or "something catchy" and the freelancer nods and starts typing. Three days later the client hates the work, and both sides think the other one failed. The brief was never understood. It was only received.

Act two is the deliverable. This is the visible work — the captions, the product descriptions, the ad copy, the flyer text. Good prompts here are what everyone talks about. They matter, but they sit on top of act one. A perfect prompt built on a misunderstood brief produces perfect work for the wrong job.

Act three is the revision. The client wants changes. Sometimes the feedback is precise: "the price is wrong, it is ₦6,000 now." Sometimes it is fog: "my MD hates it" or "make it more premium." Revision prompts are a separate skill, because they have to hold the original brief steady while changing one thing inside it. Freelancers who cannot prompt a revision end up rewriting from scratch, burning the hours they saved in act two.

Run all three acts with prompts, and the AI becomes a working system instead of a party trick. Here is how each one works, with the templates to copy.

## Act one: turning a vague brief into a working one

The most valuable prompt in client work is not the one that writes the caption. It is the one that turns a two-line WhatsApp message into a brief you can actually build on.

When the grill house owner says "make it pop," the freelancer who understands prompting does not argue. She asks the AI to interview the brief. She pastes the client's message, plus anything else she has — the menu, the Instagram page, a voice note transcript — and asks for the gaps:

> "You are a senior account manager at a Lagos digital agency. Here is everything a client has told me about a job [paste messages, menu, links]. Turn it into a structured brief with these sections: the business and what it sells, the audience, the one goal of this job, the brand voice in three words, the facts I still need, and five questions I should ask the client before I start. Flag anything in the client's message that is ambiguous. Keep it under 300 words, plain language."

What comes back is not the work. It is the shape of the work, plus a list of questions. Send those questions to the client. "Who is the Friday crowd — families, young guys, couples?" "Is the ₦4,500 suya platter the thing to push, or the new ₦7,500 family pack?" Five answers like that are worth more than five hours of drafting, because they stop the revision loop before it starts.

## Act two: drafting the deliverable without losing the client

With a real brief in hand, the deliverable prompts get easier, because the context layer is already written. The template below is the one to keep. It is built for captions, but the skeleton works for product descriptions, ad copy, and email sequences too — swap the task and the facts, keep the structure.

> "You are a social media copywriter for Lagos food and hospitality brands. Write 12 Instagram captions for [client], a grill house in Lekki Phase 1. Audience: young professionals 24–35 who come for Friday suya nights. Products to feature: the ₦4,500 suya platter, the ₦7,500 family pack, and the Friday night offer. Brand voice: warm, confident, a little playful — never slang that feels forced. Each caption under 90 words: a scroll-stopping first line, one sensory detail about the food, one naira-specific benefit, and a call to action that says 'DM to order or walk in.' No hype words we cannot prove. Here is a caption the client loved [paste] — match its energy, write new ideas."

Three things in that template do the heavy lifting. The naira-specific benefit forces the copy to sell something real instead of vibes. The banned list — no hype words we cannot prove — stops the AI from inventing "the best suya in Lagos," which is the line that gets a business in trouble. And the example at the end carries the client's voice better than any adjective could.

## Act three: the revision, where the money is made or lost

Revisions are where freelancers quietly lose their profit. A ₦150,000 content job that needs three rounds of rewrites stops being a ₦150,000 job. The prompt skill in act three is to change exactly one thing while holding everything else still.

The template for a revision starts by restating the original brief in one paragraph, then names the change:

> "You are the copywriter on this job. Original brief: 12 Instagram captions for a Lekki grill house, audience young professionals 24–35, voice warm and confident, each under 90 words with a naira benefit and a DM call to action. The client has asked for this change: the MD feels the captions sound too casual and wants them more premium, but the Friday suya night energy must stay. Here is the draft [paste]. Rewrite only the lines that sound casual. Keep the prices, the structure and the calls to action exactly as they are. Show me the three captions that changed most, and in one line each say what you changed and why."

That last instruction — say what you changed and why — is doing quiet work. It gives the freelancer something to send the client alongside the revision: "We tightened the opening lines and swapped the slang for a more premium tone, prices and offers unchanged." Clients approve faster when they can see the reasoning. A revision without an explanation invites a third round. A revision with one usually ends the conversation.

There is also the hard revision, the one where the feedback is fog. "My MD hates it." No reason, no detail. The move is not to guess. Ask the AI to generate options against the most likely causes:

> "The client rejected this draft with no specific feedback [paste draft and brief]. Give me three revised directions: one more premium, one bolder and more playful, one shorter and punchier. Keep the facts and prices identical in all three. Under 90 words each. I will show the client all three and let them pick a direction."

Three directions cost the freelancer twenty minutes. They save a week of guessing, and they make the freelancer look like the person with a process, not the person with a problem.

## The ₦ context: what this is actually worth

Prompts do not just change how fast you work. They change what the work is worth, and Nigerian freelancers who ignore that leave money on the table.

Start with the market as it actually is. A month of social media content for a small Lagos business — twelve to sixteen posts with captions — typically goes for ₦120,000 to ₦300,000, depending on the business and whether design is included. A single product description or a set of five usually sits between ₦15,000 and ₦50,000. A landing page's copy runs ₦50,000 to ₦150,000. A full brand voice and messaging job for a business that is serious about it can reach ₦250,000 to ₦500,000. These are working ranges for freelancers with a portfolio, not promises. A beginner charging the top of a range without the work to back it up will not hold a client past the first job.

Here is where prompting changes the arithmetic. Before AI, that ₦150,000 content month took a freelancer the better part of two weeks: research, drafting, revisions, back and forth. With a working prompt system — brief prompt, deliverable template, revision template — the same month takes four or five focused days. The freelancer has two choices. Take on a second client in the freed week, which is the obvious one. Or keep the same client load and charge for the outcome rather than the hours.

The second choice is the one most freelancers miss. A client does not buy your hours. They buy twelve captions that sound like their business and get approved first time. If your system delivers that in four days, the price should reflect the result, not the calendar. The honest way to say it to a client is simple: "You are paying for the strategy, the voice, and the delivery — the fact that I work fast is my problem, not your discount."

## The habits that keep client work clean

A few working rules, learned the hard way by people doing this every day.

Keep a separate prompt sheet per client, not per job. The grill house sheet holds the brand voice, the prices, the banned words, the example captions, and the three templates above, filled in. When the next month's job starts, you open the sheet, not a blank box. A freelancer with ten clients and ten sheets delivers consistent work. A freelancer with ten clients and a good memory delivers surprises.

And own every word you send. AI drafts, you direct, you deliver. When a caption has the wrong price or a description promises what the business cannot do, the client does not blame the tool. They blame the freelancer. Read the output like it has your name on it, because it does.

The second one spent it onboarding her next client.
