## The Universal Framework: Role + Context + Constraints + Examples

Forget memorising hundreds of random "prompt tricks." One framework covers nearly everything:

**1. Role — who is the AI now?**
> "You are a senior social media copywriter for Nigerian beauty brands..." / "You are a meticulous code reviewer..."

**2. Context — the situation and background material.**
> "My client sells vitamin C serum at ₦18,500. Her audience is women 22–30 in Lagos. Brand voice is warm and confident..."

**3. Constraints — scope, tone, length, format, exclusions.**
> "5 captions, each under 80 words, with a hook, a naira benefit, social proof, and a CTA. No emojis in the first line. No overpromising unrealistic skin results."

**4. Examples — one or two samples of what "great" looks like to you.**
> "Here is a caption style we love from our brand's last campaign: [paste]. Match that energy but write new ideas."

This R + C + C + E structure instantly upgrades 90% of the prompts you will ever need. When output is weak, look at which of the four was missing and fix that one.

## Industry-Specific Prompt Templates (Copy-Paste and Adapt)

### For copywriting clients

> "You are a direct-response copywriter who writes for Nigerian small businesses. Write [sales caption / ad headline / product description] for [product] sold at [price] to [audience]. Target: [goal, e.g., 20 orders this week]. Structure: hook, problem the customer feels, naira-specific benefit, proof, objection-handling line ([e.g., 'delivery nationwide']), and a clear CTA. Tone: [cheerful/classy/urgent]. Avoid: hype words you cannot prove, English that sounds translated. Include one odd-but-true detail about the product to make it feel authentic."

### For design briefs

> "You are a creative director writing a brief for a logo/brand kit for [business], a [restaurant in Yaba / tech startup in Abuja]. Deliver this brief with: brand personality in 3 words, target customer, competition notes, desired colours (no clichés like red for Africa), typography direction, usage rules, and 3 'avoid' notes. Write in plain language a designer can action without asking 10 questions."

### For code and web work

> "You are reviewing my [file/code snippet] for a [booking site]. Requirements: [list]. Check for: security issues, performance on slow Nigerian connections, mobile layout, and obvious bugs. Give me a numbered list of issues with priority tags (critical/medium/nice-to-have) and suggest fixes I can approve. Do not rewrite the whole file — just tell me what to change and why."

### For research and strategy

> "You are a market analyst focused on Nigeria. Research [market — e.g., 'protein snacks for gym-goers in Lagos']. Give me: top 5 competitors with their pricing and positioning, the problems buyers complain about most on reviews, pricing ranges that work, and 3 underserved niches. Cite where possible and flag anything that is speculation rather than fact, so I can double-check."

Every template follows the same skeleton — role, context, constraints, examples — and each ends by telling the AI **how to behave with uncertainty** ("flag speculation", "say if unsure"). That last habit is worth its weight in client trust.

## The Iteration Loop: How Pros Refine a Weak First Draft

Even the best prompt-engineered first try often needs a second pass. The professional loop:

1. **Ask for self-critique:** "Rate your output against the brief: what is the weakest line and what breaks the brand voice? Rewrite the weak parts yourself."
2. **Point at specifics, not vague feelings:** say *"the product benefit is buried under adjectives"* and *"the CTA is too soft"* rather than *"make it better."*
3. **Merge, don't delete:** "Keep line 2 and 5, rewrite the rest with my audience in mind."
4. **Chain prompts:** use the output of one prompt as the input of the next — first a strategy, then messages, then the design brief, then the review.
5. **Reverse-engineer great work:** next time the client sends a reference you admire, ask the AI to *describe the structure and techniques of that piece* — then generate new work that applies those, not recycled text.

Iteration is not a sign of a weak prompt; it is evidence of a professional's judgment. The tool drafts. You direct. The client pays for the direction.

## Building Your Personal Prompt Library (Your Unfair Advantage)

Every strong freelancer eventually realises that their prompt library is a machine that mints their own quality. Here is the system:

- **Save only what produced exceptional output.** A folder or a Notion page divided by service (copy, design, code, research, admin).
- **Annotate every saved prompt** with: the client type it works for, what to personalise, and one "gotcha" (e.g., "falter if the product actually has no social proof").
- **Version your best prompts** — keep 1.0 and the improved 2.0 with a note on what changed.
- **Turn client briefs into prompts:** the next time a brief arrives, spend 10 minutes turning its requirements into a reusable prompt, then bill those 10 minutes as strategy (which is exactly what they are).

Your library is basically a second pitch deck: pull it out and you can show a prospective client how you think — and that alone converts clients who have been burned by generic AI copy.

## Common Mistakes That Keep Freelancers at Average Prices

- **One-line prompts.** You get shower curtain-level output and blame the tool.
- **No audience specificity.** Nigeria, naira, and real cultural context are in your prompting vocabulary — use them.
- **Zero constraints.** No length, tone, or format limits returns 2,000 words you will never read.
- **Blindly accepting output.** Every deliverable passes through your judgment before a client sees it — always.
- **Never asking for uncertainty flags.** "Say if you are unsure" prevents confident-sounding nonsense that embarrasses you later.
- **Hoarding prompts in your head.** Write them down; your memory is not your prompt library.
