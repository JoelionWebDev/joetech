Every week, busy professionals in Lagos, Abuja and Port Harcourt hand over bags of clothes because they have no time to wash, dry and iron them. Students, hotels, offices, hospitals and families all need clean clothes delivered on time. That steady demand is why a laundry business remains one of the most practical small businesses to start in Nigeria. It does not need a university degree, it can start from a small shop or even from home, and it can grow one loyal customer at a time.

This guide explains how to start properly. You will learn how much money you may need, what equipment to buy first, how to choose a location, how to price your services, how to find your first customers, and how to run the daily work without losing clothes or money. The figures here are realistic starting points, not promises. Prices for machines, rent and detergents change, so check current prices in your area before you spend.

## Is a Laundry Business Right for You?

A laundry business suits you if you are organised, reliable and willing to do physical work every day. The work is repetitive: sorting, washing, drying, ironing, folding and packing. Customers judge you on two things above all else: whether their clothes come back clean and on time, and whether anything goes missing. If you can be consistent on those two points, you can build a strong reputation.

It also suits you if you live in a busy area. Estates, student hostels, office districts and residential streets with working families are good locations. People in those areas have money to pay for convenience and little time to do laundry themselves. If your street is mostly empty during the day and most neighbours wash at home, you may need to target offices, hotels or hostels nearby instead of relying only on walk-in customers.

Think about your time. A laundry shop needs someone present during opening hours to receive clothes, record orders and hand them back. If you have a full-time job elsewhere, you will need a trusted staff member or a partner to run the shop. Many owners start part-time from home, then open a shop when orders become steady. That slower start reduces risk and lets you learn the work before you pay rent.

## How Much Does It Cost to Start?

Startup costs depend on whether you start from home or rent a shop, and whether you buy new or fairly used machines. A small home-based start can cost far less than a full shop with new industrial machines. The table below is a guide, not a fixed price list. Check prices from sellers in Lagos, Onitsha, Abuja or your local market before you budget.

For a home-based start, you may need a washing machine, an iron and ironing board, a pressing table, detergents and stain removers, nylon bags, hangers, a weighing scale, and a record book or a simple app. A fairly used washing machine can cost much less than a new one, but ask a technician to test it before you pay. A new semi-automatic machine is a common starting choice because it is cheaper and easier to repair than a fully automatic one.

For a small shop, add rent, a shop sign, shelves, a counter, a generator or inverter, and a water supply. Rent varies widely by area. A small shop in a busy Lagos estate may cost several hundred thousand naira a year, while a shop in a smaller town may cost far less. Do not rent the most expensive shop on the street for your first year. Choose a visible but affordable location and spend the money you save on a good machine and good detergents.

Keep a separate budget for your first three months of running costs: detergent, water, fuel or electricity, transport for pickup and delivery, and small repairs. Many new laundry businesses fail not because they have no customers, but because they run out of cash in the second month. Keep at least one month of running costs in reserve before you open.

## Choose a Good Location

Location matters more than a fancy shop. Look for a place where your target customers already pass every day: near an estate gate, a busy junction, a market, a school or an office complex. A shop that is easy to find and easy to park near will get more walk-in customers than a cheaper shop hidden inside a street.

Check water and power before you sign a rent agreement. Laundry work needs a steady water supply and reliable electricity. Ask neighbours how often power fails, whether the area has borehole water, and what other laundry shops nearby charge. Visit the area at different times of day to see foot traffic. A street that is busy in the morning but empty in the evening may still work if most of your customers drop clothes before work and collect them after work.

Check the competition. If three laundry shops already operate on the same street, you need a clear reason for customers to choose you: faster turnaround, pickup and delivery, better ironing, or a service the others do not offer, such as washing duvets or cleaning suits. If there is no competition, ask why. It may be a genuine opportunity, or it may mean there is little demand in that area.

If you start from home, your location is your house. That can work if you live in a busy estate and your neighbours know you. Put a clear sign outside, tell your estate group, and offer pickup and delivery so customers do not have to find your door. Home-based laundry keeps costs low, but you must keep your work area clean and separate from your living space so customers trust your hygiene.

## Register Your Business and Set Up Properly

Register a business name with the Corporate Affairs Commission when you are ready to operate as a formal business. A registered name makes it easier to open a business bank account, work with hotels and offices, and build trust. You can start small and register as you grow, but do not delay it for too long if you want corporate clients. A business account also keeps your laundry money separate from your personal spending, which makes it easier to know whether you are making a profit.

Get the basic tools for record keeping from day one. Use a receipt book or a simple digital system to record every order: customer name, phone number, items received, condition of each item, price, date received and date due. Give the customer a copy or a photo of the receipt. That record protects you if a customer claims an item is missing, and it protects the customer if you make a mistake.

Set your opening hours and display them clearly. Most laundry shops in Nigeria open from around 8 am to 7 pm, six days a week, with Sunday as a rest day or a shorter day. Choose hours you can keep consistently. Customers plan around your hours, so changing them without notice frustrates them. If you offer pickup and delivery, state the times and areas clearly.

Buy insurance or set aside a small fund for damaged or lost items. Accidents happen: a colour can run, a button can break, or an item can be misplaced. Having a clear policy and a small reserve lets you replace or compensate fairly without a long argument. Tell customers your policy when they first use your service, not after a problem happens.

## Buy the Right Equipment First

Start with the essentials and add more as orders grow. Your first list should include a washing machine, a dryer or a good drying space, an iron and ironing board, a pressing table, a weighing scale, buckets, detergents, stain removers, bleach for whites where appropriate, fabric softener, nylon bags, hangers and a storage rack. A generator or inverter is important in many Nigerian areas because power cuts can delay drying and ironing.

Choose a washing machine that matches your expected volume. A small domestic machine is enough for a home-based start with a few customers a day. A larger semi-industrial machine is better if you plan to serve a hotel, a hostel or an office. Ask the seller about spare parts and repairers in your area. A cheap machine that no one can repair will cost you more in lost orders than a slightly more expensive machine with local support.

Learn to use detergents correctly. Different fabrics need different care. Cotton, linen, wool, silk and synthetic fabrics do not all wash the same way. Read the labels on clothes, sort by colour and fabric, and test a new detergent on a small area first. Good washing protects clothes and reduces complaints. Cheap detergent that damages clothes will cost you customers.

Keep your machines clean. Clean the washing machine filter, wipe the iron, and wash your buckets regularly. A dirty machine leaves smells and stains on clothes. Set a weekly cleaning routine and stick to it. Customers may never see your machine, but they will notice if their clothes smell fresh or stale.

## Set Prices That Cover Costs and Make a Profit

Price by item, by weight, or by bundle, and be consistent. Many Nigerian laundry shops charge per item: a shirt, a trouser, a dress, a bedsheet or a duvet each has a price. Others charge per kilogram for everyday clothes. Choose the method your customers understand easily and that you can apply fairly every time. Display your price list on the wall and share it on WhatsApp so customers can check it before they bring clothes.

Work out your cost per item before you set a price. Add detergent, water, electricity or fuel, ironing time, packaging and transport. Then add your profit. For example, if washing and ironing a shirt costs you ₦300 in materials and power, charging ₦500 leaves a margin, while charging ₦350 leaves almost nothing after a mistake or a rewash. A price that is too low may bring many customers but little profit and a lot of stress.

Offer bundles for regular customers. A weekly bundle for a family, a monthly plan for a bachelor, or a per-kilogram rate for bulk washing can encourage larger orders. A hotel or office may prefer a monthly contract with a fixed rate. Make sure any bundle still leaves a profit after you count the real costs. A bundle that loses money on every order is not a promotion; it is a problem.

Review your prices every few months. Fuel, detergent and rent prices change, and your prices should reflect that. When you increase a price, tell regular customers in advance and explain briefly: "From next month, shirts will be ₦600 because detergent and power costs have risen." Most customers accept a small, honest increase if your service stays good. A sudden increase without warning feels unfair.

Do not compete only on being the cheapest. The cheapest laundry shop often cuts corners on detergent, ironing or packaging, and customers notice. Compete on reliability, cleanliness, careful handling and on-time delivery. Those qualities allow you to charge a fair price and keep customers who value their clothes.

## Find Your First Customers

Your first customers will come from your neighbourhood. Tell your neighbours, your estate group, your church or mosque community, and your friends that you have opened. Offer a simple opening promotion, such as free pickup and delivery for the first week, or a discount on the first wash. The goal is to get people to try you once. Good service will bring them back.

Visit nearby offices, hotels, hostels and schools. Introduce yourself, leave a price list and a phone number, and offer a trial wash. A small hotel may need bed linen washed daily. An office may need staff uniforms washed weekly. A hostel may need student laundry at the end of the week. Corporate and institutional customers order regularly and pay in bulk, which gives you steady income.

Use WhatsApp Status and a simple flyer to advertise locally. Post before-and-after photos of clean, well-ironed clothes, your price list, and your pickup times. Share your flyer in your estate WhatsApp group where allowed. A short video of you ironing a shirt neatly or folding clothes properly can convince a sceptical neighbour more than a long advert.

Ask happy customers to recommend you. After a good delivery, say: "Thank you. If you know anyone nearby who needs laundry, please share my number." Word of mouth is the strongest marketing for a laundry business because people trust a neighbour's recommendation about who handles their clothes carefully. A referral from one family can bring a whole street.

## Run Daily Operations Without Losing Clothes

Create a simple workflow and follow it every time. When clothes arrive, count them with the customer present, check for stains, tears or missing buttons, and note the condition on the receipt. Tag each bundle with the customer's name and number. Sort by colour and fabric before washing. Wash, dry, iron, fold, pack and label. Hand back only against the receipt or a confirmed phone number.

Never mix one customer's clothes with another's. Use separate bags, baskets or tags for each order. Mixing clothes is the fastest way to lose an item and lose a customer. If you have staff, train them on this rule on day one and check it daily. A small mistake in sorting can become a big complaint.

Set a clear turnaround time and keep it. Same-day service for a few items, next-day for a normal load, and two days for duvets or bulk orders is a common pattern. Tell the customer the collection date when they drop the clothes, and send a reminder message when the order is ready. If you will be late, call or message the customer before the agreed time. A warning is far better than a missed deadline.

Handle stains and delicate items with care. Ask the customer about a stain when they bring the item, and explain if a stain may not come out completely. For suits, silk, wool and beaded clothes, use the correct method or send them to a specialist if you cannot handle them safely. It is better to decline a risky item honestly than to damage it and pay for a replacement.

Keep your shop clean and organised. A clean counter, neatly folded clothes, labelled shelves and a fresh smell tell customers that you care about hygiene. A messy shop with clothes on the floor tells them the opposite, even if your washing is good. Cleanliness is part of your product.


## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I start a laundry business from home?**
Yes. Many owners start from home, serve neighbours and estate customers, and offer pickup and delivery. Keep your work area clean, use a clear sign, and keep customer clothes separate from your household laundry.

**How do I price laundry services?**
Price by item, by weight or by bundle. Work out your real costs for detergent, water, power, packaging and transport, then add a profit margin. Display your price list clearly and review it when costs change.

**How do I get hotel or office customers?**
Visit them with a price list, offer a trial wash, and agree clear terms for turnaround, pickup and payment. Hotels and offices value reliability and a formal receipt more than the lowest price.

**What if I damage or lose a customer's cloth?**
Apologise, investigate quickly, and offer a fair solution such as repair, replacement or compensation according to your policy. Tell customers your policy when they first use your service, and keep a small reserve for such cases.

A laundry business rewards consistency more than size. Start with good equipment, clear prices, careful handling and honest delivery times. Serve your street well, then your estate, then nearby offices and hotels. Keep your records clean, save for repairs, and listen to your customers. Over time, those daily habits turn a small laundry shop into a trusted local business that pays its bills and grows steadily.
