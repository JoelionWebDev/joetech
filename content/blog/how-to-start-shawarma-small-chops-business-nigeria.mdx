## Why Shawarma and Small Chops Sell So Well

Shawarma and small chops are two of the most reliable food products in Nigeria. Shawarma is a filling, affordable meal that office workers, students, and families buy for lunch, dinner, and late-night snacks. Small chops — spring rolls, samosas, puff puff, chicken wings, and meatballs — are the food of parties, meetings, and celebrations. Together, they give you two markets: daily walk-in customers and weekend event orders.

The business works because the demand is visible. Walk past a busy shawarma spot in Lekki, Ikeja, Surulere, or Wuse at 7 p.m. and you will see a queue. Small chops vendors who supply parties in Lagos often take orders days in advance. People eat every day, and when they celebrate, they order food in bulk. A vendor who is consistent with taste and hygiene can build a loyal customer base within a few months.

You can start small. Many vendors begin with a roadside kiosk, a small shop, or even from home with delivery only. The key is to get the taste right, keep your prices fair, and be where hungry customers already are. This guide covers the costs, the equipment, the daily routine, and the pricing that makes the business profitable.

## How Much Capital Do You Need?

A realistic starting budget is ₦250,000 to ₦500,000, depending on whether you already have a location. Here is a breakdown for a small setup of about ₦350,000:

Equipment: ₦150,000 to ₦200,000. This covers a shawarma grill or toaster, a deep fryer, a work table, knives and chopping boards, and storage containers. A good second-hand grill can cut this cost, but check that it heats evenly before you pay for it.

First stock: ₦80,000 to ₦100,000. Chicken, beef, shawarma bread, cabbage, carrots, mayonnaise, ketchup, spring roll sheets, flour, oil, and packaging. Buy fresh stock in small quantities at first — meat and vegetables spoil quickly, and waste eats your profit.

Packaging and branding: ₦20,000 to ₦30,000. Foil, paper bags, small chops boxes, and stickers with your business name and phone number. Good packaging keeps food hot, looks professional, and reminds customers how to order again.

Rent and setup: ₦50,000 to ₦100,000. If you rent a small kiosk or a corner in a busy area, budget for rent and a small deposit. If you start from home with delivery only, you can skip this cost and spend the money on better equipment and more stock.

Reserve: ₦20,000. Keep this for a slow first week, a broken gas cylinder, or a bulk order that needs extra stock. Never spend your last naira on opening day.

If your budget is smaller, start with small chops alone. Small chops need less equipment — a fryer, a table, and packaging — and you can take party orders from home while you save for a shawarma grill. Add shawarma once your small chops sales are steady.

## Equipment You Actually Need

You do not need a full restaurant kitchen. You need the right few items, kept clean and in good working order.

A shawarma grill or toaster is the heart of the business. It toasts the wrap evenly and gives it the crisp finish customers expect. Test it before you buy it, and learn how long each wrap needs so you do not burn the first orders of the day.

A deep fryer, or a large pot with a thermometer if you are starting very small, is essential for spring rolls, samosas, and chicken wings. The oil must be hot enough — if it is too cool, the chops absorb oil and taste greasy. Change your oil regularly. Customers can taste old oil, and they will not come back.

A work table, chopping boards, and sharp knives keep your preparation fast and safe. Use separate boards for meat and vegetables. This is not just good hygiene — it is what keeps your customers healthy and your reputation clean.

A refrigerator or a cooler box is essential for storing chicken, beef, and mayonnaise. In Lagos heat, meat left out for a few hours is a health risk. If you cannot afford a fridge yet, buy meat daily in the exact quantity you need, and store mayonnaise and sauces in a cool box with ice.

A gas cylinder and a backup plan matter more than most beginners think. Running out of gas in the middle of a dinner rush means lost sales and angry customers. Keep a small backup cylinder, or know the nearest gas refill point and its closing time.

## Finding a Good Location

Location decides how many customers you see each day. The best spots for shawarma are busy streets near offices, schools, markets, and residential estates. People buy shawarma on their way home from work, after lectures, or when they do not feel like cooking. A spot near a bus stop, a bank, or a popular junction gets natural foot traffic without you paying for adverts.

For small chops, location matters less than reputation, because most orders come through WhatsApp and phone calls for parties. But a visible spot still helps — people who taste your small chops at a party will look for your shop the next day.

Before you commit to a location, visit it at different times. Count how many people pass by between 5 p.m. and 9 p.m., the busiest hours for shawarma. Check whether there is a competitor nearby and what they charge. Two shawarma spots on the same street can both do well if the street is busy enough, but three may split the customers too thinly.

Check the practical things: Is there light? Is there water nearby? Can a dispatch rider park and collect orders easily? Can customers wait comfortably? A spot with no light and no water will cost you more in generator fuel and stress than a slightly more expensive spot with both.

## Sourcing Ingredients and Keeping Quality Consistent

Your taste is your business. Customers return for the same flavour they enjoyed last time, so buy your ingredients from the same suppliers once you find good ones.

Buy chicken and beef from a trusted butcher or cold room, and inspect it every time. Fresh meat has no strong smell and a firm texture. Buy shawarma bread from a bakery that delivers daily — stale bread ruins a wrap no matter how good the filling is. Buy cabbage, carrots, and onions fresh from the market each morning, and wash them properly before use.

Make your sauces in-house if you can. A good garlic sauce or mayonnaise mix, made to your own recipe, is what makes your shawarma different from the next vendor's. Write your recipe down, with exact quantities, and follow it every time. If you guess the quantities each day, the taste will change each day, and customers will notice.

Control your portions. Weigh or count the chicken that goes into each wrap, so every customer gets the same amount. Portion control is also how you protect your profit — if one worker puts twice the chicken in each wrap, your margin disappears even when sales are good.

## Step-by-Step: Your First Month

### Week 1: Prepare and test

Set up your equipment, buy your first stock, and cook for friends and family. Ask them to be honest about the taste, the size, and the price they would pay. Adjust your recipe and your portions before you sell to strangers. Take clear photos of your food in daylight for WhatsApp and Instagram.

### Week 2: Open quietly and learn

Open your spot or start taking delivery orders. Sell a limited menu — for example, chicken shawarma, beef shawarma, and one small chops pack. A small menu is faster to prepare, easier to keep consistent, and cheaper to stock. Note what sells, what customers ask for, and what time the rush starts. Fix problems quickly: if orders are slow, simplify your process; if food runs out early, buy more stock the next day.

### Week 3: Add party orders

Tell your customers that you supply small chops for parties, birthdays, and office meetings. Offer three pack sizes — for example, a pack for 20 people, a pack for 50 people, and a pack for 100 people. Ask for a 50 percent deposit before you buy stock for a party order, and confirm the delivery time and address in writing on WhatsApp.

### Week 4: Review and adjust

Add up your sales, your stock costs, and your other expenses. Which product made the most profit? Which hours were busiest? What did customers complain about? Use the answers to decide your prices, your opening hours, and your next stock order. A vendor who reviews the numbers every week grows faster than one who only counts the cash in the till.

## Pricing and Worked Profit Example

Price your food to cover the cost of ingredients, gas, packaging, and your time, with profit left over. Here is a worked example for a chicken shawarma:

Ingredients per wrap: chicken ₦800, bread ₦250, vegetables and sauce ₦300, foil and packaging ₦150. Total ingredient cost is ₦1,500. Add gas and other running costs of about ₦200 per wrap. Your total cost is ₦1,700. If you sell the wrap for ₦3,000, your profit is ₦1,300 per wrap. Sell 40 wraps a day and your daily profit is ₦52,000 before rent and other fixed costs.

For small chops, price by the pack. A pack for 20 people might contain 20 spring rolls, 20 samosas, 20 puff puff, and 10 chicken wings. If the ingredients and packaging cost ₦18,000 and you sell the pack for ₦30,000, your profit is ₦12,000 per pack. Two party packs in a weekend can be more profitable than a whole day of walk-in sales.

Check your competitors' prices, but do not copy them blindly. If your cost is higher because you use more chicken, charge a little more and explain the difference — customers will pay for a wrap that is full and tasty. If your cost is lower, you can charge the same price and keep more profit, or charge slightly less and win more customers. Either way, know your own cost first.

Offer a small discount for bulk orders, but keep it modest. Ten percent off a large party pack is generous. Thirty percent off will eat your margin and teach customers to expect cheap prices.

## Marketing Without a Big Budget

Your best marketing is a customer who enjoyed your food and tells a friend. Make that easy: put your phone number and WhatsApp link on every pack, and ask happy customers to share a photo and tag you.

Post on WhatsApp Status every day. A short video of shawarma on the grill, steam rising from fresh small chops, or a party pack being boxed will make people hungry. Post in the late afternoon, when people are thinking about dinner, and again in the evening for late-night buyers.

Serve offices and estates. Visit nearby offices with a small free tasting pack and a price list. Offer a weekly deal — for example, a discount on Friday orders for the whole office. One office that orders 20 wraps every Friday is worth more than twenty one-time walk-in customers.

Partner with event planners, DJs, and photographers. They meet people who are planning parties every week. Offer them a small commission or a free tasting pack for every customer they send you who places an order. A simple agreement, kept honestly, can bring steady party work.

Use Instagram and TikTok for short food videos. You do not need professional equipment — a clean table, good daylight, and a phone camera are enough. Show the food being made, not just the finished product. People buy food they have watched being cooked.

## Hygiene, Safety, and Customer Trust

Food businesses live or die on trust. One case of food poisoning can end a business that took months to build. Wash your hands often, keep your work area clean, cover your hair while cooking, and keep raw meat away from ready-to-eat food. Wash vegetables properly. Store mayonnaise and sauces in a cool place, and throw away any sauce that smells off — never serve it and hope for the best.

Use clean water for cooking and washing. If your area has water challenges, buy water from a trusted source and store it in clean, covered containers. Keep your gas, oil, and cleaning products away from food.

Be honest about ingredients. If a customer asks whether your shawarma contains a particular ingredient, answer truthfully. If you run out of chicken, say so — do not substitute a cheaper meat and hope nobody notices. Customers who trust you will forgive a sold-out item. They will not forgive being misled.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

Buying too much perishable stock is the most common mistake. Chicken and vegetables spoil fast in Nigerian heat. Buy what you can sell in one or two days, and buy again. A fridge helps, but it does not make old stock fresh.

Inconsistent taste is the second. If your shawarma tastes different every day, customers stop trusting you. Write your recipes down and follow them. Train anyone who helps you to follow the same recipe and the same portions.

Pricing without knowing your costs is the third. A wrap that sells for ₦2,500 but costs ₦2,400 to make leaves you almost nothing after gas and rent. Calculate your cost per item, then set your price.

Ignoring party orders is the fourth. Walk-in sales pay your daily bills, but party packs pay your rent. Make it easy for people to order for events, with clear pack sizes, clear prices, and a deposit system.

Poor packaging is the fifth. A wrap that falls apart, or small chops that arrive cold and squashed, will not get a repeat order no matter how good they tasted in your kitchen. Invest in foil, sturdy boxes, and bags that keep food hot.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does it cost to start a shawarma business in Nigeria?

A small setup costs about ₦250,000 to ₦500,000, including equipment, first stock, packaging, and a small reserve. Starting with small chops alone from home can cost less, around ₦100,000 to ₦150,000, because you need less equipment and no shop rent.

### Do I need a shop to sell shawarma and small chops?

No, but a visible spot helps for walk-in shawarma sales. Many vendors start from home with delivery only, then rent a kiosk once their daily orders are steady. For small chops, most sales come through WhatsApp and phone orders for parties, so a shop is less important at the start.

### How do I get party orders for small chops?

Tell every customer you supply parties, post your pack sizes and prices on WhatsApp Status, and partner with event planners and photographers. Offer a tasting pack to a few event planners so they can recommend you with confidence. Ask for a 50 percent deposit before you buy stock for a large order.

### How do I keep my shawarma taste consistent?

Write your recipes down with exact quantities, buy from the same suppliers, and control your portions. Train anyone who helps you to follow the same steps. Taste your own food every day before you open — if it is not good enough for you, it is not good enough for your customers.

## Final Word

A shawarma and small chops business rewards vendors who get the basics right: fresh ingredients, consistent taste, clean preparation, fair prices, and quick, friendly service. Start with a small menu, learn your costs, and build your reputation one customer at a time. Serve offices, estates, and parties well, and your customers will do your marketing for you. The food business is hard work — long hours, hot kitchens, and busy weekends — but for a vendor who cooks well and treats customers honestly, it can provide steady income for years.
