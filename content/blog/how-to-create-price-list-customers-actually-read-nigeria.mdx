Amaka runs a hair studio in Lekki Phase 1. For a year, whenever a customer asked for her prices, she sent the same long WhatsApp message: twenty services, typed in a hurry, prices scattered through the text, some in naira, some as "15k", one line that just said "braids — DM for price." Customers read the first three lines, asked her to repeat the rest, and often booked the cheapest thing on the list because it was the only price they could find quickly.

Then she changed nothing about her prices and everything about the page. Same services, same figures, laid out so a customer's eyes landed where Amaka wanted them. Bookings for her mid-range styles rose within a month. Nobody had haggled her down. Nobody had asked her to repeat a price. The list had started doing its job.

A price list is not read like a letter. It is scanned, in a few seconds, on a phone, usually while the customer is doing something else. This article is about that scan: where the eye goes first, how grouping steers it, why the most expensive item belongs at the top, what to leave off the page entirely, and how the same list changes shape between print and a WhatsApp image.

## What the Eye Does First

A customer opening a price list does three things in about five seconds. She reads the business name, she finds the section she came for, and she checks one price. If any of those three steps stalls, she messages you to ask — or she messages your competitor instead.

That is why the top of the page matters more than the bottom. Business name, one line saying what you do, and the date. "Amaka's Hair Studio — Lekki Phase 1. Price List, November 2026." The date is doing quiet work: it tells the customer this list is current, which matters in a country where prices move every few months. A list with no date looks like it was made in January and forgotten, even when the prices were updated yesterday.

Under the name, the eye looks for a heading it recognises. Headings are the signposts, so make them impossible to miss: bigger than the items beneath them, bold, with a blank line above and below. "Braids", "Wigs", "Nails", "Makeup" — four words, each standing alone, instead of twenty services running together in one block. The space between groups is not wasted space. It is what lets a customer jump to "Nails" without reading about wigs first. A list with no breathing room reads as one long wall, and walls get skipped.

Inside a group, each line should hold three things and nothing more: the item, one short detail, and the price. "Knotless braids, shoulder length — ₦25,000." The detail answers the question the customer would ask next — how long, how big, what is included — in five words or fewer. The long version belongs on your Instagram page or your website, not on the list. "Knotless braids, made with premium attachment, done by our senior stylist, available in all colours, takes about four hours, prices start from..." is a paragraph pretending to be a price, and the customer will not finish it.

Prices sit in one straight column down the right-hand side, every one of them in the same format: ₦25,000, ₦18,000, ₦40,000. Never mix "25k" with "₦25,000" and "N25000" on the same page. One format, one column, one symbol, all the way down.

## The Salon, Before and After

Here is Amaka's old list, more or less as she used to send it. Read it the way a customer would — quickly, on a phone, while half-listening to something else.

> AMAKA HAIR STUDIO price list!!! braids 15k, knotless 25k, ghana weaving N18000, wig revamp 10000, frontal install 35000, closure install 25,000, manicure 8000 pedicure 10000, gel nails 15000, acrylic 20000, makeup 30000, gele tying 15000, bridal package DM for price, home service available, we use good products, open mon-sat 9am-7pm, call 0803...

Everything is there and nothing can be found. The cheapest service comes first, so the whole studio looks cheap. Three different price formats sit side by side. The bridal package — her most profitable service — hides behind "DM for price", which is the fastest way to make a bride look elsewhere. The list ends in a ramble about opening hours, which belongs at the bottom in a short note, not tangled into the prices.

Here is the same studio, the same services, the same prices, redesigned.

> **Price List, November 2026**

> Bridal package, hair + makeup + gele — ₦150,000
> Owambe glam, makeup + gele tying — ₦55,000

The services did not change. The reading did. A customer can find "Nails" in a second, compare three options, and see the price without hunting. The note at the bottom answers the next question — home service, booking, payment — without cluttering the prices themselves.

## Anchoring: Why the Expensive Item Goes First

The redesigned list opens with the bridal package at ₦150,000, and that is deliberate. The first price a customer sees sets the scale for every price after it. When the first number is ₦150,000, the ₦55,000 owambe glam below it reads as reasonable, and the ₦25,000 knotless braids further down read as a bargain. If the list opened with the ₦15,000 classic braids, as the old one did, the same ₦25,000 knotless braids would read as expensive, and the ₦150,000 bridal package would read as absurd.

This is anchoring, and it works because customers judge prices against each other, not against an absolute scale. Put the premium item first in every group, not just at the top of the page. In "Braids", knotless at ₦25,000 leads, and classic at ₦15,000 follows. In "Nails", acrylic at ₦20,000 leads, and manicure at ₦8,000 closes the group. The item at the top of each group is rarely the one Amaka most wants to sell. The one in the middle usually is — the knotless braids, the gel nails — and sitting between a premium price above and a basic price below, it looks like the sensible choice. That middle item is where her profit lives, and the anchor is what makes it look sensible.

## The Food Vendor: Grouping by How Customers Order

Mama Ejiro sells from a kitchen in Surulere, mostly through WhatsApp, and her customers order differently from Amaka's. They do not browse. They arrive hungry, with a budget and a headcount, and they want to know what feeds four people for ₦20,000. Her list is grouped by the decision, not by the dish.

Her groups are "Plates", "Trays", "Small Chops", and "Drinks". Plates first, because that is what most customers want: jollof rice and chicken ₦4,500, fried rice and turkey ₦5,000, ofada rice and stew ₦5,500. Trays next, anchored by the big one: party jollof tray, serves 20 — ₦65,000, then the tray that serves 10 at ₦35,000, then the small tray at ₦18,000. Small chops follow — 20 pieces for ₦8,000 — because that is the add-on, and add-ons belong after the main decision. Drinks close the list.

The detail on each line does the same job as in the salon: it answers the next question. "Serves 20" is the difference between a customer ordering the right tray and a customer ordering two small ones and blaming you when the food runs out. Portion size, piece count, number of servings — for food, that detail is the product.

## What to Leave Out

A price list earns trust by what it refuses to carry. Your cost price never appears on it. Your supplier's name never appears on it. "Prices are negotiable" never appears on it, because it tells every customer to haggle and tells the ones who hate haggling to leave. Neither does your life story. Amaka's journey into hairdressing belongs in her bio, not between the braids and the wigs. Neither do items you are unsure about, prices you have not confirmed, or products you no longer stock. One dead item on a list makes a customer doubt every other line on it.

Delivery and extra charges are the exception to leaving things out — they must appear, but at the bottom, in the note, not attached to every price. "Delivery in Lekki ₦1,500, Ajah ₦2,500, Ikeja ₦4,000. Order before 10 a.m. for same-day lunch delivery." A customer who discovers a ₦4,000 delivery fee at checkout feels cheated, even when the fee is fair.

## Print and WhatsApp Are Two Different Lists

Amaka keeps three versions of her list, and they do not look alike. The print version is an A4 sheet, laminated, pinned on the wall of the studio and handed to walk-in customers. The font is large enough to read from a metre away, the groups are the same, and there is a QR code at the bottom that opens her WhatsApp. Print forgives length: two pages are fine, because the customer is standing still and the paper is not going anywhere.

The WhatsApp image is a different animal. It is a square image, 1080 by 1080, made in Canva, with her studio name at the top and twelve services at most, in a font big enough to read without pinching the screen. Twelve is the limit because a phone screen is small and a thumb is impatient. The full list lives in a PDF she sends when a customer asks for everything. The image is the shop window; the PDF is the catalogue.

The third version is the plain text she pastes into a chat, saved in her notes app: the ten most popular services, one per line, prices in the same format, no pictures, no decoration. It answers the question in the chat where the question was asked, without making the customer open a file.

All three versions carry the same prices and the same date. The day they disagree, a customer will find the cheaper one.

## Keeping It Honest

A price list goes stale faster than most sellers expect. Amaka checks hers on the first Sunday of every month, and whenever a supplier raises a price. She changes the date at the top, removes anything she no longer offers, and adds new services the week they launch. A list dated November that is still circulating in February tells customers the prices are old, even if they were updated last week and the date was forgotten.

Read your own list the way a stranger would: on a phone, in a hurry, looking for one price. If you cannot find it in five seconds, your customer cannot either. Move the heading, shorten the line, put the premium item first, and take something off the page. The best price list is not the one with the most information. It is the one a customer reads, finds what she came for, and orders from.
