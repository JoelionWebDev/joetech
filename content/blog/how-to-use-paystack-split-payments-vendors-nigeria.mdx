If you run a business that sells other people's products or services, you know the pain of paying vendors manually. A customer pays ₦50,000 for an order on your platform. You need to send ₦35,000 to the vendor, keep ₦10,000 as your commission, send ₦5,000 to a delivery partner, and remember to do it all before the vendor starts asking where their money is. Multiply that by twenty orders a day, and you are spending hours on bank transfers, reconciling spreadsheets, answering "Have you sent my money?" messages, and occasionally sending the wrong amount to the wrong account. One mistake can damage a vendor relationship that took months to build.

Paystack split payments solve that problem by dividing a single customer payment automatically, at the moment the customer pays, and sending each share directly to the right account. The vendor gets their share without you lifting a finger. You keep your commission without chasing it. Your delivery partner gets paid on time. Everyone can see the arrangement clearly, and the manual work disappears. For marketplaces, event platforms, food delivery services, online stores that sell for multiple vendors, schools that share fees with partners, and any Nigerian business that splits revenue, split payments turn a daily headache into a system that runs itself.

This guide explains how Paystack split payments work in Nigeria, when to use them, how to set them up step by step, how to choose the right split type, how to handle fees and taxes, and how to avoid the common mistakes that cause failed payouts and unhappy vendors. You do not need to be a developer to understand the principles, and if you work with a developer, this guide will help you explain exactly what you need.

## What Paystack Split Payments Are, in Plain Language

A split payment is a single payment from a customer that is automatically divided into two or more parts and sent to different bank accounts. Instead of the full amount landing in your account and you manually transferring portions to vendors, Paystack does the division for you, instantly, according to rules you set in advance.

Here is a simple example. You run an online marketplace in Lagos where fashion vendors sell their clothes. A customer buys a dress for ₦30,000. You have agreed with the vendor that they receive 80% of the sale and you keep 20% as commission. With a split payment, the customer pays ₦30,000 once, through your Paystack checkout. Paystack automatically sends ₦24,000 to the vendor's bank account and keeps ₦6,000 in your account. You do not need to log into your bank app, you do not need to remember the vendor's account number, and the vendor does not need to wait for you to find time to pay them. The money moves the moment the payment succeeds.

That automatic division is the core benefit. It saves time, it reduces errors, it builds trust with vendors, and it gives you a clear record of every split. You can see, for each transaction, how much the customer paid, how much each vendor received, and how much you kept. That record makes accounting, reconciliation and tax preparation far easier than a pile of manual transfer receipts.

Split payments are different from simply collecting money and paying vendors later. When you collect and pay later, the money sits in your account, you are responsible for holding it, and vendors have to trust that you will pay them on time. When you use split payments, the vendor's share goes directly to them, which is more transparent and more trustworthy. Vendors who are paid automatically and on time are more likely to stay on your platform, list more products, and recommend you to other vendors.

Paystack offers split payments through two main concepts: subaccounts and split configurations. A subaccount represents a vendor or partner: it holds their bank account details and a share of the payment. A split configuration, often called a split, is the rule that says how a payment should be divided among the subaccounts and your main account. You create a subaccount for each vendor, you create a split that includes those subaccounts, and you attach that split to a payment. When the customer pays, Paystack follows the split rule and divides the money accordingly.

## When Split Payments Make Sense for Your Business

Split payments are not for every business. If you sell only your own products and keep all the revenue, you do not need them. They make sense when your business regularly shares revenue with other people or businesses, and when manual payments are taking too much time or causing too many problems.

Marketplaces are the clearest use case. If you run an online store where multiple vendors list products — fashion, food, groceries, electronics, handmade goods — each sale needs to be shared between you and the vendor who supplied the product. Split payments automate that sharing for every order, no matter how many vendors you have. A marketplace with fifty vendors and hundreds of orders a month cannot survive on manual transfers. Split payments make it scalable.

Event and ticketing businesses also benefit. If you sell tickets for an event and share revenue with the event organiser, the venue, and a promoter, a split payment can divide the ticket price among all of them automatically. Each party gets their share as tickets sell, rather than waiting for a settlement weeks after the event. That immediate payment builds confidence, especially for organisers who have paid upfront costs for the venue and promotion.

Food delivery and logistics platforms use split payments to pay restaurants, riders and the platform itself from a single customer order. A customer orders food for ₦12,000. The restaurant receives its share, the rider receives a delivery fee, and the platform keeps its commission, all from the same payment. That automation is essential when you are handling dozens of orders a day across Lagos, Abuja and Port Harcourt.

Service platforms, such as a platform that connects customers with photographers, makeup artists, cleaners or tutors, can use split payments to share the service fee between the professional and the platform. The professional gets paid promptly, the platform keeps its commission, and the customer only makes one payment. That simplicity encourages customers to book again.

Schools, training platforms and churches that share fees with partners can also use split payments. For example, a training platform that sells a course and shares revenue with the instructor, or a school that collects fees and shares a portion with a partner organisation, can automate the division and avoid end-of-month reconciliation arguments.

If you are unsure whether split payments fit your business, ask yourself three questions. Do you regularly pay more than five vendors or partners? Do manual payments take more than a few hours a week? Have vendors ever complained about late or incorrect payments? If the answer to any of these is yes, split payments are worth considering. If you only pay one vendor occasionally, a manual transfer may still be simpler. The goal is to automate work that is repetitive, time-consuming and error-prone, not to add complexity where it is not needed.

## How Split Payments Work on Paystack

To use split payments well, you need to understand the basic building blocks, even if a developer handles the technical setup for you. Knowing the concepts will help you make good decisions and communicate clearly with your team.

A subaccount is a record for a vendor or partner. It contains the person's or business's name, their bank account number, the bank name, and the percentage or flat amount they should receive from a payment. You create one subaccount per vendor. For example, if you have twenty fashion vendors on your marketplace, you will have twenty subaccounts, one for each vendor. Each subaccount is linked to that vendor's own bank account, so their share goes directly to them.

A split is a rule that groups subaccounts together and defines how a payment is divided. A split can include one or many subaccounts, and it can also include your main account. For example, you might create a split called "Fashion Marketplace Split" that includes Vendor A at 80% and your main account at 20%. Or you might create a split for a specific event that includes the organiser at 70%, the venue at 20%, and your platform at 10%. You can create different splits for different products, events or vendor groups, and you can reuse a split for many payments.

When a customer makes a payment, you tell Paystack which split to apply. Paystack then calculates each share, deducts Paystack's transaction fee according to your settings, and sends each share to the correct account. The split happens automatically, and you can view the breakdown in your Paystack dashboard for each transaction. You will see the total amount paid, the fee, and the amount each subaccount received.

There are two main ways to define a share: by percentage and by flat amount. A percentage share means the vendor receives a percentage of the total payment, such as 80%. That works well when the product price varies, because the vendor's share scales with the price. A flat amount means the vendor receives a fixed naira amount, such as ₦5,000, regardless of the total payment. That works well for a fixed fee, such as a delivery fee or a fixed commission for a service. You can mix percentages and flat amounts in the same split, as long as the total does not exceed the payment amount.

Paystack also lets you decide who bears the transaction fee: you, the vendor, or a shared arrangement. By default, the fee is deducted from the main account, but you can configure it so that the fee is shared proportionally among all parties, or borne by a specific subaccount. That choice matters for your margins, so decide it in advance and agree it with your vendors in writing. A vendor who expects to receive 80% of ₦30,000 and receives less because the fee was deducted from their share will be unhappy if they were not told in advance.

## Setting Up Subaccounts for Your Vendors

Setting up subaccounts is the first practical step, and it requires accurate information from your vendors. A mistake in a bank account number will cause a failed payout, a delayed payment, and a frustrated vendor, so take this step seriously and verify every detail.

Start by collecting the vendor's details in a standard form. You need their full name or business name as it appears on their bank account, their bank account number, and the name of their bank. If the vendor is a registered business, you may also need their business registration details for your own records. Use a Google Form or a simple paper form to collect this information, and ask the vendor to double-check it before they submit it. Tell them clearly that this account is where their payments will go, so they should provide the account they want to be paid into.

Verify the account before you create the subaccount. Paystack provides a way to verify a bank account number, and you should use it, or ask the vendor to send a screenshot of their bank app showing the account name and number. A name mismatch — for example, the account is in a personal name but the vendor gave you a business name — can cause a payout to fail or to be flagged. Resolve any mismatch before you go live. It is far easier to fix a detail now than to chase a failed payout later.

Create the subaccount in your Paystack dashboard or through the Paystack API if you have a developer. In the dashboard, you will enter the vendor's name, bank, account number, and the percentage or flat amount they should receive. You can also add a description, such as the vendor's store name or product category, to help you identify the subaccount later. Give each subaccount a clear, consistent name, such as "Vendor - Adaeze Fashion" or "Vendor - Lagos Food Hub", so you can find it quickly when you have many vendors.

Test the subaccount with a small payment before you use it for real sales. Make a small test transaction, such as ₦1,000, apply the split, and confirm that the vendor receives their share in the correct account. Ask the vendor to confirm receipt on WhatsApp. That test will catch errors in the account details, the split configuration, and the fee settings before real customers are involved. Do not skip the test because you are in a hurry. A failed test costs you a few minutes. A failed live payout costs you a vendor's trust.

Keep a record of every subaccount you create, including the vendor's name, phone number, bank, account number, share percentage, and the date you created it. Store that record securely, and do not share bank details publicly. That record is your reference if a vendor changes their bank account, if a payout fails, or if you need to audit your payments. When a vendor changes their account, update the subaccount immediately and test it again with a small payment.

## Creating a Split and Applying It to Payments

Once your subaccounts exist, the next step is to create a split that defines how payments are divided. The split is the rule, and it should reflect the commercial agreement you have with your vendors.

Decide the split structure before you create it in Paystack. For a simple marketplace, the structure might be: vendor receives 80%, platform keeps 20%. For a food delivery order, it might be: restaurant receives 70%, rider receives 10% as a flat delivery fee, platform keeps 20%. For an event, it might be: organiser receives 70%, venue receives 20%, platform keeps 10%. Write the structure down, agree it with each party in writing — even a WhatsApp message confirming the percentages counts — and only then create the split in Paystack. That written agreement prevents arguments later about what was promised.

In the Paystack dashboard, you create a split by giving it a name, choosing the split type (percentage or flat, or a mix), and adding the subaccounts and their shares. You can add multiple subaccounts to a single split, and you can set the share for each one. Paystack will show you the total and warn you if the shares add up to more than the payment amount. Double-check the numbers before you save. A split that adds up to 110% will fail, and a split that adds up to 90% will leave 10% unallocated, which can cause confusion about where the remaining money should go.

Decide how the split will be applied to payments. If you have a website or an app, your developer will attach the split to the payment request when the customer checks out. For a marketplace, the split applied will depend on which vendor's product the customer is buying, so the system needs to select the correct split for each order. That logic is usually handled by your developer, but you should understand it well enough to test it: place a test order for Vendor A's product and confirm that Vendor A receives the correct share, then place a test order for Vendor B's product and confirm the same.

If you do not have a website and you use Paystack payment links, you can still use split payments by creating a payment link that uses a specific split. That works well for small businesses that sell through WhatsApp or Instagram and use payment links to collect money. Create a separate payment link for each vendor or each product group, attach the correct split to each link, and share the link with the customer. The customer pays through the link, and Paystack splits the payment automatically.

Test every split with a real, small transaction before you use it for customers. Pay with your own card or transfer, check the Paystack dashboard to see the breakdown, and ask each recipient to confirm they received their share. If the breakdown is wrong, fix the split and test again. That testing discipline is what separates a smooth launch from a launch where vendors are paid incorrectly on the first day.

Set up your subaccounts carefully, agree every split in writing, and test each one with a small payment before you go live. Understand your fees, keep clear records, and pay vendors transparently. Do that, and split payments will free you from manual transfers and let you focus on growing a marketplace your vendors trust.
