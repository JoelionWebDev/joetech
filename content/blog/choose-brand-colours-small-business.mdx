A customer scrolls past your flyer on Instagram, glances at your shop sign in Lekki, and later sees your WhatsApp status. They may not remember your business name after one look, but they will remember the colour. That is the quiet power of brand colours: they make your business recognisable before anyone reads a word.

Many small business owners in Nigeria pick colours by feeling alone, or copy a competitor because their branding looks good. The result is often a mix of colours that clash, a logo that is hard to read, and marketing that looks different every single week. Customers notice this, even if they cannot explain it. A business with steady, well-chosen colours looks organised and trustworthy. Choosing brand colours is not about being a designer. It is about making a few careful decisions and then sticking to them.

## Start with what your business should feel like

Colours carry feelings, and your customers feel them before they think about them. A bank that wants to look steady will not use the same colours as a children's party business that wants to look playful. Before you open any colour chart, write down three words that describe how you want customers to feel about your business.

A fashion designer in Lagos might choose elegant, confident, and warm. A pharmacy might choose clean, calm, and trustworthy. A food business might choose fresh, appetising, and friendly. These words become your test for every colour you consider. If a colour does not match at least two of your three words, it does not belong in your palette, no matter how much you personally like it.

Also think about your customers, not only yourself. Your favourite colour may be purple, but if you run a building materials shop whose customers are mostly contractors, a deep blue or a strong earthy tone will feel more serious and reliable to them. Your brand colours are a business decision, and the customer is the one who has to feel comfortable with them.

## Learn the simple rules before you pick

You do not need a design degree to choose well. A few basic rules will carry you most of the way.

First, keep the palette small. Most strong small-business brands use three colours: one main colour, one supporting colour, and one neutral colour for backgrounds and text. The main colour is the one people associate with you, the one on your logo and your shop sign. The supporting colour adds variety on flyers and social media posts. The neutral, usually white, black, or a soft grey, keeps everything readable. More than four colours usually makes a small business look scattered rather than creative.

Second, make sure your main colour and your text colour have strong contrast. Dark text on a light background is easy to read; light text on a dark background can work too. What fails is medium text on a medium background, like grey text on a light blue banner, or yellow text on white. If a customer has to squint, they will move on.

Third, think about where the colours will appear. A colour that looks rich on a computer screen can look dull when printed on a banner, and a colour that looks good on a banner can look harsh on a small phone screen. Test your colours in the real places they will live: your WhatsApp display picture, an Instagram post, a printed flyer, and your shop sign. If a colour fails in one of those places, adjust it before you commit.

## Pick your palette step by step

Here is a simple process any business owner can follow in an afternoon.

Step one: choose your main colour from your three feeling words. Blue suggests trust and calm, which suits professional services, health, and finance. Green suggests freshness and growth, which suits food, agriculture, and wellness. Orange and warm red suggest energy and appetite, which suits food, entertainment, and retail. Black and gold suggest luxury, which suits fashion, beauty, and premium services. Pick the one that matches your words and your industry.

Step two: choose a supporting colour that sits well beside it. A safe method is to pair a strong colour with a softer version of a related colour, or with a warm neutral such as cream or warm grey. If your main colour is a deep blue, a soft sky blue or a warm gold can support it. Avoid pairing two loud colours at full strength, such as bright red with bright yellow, unless your business is deliberately loud and playful.

Step three: choose your neutral. White is the simplest and cleanest choice for most small businesses, and it makes every other colour look better. Black works well for text and for premium brands. A soft grey is useful for backgrounds on a website or a price list. Write down the exact colour values once you decide, using the hex codes your designer gives you, and keep them in one document so every designer, printer, and social media person you work with uses the same shades.

Step four: apply the palette to one real piece of marketing and look at it honestly. Design a simple flyer or a WhatsApp status image with your three colours and your business name. Show it to three people who are not involved in your business and ask them what kind of business it looks like and how it makes them feel. If their answers match your three feeling words, your palette is working. If they describe something completely different, change one colour and test again.

## Mistakes that make small businesses look unprofessional

A few common mistakes undo good colour choices. Watch out for these.

The first mistake is using too many colours. A flyer with six bright colours looks like a carnival, not a business. Stick to your three, and let white space do the rest of the work.

The second mistake is changing colours for every campaign. A Valentine's Day flyer in red and a Sallah flyer in green are fine as seasonal touches, but your logo, your main colour, and your overall look should stay the same. Customers recognise you by what stays constant.

The third mistake is copying a big brand's colours. Using the same blue as a famous bank or the same red as a famous soft drink does not make customers trust you more. It makes your business forgettable, because people associate those colours with the bigger brand, not with you.

The fourth mistake is choosing colours that are hard to read. Light grey text on a white background, yellow text on a white background, and thin coloured text on a busy photo are all common problems. Readability is not a design preference; it is a business requirement. If customers cannot read your price, your phone number, or your address, the design has failed no matter how pretty it looks.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How many colours should a small business use?**

Three: one main colour, one supporting colour, and one neutral such as white, black, or soft grey. That is enough to look interesting without looking scattered.

**Should my brand colours match my logo?**

Yes. The main colour of your logo usually becomes the main colour of your brand. If your logo already exists, build your palette around its colours.

**What if my favourite colour does not suit my business?**

Use it in a smaller role. If your favourite colour is bright pink and you run a law firm, it should not be your main colour, but it can appear as a small accent. Your taste matters, but the customer's expectation matters more.

**Do I need a designer to choose my brand colours?**

Not to choose them, but a designer is useful for refining them. Pick the direction yourself, then ask a designer to fine-tune the exact shades and apply them to your logo, flyer, and social media templates.

## Choose colours your customers will remember

Your brand colours are one of the cheapest and most powerful tools your business has. They cost nothing to choose well, and they work for you every time a customer sees your shop, your status, or your flyer. Pick three colours that match how you want customers to feel, test them where customers actually see them, and then use them the same way everywhere. Over time, those colours become a shortcut in your customers' minds: they see the colour, and they think of you before they read a single word.
