Ask a small business owner in Nigeria how much profit they made last month, and you will often get a pause, a guess, or a laugh. Not because the business is doing badly, but because the owner honestly does not know. The money from sales goes into the same account as the money for food, rent, school fees, and family emergencies. When cash is needed at home, it comes out of the business. When the business needs stock, the money comes from wherever it is available. At the end of the month, there is no clear answer to the simplest question in business: did I make money or lose money?

Mixing business money with personal money is one of the most common habits among Nigerian small businesses, and one of the most damaging. It hides your real profit, it makes it impossible to know whether you can afford to grow, and it quietly drains the business until one day there is no money for stock and no one can explain where it went. It also causes problems when you need a loan, when you register your business properly, or when you want to know whether a product line is worth keeping. A bank that asks for your business records will not be impressed by a personal account full of food purchases and transfers to relatives.

Separating your money does not require an accountant or expensive software. It requires one decision — that business money and personal money live in different places — and a few simple habits that take minutes a day. This guide shows you how to open and use a business account, pay yourself a proper salary, keep simple records that show your real profit, and handle the moments when the two sides of your life try to mix again.

## Open a Business Account and Use It for Everything

The first step is to open a separate bank account for your business and route all business money through it. Every sale you make, whether by transfer, POS, or cash, should end up in this account. Every business expense — stock, transport, packaging, rent for the shop, staff wages, advertising — should be paid from this account. Your personal spending should never come out of it. When money flows through one account, you can see at a glance how much came in, how much went out, and how much is left.

Most Nigerian banks offer accounts designed for small businesses, and many can be opened with a business name registration, your means of identification, your BVN, and a passport photograph. Some banks also offer accounts for sole traders that need less documentation. Visit two or three banks and compare: ask about the monthly charges, the transfer fees, the POS charges, and whether they offer a mobile app that makes it easy to check your balance and send money. Choose the bank whose charges are fair and whose app you find easy to use, because you will check this account often.

If you are a sole trader, you may be tempted to open a second personal account and call it your business account. That is better than nothing, and it is a reasonable first step if you cannot yet open a formal business account. But it is only a halfway measure. A proper business account in your business name makes your business look serious to customers, suppliers, and banks, and it makes your records far cleaner. Aim to open the formal business account as soon as you can, even if you start with a second personal account this week.

Once the account is open, commit to the rule: no business money enters your personal account, and no personal spending leaves the business account. If a customer mistakenly pays into your personal account, transfer the money to the business account the same day. If you pay for a business item with your personal money, record it and pay yourself back from the business account. The rule only works if there are no exceptions, because every exception becomes a habit, and every habit becomes a mess.

## Pay Yourself a Salary, Not Whatever Is in the Till

The biggest leak in most small businesses is the owner. When the business and the owner share one purse, every personal need becomes a business expense. School fees, a family wedding, a new phone, a medical bill — each one takes a little from the business, and none of it is recorded as the owner's pay. At the end of the month, the owner has no idea how much they actually took, and the business has no idea how much it actually earned. This is how profitable businesses go broke.

The fix is to pay yourself a fixed salary, just as you would pay an employee. Decide on a monthly amount that covers your personal needs and is realistic for what the business earns. If your business makes about 300,000 naira in profit in a good month, a salary of 100,000 or 120,000 naira may be reasonable. If the business is still small, start with a smaller amount — even 50,000 naira a month is a good start. The exact figure matters less than the discipline: the same amount, paid on the same day each month, transferred from the business account to your personal account.

Treat that salary as your personal money. Once it lands in your personal account, spend it however you like — food, rent, transport, savings — without guilt. The business has paid you, and what remains in the business account belongs to the business. If you need more money personally in a particular month, do not simply take it from the business. Ask yourself whether the business can afford an extra payment, record it as an additional salary or an owner's withdrawal, and keep it visible in your records. What gets recorded gets controlled. What is taken silently disappears.

## Keep Simple Records You Can Actually Maintain

Many business owners avoid record keeping because they imagine it means complicated accounting. It does not. For a small business, good records mean writing down what came in and what went out, every day, in one place. A simple notebook works. A spreadsheet on your phone works even better, because you can add it up quickly. The tool does not matter. The daily habit matters.

Record every sale and every expense, on the day it happens. For sales, note the date, what was sold, and how much was received. For expenses, note the date, what was bought, and how much was spent. If you sell on credit, write down who owes you and how much, and cross it off when they pay. At the end of each week, add up the sales and the expenses. At the end of each month, do the same. Your profit for the month is your total sales minus your total expenses, including your salary. If that number is positive, your business made money. If it is negative, your business lost money, and you need to find out why.

Keep your receipts and proof of payment. When you buy stock, keep the receipt or take a photo of it. When a customer pays by transfer, save the alert or screenshot. When you pay for delivery, keep the waybill. These records protect you in two ways: they prove your expenses if anyone ever questions them, and they let you check your own numbers when something does not add up. Keep them in one envelope or one folder on your phone, organised by month. You do not need a filing cabinet. You need one place where everything goes.

Set aside time each week — even thirty minutes on a Sunday evening — to review your records. Look at your total sales for the week, your biggest expenses, and how much cash is in the business account. Ask yourself three questions: did I make a profit this week, is there enough money for next week's stock, and is there anything I spent money on that did not help the business? This short weekly review is the habit that turns record keeping from a chore into a tool. Business owners who review their numbers weekly make better decisions than those who only look at them when there is a crisis.

## Build a Small Buffer Before You Spend on Growth

A business without savings is a business one bad week away from trouble. A delivery goes wrong, a supplier raises prices, a customer delays payment, or sales drop during a slow season — and suddenly there is no money for stock. Many business owners respond by borrowing, often at high cost, or by dipping into personal savings. The better response is to have a small buffer already sitting in the business account, built up slowly from your profits.

Aim to keep a business buffer equal to about one month of your regular expenses. If your shop costs you roughly 150,000 naira a month in stock, rent, transport, and wages, then a buffer of 150,000 naira in the business account gives you breathing room when things go wrong. You do not need to build it in one month. Set aside a small amount from every week's profit — even 5,000 or 10,000 naira — and move it into a separate savings account or a fixed deposit linked to the business. The amount grows faster than you expect, and the peace of mind is worth more than the money itself.

Once your buffer is in place, you can think about growth from a position of strength. Want to buy more stock, rent a bigger space, or hire an assistant? Check your profit records, check your buffer, and decide whether the business can afford it without risking its survival. Growth paid for with profit is sustainable. Growth paid for with money that should have gone to stock or rent is how businesses collapse. The buffer is what lets you tell the difference.

## Handle the Situations Where Money Gets Mixed

Even with the best intentions, there are moments when business and personal money try to mix. A relative asks for a loan from the business. A family emergency needs cash immediately. A customer pays you in cash and you need money for transport home. These moments are normal in Nigerian life, and handling them badly is what undoes months of good discipline. The answer is not to pretend they will never happen. The answer is to have a rule for each one before it happens.

For family and friends who ask for money from the business, the rule is simple: the business does not lend money. If you want to help someone, help them from your salary, after it has been paid to you. If your salary is not enough to help, then the business cannot afford to help either. This is hard, especially in a culture where family support is expected, but it is also the reason many family businesses fail. Every loan taken from the business without a record is money the business will never see again. Be kind, but be clear: the business account is not a family account.

For emergencies, keep a small personal emergency fund separate from both your business account and your daily personal spending. Even 50,000 naira set aside for emergencies means you do not have to raid the business when something unexpected happens. Build it slowly, the same way you build your business buffer. Emergencies are not a reason to break your separation rule. They are the reason the rule exists.

For cash sales, handle the cash properly. When a customer pays you in cash, put the money in a cash box or an envelope marked for the business, and deposit it into the business account at the end of the day or the next morning. Do not spend cash from sales on personal items on the way home. That small habit — spending the day's cash before it reaches the bank — is one of the most common ways business money disappears. If you need transport money, take it from your salary, not from the day's sales.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I really need a business bank account if my business is very small?

Yes, even for a very small business. A separate account is the single most useful step you can take, because it makes your money visible. Without it, you cannot know your real profit, and you cannot prove your income when you need a loan or a grant. Start with a second personal account if a formal business account is not possible yet, and upgrade to a business account in your business name as soon as you can.

### How much salary should I pay myself?

It depends on what your business earns. A good starting point is an amount that covers your basic personal needs without taking more than about a third of your average monthly profit. If your business makes 150,000 naira in profit in a typical month, a salary of 50,000 naira is reasonable. Review it every three months and adjust it as profits change. The exact figure matters less than the habit of paying yourself a fixed amount on a fixed day.

### What if my business is not making enough profit to pay me a salary?

Pay yourself something small, even if it is only 20,000 or 30,000 naira a month. The habit matters more than the amount, and a small salary keeps the separation real. At the same time, look hard at your numbers: are your prices too low, are your expenses too high, or are you not selling enough? A business that cannot pay its owner anything after several months needs a change in pricing, costs, or sales, not just patience.

### Can I use my business account to buy something personal if I pay it back?

It is better not to, because each exception weakens the rule. If you must, record it the same day as a personal withdrawal, and transfer the money back to the business account within a few days. Treat it as a loan from the business to you, with a clear record. If these withdrawals happen often, your salary is probably too low, and you should review it rather than keep borrowing from the business.

### What records do I need to keep for tax purposes?

Keep records of all your sales and all your expenses, with dates and amounts, plus your receipts and payment confirmations. The Federal Inland Revenue Service and your state tax authority may ask for these if your business is registered. Even if your business is small and informal, clean records make it easy to register properly later and to show a bank or an investor that your business is real and well managed.

## What to Do Next

First, open a separate bank account for your business this week. Compare two or three banks for charges and mobile app quality, open the account in your business name if you can, and start routing every sale and every business expense through it from the day it is opened. Move any business money currently sitting in your personal account into the new account on the same day.

Second, decide on your monthly salary and set the date you will pay it. Choose an amount your business can afford, even if it is small, and transfer it from the business account to your personal account on the same day each month. Write the amount and the date down where you will see it, and treat it as a fixed commitment, not an optional one.

Third, start a simple daily record today. Use a notebook or a spreadsheet on your phone, and write down every sale and every expense before the day ends. Review your totals every Sunday evening. Within a month, you will know your real profit for the first time — and that knowledge is the foundation of every good business decision you will make from now on.
