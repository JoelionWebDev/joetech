## Why Food Delivery Is Growing in Nigeria

Food delivery is growing fast in Nigerian cities because people's lives are getting busier. Office workers in Lekki, Ikeja, and Wuse do not always have time to cook or to drive to a restaurant. Students order food to their hostels. Families order dinner on weekends. Restaurants know this, but many of them do not have their own riders. That gap is your business opportunity.

A food delivery business connects hungry customers with restaurants and home caterers, and earns money on every delivery. You do not need to cook the food yourself. You need reliable riders, a simple way for customers to order, and a reputation for delivering hot food on time. Get those three things right and customers will order from you again and again.

You can start with one bike and one rider — even with your own bike — and grow as orders increase. This guide explains the models to choose from, the real costs, how to work with restaurants, and how to price deliveries so the business makes a profit.

## Choose Your Business Model First

There are three common models for a food delivery business in Nigeria. Choose one to start with, and add the others later if they fit.

The first model is a delivery-only service. You partner with restaurants and caterers who cook the food, and you handle the delivery. The customer orders from the restaurant, the restaurant calls you, and your rider collects the food and delivers it. You earn a delivery fee on each order. This model needs the least capital because you do not buy food or run a kitchen.

The second model is a food ordering platform. Customers order through your WhatsApp, Instagram, or a simple ordering page, you buy the food from a partner restaurant, and your rider delivers it. You earn a delivery fee plus a small commission from the restaurant. This model gives you more control over the customer experience, but it needs more working capital because you pay the restaurant before the customer pays you.

The third model is a cloud kitchen with delivery. You cook the food yourself — or hire a cook — and deliver it directly to customers. This model earns the highest margin per order, because you keep both the food profit and the delivery fee. It also needs the most capital, a kitchen, and food handling skills. Most beginners should start with delivery-only, learn the routes and the customers, and consider a kitchen later.

Pick the model that matches your money and your skills. A rider with a bike and good knowledge of Lagos streets should start with delivery-only. A good cook with a kitchen should consider the cloud kitchen model. Do not try all three at once.

## How Much Capital Do You Need?

A delivery-only business can start with ₦300,000 to ₦700,000. A cloud kitchen needs more, often ₦800,000 to ₦1,500,000. Here is a breakdown for a delivery-only start of about ₦500,000:

A motorcycle: ₦250,000 to ₦400,000 for a fairly used bike in good condition, or more for a new one. The bike is your main asset. Buy one that is fuel-efficient, easy to repair, and common enough that spare parts are available in your area. Check the engine, the brakes, and the tyres before you pay.

Delivery boxes and rider gear: ₦30,000 to ₦50,000. An insulated delivery box keeps food hot, a rain cover protects it, and a branded shirt or jacket makes your rider easy to recognise. Customers trust a rider who looks professional.

Phone and ordering tools: ₦20,000 to ₦40,000. A reliable Android phone with a good battery, a power bank, and mobile data. Your phone is your control room — orders, rider tracking, and customer messages all run through it.

Registration and insurance: ₦30,000 to ₦60,000. Register your business name, and insure the bike against theft and accidents if you can. Insurance feels expensive until the day you need it.

Working capital and reserve: ₦50,000 to ₦100,000. This covers fuel for the first weeks, rider pay, and a reserve for repairs. A bike that breaks down with no repair money stops your whole business.

If you already own a bike, your startup cost drops sharply. If you do not, consider hiring a rider who owns a bike and paying them per delivery, rather than buying a bike on your first day. That way you test the demand before you spend on an asset.

## Partnering with Restaurants and Caterers

Restaurants are your suppliers and your partners. Start by listing the restaurants, bukas, and caterers in your area that already have customers but no delivery service. Visit them in person, introduce yourself, and explain what you offer: fast, reliable delivery, careful handling of food, and a clear delivery fee.

Agree the terms in writing, even if it is just a WhatsApp message you both confirm. Agree the delivery fee, who pays it, how orders are confirmed, how long the restaurant needs to prepare an order, and what happens if food is late or damaged. Clear terms prevent arguments later.

Start with three to five restaurants, not twenty. A small group lets you learn their menus, their preparation times, and their busiest hours. Visit during lunch and dinner rushes to see how they work. A restaurant that takes an hour to prepare a simple order will make your riders late and your customers angry, no matter how fast the rider rides.

Offer the restaurant something useful. A weekly report of how many orders you delivered for them, a small discount on delivery for their regular customers, or a free delivery on their slowest day can turn a trial partnership into a long-term one. Restaurants keep the delivery partners who make their customers happy.

## Setting Up Ordering on WhatsApp and Instagram

Most small food delivery businesses in Nigeria run on WhatsApp Business. Set up a profile with your business name, your delivery areas, your opening hours, and your delivery fees. Use the catalogue feature to list your partner restaurants and their popular dishes with prices.

Make ordering simple. A customer should be able to send one message — the dish, the quantity, and their address — and get a clear reply with the total cost, including delivery, and the expected delivery time. If ordering takes five messages back and forth, customers will give up.

Use Instagram to show food, not just to list prices. Short videos of a rider collecting a hot meal, or a customer receiving their order, build trust. Post your delivery areas and your hours every week, so new followers know exactly where you deliver.

Keep your delivery areas tight at first. Choose one or two neighbourhoods — for example, Lekki Phase 1 and Chevron, or Ikeja and its surrounding estates — and serve them well. A small area means faster deliveries, lower fuel costs, and happier customers. Expand to new areas only when your current area is covered reliably.

## Pricing Deliveries for Profit

Delivery pricing must cover fuel, rider pay, bike maintenance, and your profit. Here is a worked example:

A delivery within your core area costs you about ₦800 in fuel and rider pay, plus ₦200 for bike maintenance and phone costs. Your total cost is ₦1,000. If you charge ₦1,500 for that delivery, your profit is ₦500 per order. Complete 20 deliveries a day and your daily profit is ₦10,000. Over 26 working days, that is ₦260,000 a month before rent and other fixed costs.

Charge by distance, not by a single flat fee. A delivery across the street should cost less than a delivery across town. Create three zones: a near zone, a middle zone, and a far zone, with a clear fee for each. Publish the zones and the fees so customers know the cost before they order.

Consider a small commission from restaurants as well. If a restaurant agrees to pay you 10 percent of the food price for each order you deliver, a ₦5,000 food order earns you ₦500 extra. Agree this in advance, and do not hide it from the customer — the customer pays the menu price plus the delivery fee, and the commission is between you and the restaurant.

Avoid free delivery unless you can afford it. Free delivery brings many orders, but each one costs you money. If you want to offer it, limit it to orders above a certain amount — for example, free delivery on orders above ₦15,000 — or to one day a week as a promotion.

## Hiring and Managing Riders

Your riders are your business. Customers may never meet you, but they meet your rider at their door. Hire riders who know the area well, ride safely, and speak politely to customers.

Check the basics before you hire: a valid rider's permit, a working phone, knowledge of the streets in your delivery area, and a reference from a previous employer or a known person. Test them with a few trial deliveries before you give them regular work.

Pay riders fairly and on time. You can pay a fixed daily amount, a rate per delivery, or a mix of both. A per-delivery rate motivates riders to complete more orders, but a small daily base gives them security on slow days. Agree the pay structure before they start, and put it in writing.

Set clear rules: riders must confirm collection with a photo or a message, must call the customer if they cannot find the address, must keep food upright in the delivery box, and must report any delay immediately. A rider who hides a delay causes more damage than the delay itself.

Track every delivery. A simple sheet — order number, restaurant, customer, rider, time collected, time delivered — tells you which riders are fast, which areas are slow, and where problems happen. Review it weekly and talk to your riders about what you find. Good riders appreciate a manager who notices their work.

## Handling Late, Cold, or Wrong Orders

Things will go wrong. A restaurant will be late, a rider will take a wrong turn, or a customer will receive the wrong dish. How you handle it decides whether that customer orders again.

When an order is late, tell the customer before they call you. A short message — what happened, and the new delivery time — is far better than silence. Offer a small apology, such as a discount on their next order, when the delay is your fault. Do not blame the restaurant to the customer. Fix it, apologise, and sort the cause with the restaurant afterwards.

If food arrives cold or spilled, apologise, offer a replacement or a refund, and find out what went wrong. A rider who rushes over bad roads with a loose box will spill food. A box that does not close properly will let heat out. Fix the cause, not just the complaint.

Keep a record of complaints. If the same restaurant is late three times in a week, talk to them. If the same rider is late every day, retrain them or replace them. Patterns tell you where the business is breaking down.

## Marketing That Brings Repeat Orders

Your best marketing is a hot meal delivered on time. After that, make it easy for customers to order again. Save their address and their usual order, with their permission, so repeat ordering takes one message.

Post on WhatsApp Status daily, in the hours before lunch and dinner. A photo of a popular dish, a short video of a rider setting out, or a reminder of your delivery areas will catch people when they are deciding what to eat.

Target offices and estates. Offer a weekly lunch deal for an office — for example, a set menu delivered at the same time every weekday. One office ordering 15 lunches a day is more valuable than fifteen separate customers ordering once.

Partner with gyms, salons, and schools in your area. Their customers are your customers. A small flyer or a banner in a busy salon, with your WhatsApp number and delivery areas, can bring orders for months.

Ask for reviews. After a successful delivery, ask the customer to share a short review on WhatsApp or Instagram. A screenshot of a happy customer, shared with permission, is more persuasive than any advert you write yourself.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

Delivering too far, too soon, is the most common mistake. A wide delivery area means long trips, cold food, high fuel costs, and late deliveries. Start small, serve one area well, and expand only when you can do it without slowing down.

Undercharging is the second. A delivery fee that does not cover fuel, rider pay, and maintenance will bankrupt you quietly, even while orders increase. Calculate your cost per delivery and charge above it.

Ignoring bike maintenance is the third. A bike that breaks down during a dinner rush loses you the evening's orders and the repair cost. Service the bike regularly, check tyres and brakes weekly, and keep a small repair reserve.

Poor communication is the fourth. Customers forgive a late delivery when they are told early. They do not forgive silence. Keep customers updated, answer messages quickly, and confirm every order before the rider sets out.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does it cost to start a food delivery business in Nigeria?

A delivery-only business costs about ₦300,000 to ₦700,000, mostly for a motorcycle, delivery gear, a phone, registration, and working capital. If you already own a bike, you can start for far less. A cloud kitchen with delivery costs more, often ₦800,000 to ₦1,500,000.

### Do I need to cook the food myself?

No. In the delivery-only model, restaurants cook the food and you deliver it. You only need to cook if you choose the cloud kitchen model, where you prepare the food and deliver it yourself.

### How do I find restaurants to partner with?

Visit restaurants, bukas, and caterers in your area in person. Explain your delivery service, agree the delivery fee and preparation times, and start with three to five partners. Offer reliable, on-time delivery and a weekly report of orders to build trust.

### How much can a food delivery business earn?

It depends on your daily orders and your delivery fees. A business completing 20 deliveries a day at ₦500 profit per delivery earns about ₦10,000 a day before fixed costs. Earnings grow as you add restaurants, riders, and delivery areas, but only if your cost per delivery stays under your fee.

## Final Word

A food delivery business succeeds on reliability. Customers do not remember the fastest rider or the cheapest fee. They remember the business that delivered hot food, on time, every time, and that answered the phone when something went wrong. Start with one area, a few good restaurants, and one reliable rider. Learn your costs, keep your customers updated, and grow one neighbourhood at a time. Do that well, and your customers will order from you not because you are the only option, but because you are the one they trust.
