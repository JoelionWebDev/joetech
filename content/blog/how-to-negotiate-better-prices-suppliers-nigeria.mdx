Every naira you save on buying is a naira added to your profit. Yet most small business owners in Nigeria spend hours negotiating with customers over ₦500, and accept their supplier's first price without a word. That is backwards. Your profit is made when you buy, not only when you sell. If you buy a bag of rice for ₦85,000 when your competitor buys it for ₦78,000, you start every sale at a disadvantage — and no amount of clever marketing will fix it.

Negotiation is not quarrelling or begging. It is a professional conversation in which both sides try to reach a price that works. Nigerian markets — from Balogun to Onitsha Main Market, from Mile 12 to Ariaria — run on negotiation. Everybody expects it. A supplier who quotes you a price expects you to push back. If you do not, they will assume you are happy, and the price will stay high.

This guide shows you how to prepare for a negotiation, what to say, which levers actually move prices in Nigeria, and how to keep a good relationship with your supplier while paying less.

## Before You Negotiate: Know Your Numbers

You cannot negotiate well if you do not know what you are paying now, what the market price is, and what your business can afford. Preparation is where most negotiations are won, before a single word is spoken.

### Record your current costs

Write down, for your top 10 items, what you pay now, who you buy from, how often you buy, and how much you buy each time. If you do not have this written down, spend a week recording it. A food vendor should know the price of a bag of rice, a carton of chicken, a keg of palm oil and a bag of pepper. A fashion retailer should know the cost per bale, per dozen, and per piece. A phone accessories seller should know the cost of each model of charger, case and screen guard.

### Find the real market price

Visit at least three other suppliers and ask for the same item, in the same quantity, with the same quality. Check prices in a different market if you can: the price of yams in Mile 12 is not the price of yams in Bodija. Ask other business owners in your trade association what they pay. Search online marketplaces and WhatsApp supplier groups, but verify with a phone call, because online prices are often outdated.

Write the range down: the lowest price you found, the highest, and the average. That range is your negotiating power. If your supplier charges ₦90,000 and two other suppliers charge ₦80,000 and ₦82,000 for the same quality, you have a fact, not a feeling.

### Know your walk-away price

Decide, before you talk, the highest price you will accept and the point at which you will walk away. This is your walk-away price. If you do not decide it in advance, you will be pressured into accepting a bad deal in the moment. Write it on your phone and keep it in your pocket during the negotiation.

Also know your volume. How much do you buy in a month? How much will you buy in the next three months? A supplier will give a better price to a customer who buys 50 cartons a month than to one who buys two. If your volume is small, be honest about it, and focus on other levers, like paying cash or buying regularly.

## The 7 Levers That Actually Lower Prices

Price is not the only thing you can negotiate. In Nigerian markets, several other terms matter just as much, and suppliers are often more willing to move on these than on the headline price.

### 1. Buy in bulk

This is the most powerful lever. Buying 20 bags at once costs less per bag than buying two. If you cannot afford 20 bags alone, join with two or three other small businesses and buy together. Traders in Onitsha and Ariaria do this every day. Agree in advance how you will split the goods and the cost, and put it in writing, even if it is just a WhatsApp message confirming the split.

Be careful with perishable goods. Buying 100 crates of tomatoes at a discount is a bad deal if half of them spoil before you sell them. Bulk buying works best for goods with a long shelf life: rice, oil, flour, cement, fabric, phone accessories, packaged goods.

### 2. Pay cash, and pay fast

Cash is king in Nigerian markets. A supplier who gets paid today can use that money to buy more stock today. A supplier who waits two weeks for a transfer is effectively lending you money. Offer to pay in cash, or by instant transfer on the spot, in exchange for a discount. Even a 3 to 5 percent discount for immediate payment is worth taking.

If you regularly buy on credit, consider the opposite: offer to pay a week early in exchange for a better price. Suppliers remember customers who pay early, and they reward them.

### 3. Commit to regular orders

Suppliers love predictable customers. If you promise to buy 10 cartons every week for the next three months, and you keep that promise, you become a customer worth keeping. Ask for a price in return for that commitment: "If I commit to 10 cartons a week for three months, what is your best price?" Get the commitment and the price in writing, even if it is a simple message.

### 4. Compare, and say so politely

You do not need to threaten a supplier. You need to let them know you have options. "I like your quality, and I have been buying from you for a year. But I have been quoted ₦78,000 for the same bag by another supplier. Can you match ₦80,000 so I can stay with you?" That sentence is respectful, honest, and powerful. It tells the supplier that you value the relationship, but you also know the market.

Never lie about a competitor's price. Suppliers talk to each other, and a lie discovered later destroys trust permanently.

### 5. Negotiate the whole deal, not just the price

If the supplier will not move on price, ask about delivery, packaging, payment terms, or a free extra. "If the price stays at ₦85,000, can you deliver it to my shop for free?" Free delivery from Mile 12 to Lekki can be worth ₦10,000 or more. "Can you give me 30 days to pay instead of 14?" That extra time is worth money too. "Can you add one extra carton free if I buy 20?" Traders call this "jara," and it is a legitimate part of negotiation.

### 6. Time your buying well

Prices move with the seasons. Yams are cheapest at harvest. Tomatoes are cheapest when they flood the market, and expensive when they are scarce. Imported goods rise when the naira falls and when clearing costs rise at the port. Buy in season and store what you can. A caterer who buys palm oil in bulk when it is cheap saves money for months.

Also buy at the right time of the week and month. Market prices are often lower early in the morning when traders want to make their first sale, and at month end when suppliers want to hit their targets. It is not a huge difference, but it adds up over a year.

### 7. Build the relationship

In Nigeria, business is personal. The supplier who knows your name, your business, and your family will give you a better price than the stranger who walks in once. Visit your supplier regularly, even when you are not buying. Greet them. Ask about their family. Pay on time. Recommend them to other buyers when they deserve it.

A good relationship also gives you information. Your supplier will tell you when a price is about to rise, so you can buy before it does. They will tell you when a new product arrives. They will give you the first pick of the best stock. That information is worth more than a one-time discount.

But a relationship is not a reason to overpay forever. Loyalty should be mutual. If you have been loyal for a year and your supplier's prices are now well above the market, a loyal customer deserves a fair price. Say so, respectfully.

## How the Conversation Should Go

Negotiation is a conversation, not a confrontation. Here is a structure that works in Nigerian markets, whether you are in a shop in Balogun or on the phone with a distributor in Kano.

**Open warmly.** Greet the supplier. Ask how business is. Show interest before you talk about price. A rushed, aggressive opening puts people on guard.

**State what you want clearly.** "I want to buy 20 bags of rice every month, and I want us to agree a price that works for both of us for the next three months." Clarity shows you are serious.

**Let them speak first.** Ask, "What is your best price for 20 bags, paid in cash today?" Let them give a number. Do not accept the first number. The first number is almost never the best number.

**Counter with a reason, not just a number.** "I was hoping for ₦78,000, because that is what I have been quoted elsewhere, and my margin is tight at the moment." A reason is harder to refuse than a bare number.

**Pause and listen.** After you counter, be quiet. Silence is uncomfortable, and many people fill it by improving their offer. Let the supplier respond. Do not rush to accept the first counter-offer. Ask, "Is that the best you can do?"

**Trade, do not just take.** If they move on price, offer something in return: a bigger order, faster payment, a longer commitment. Negotiation works best when both sides give something.

**Confirm in writing.** When you agree, write it down immediately: item, quantity, price, delivery, payment terms, and date. Send it by WhatsApp and ask the supplier to confirm with a reply. That message is your record if there is a disagreement later.

**Thank them.** End warmly, even if you did not get everything you wanted. You will negotiate with this person again, and how you leave the conversation matters.

### What not to do

Do not insult the supplier's price or their goods. Do not threaten to leave in anger. Do not bluff about an order you cannot place. Do not negotiate when you are in a hurry — a supplier can sense desperation, and the price will reflect it. And do not agree to a price you cannot afford just to end an awkward conversation. It is better to walk away politely and return tomorrow.

## Negotiating with Different Kinds of Suppliers

### Market traders

Market traders expect negotiation. Their first price is a starting point, not a final price. Be friendly, be firm, and be ready to walk to the next stall. Walking away is the strongest signal you can send in a market. Often, the trader will call you back with a better price before you reach the next stall. But only walk away if you are genuinely willing to buy elsewhere. A bluff that is called leaves you with nothing.

### Distributors and wholesalers

Distributors are more formal. They have price lists, and their margins are thinner than a market trader's. Negotiation here is about volume, payment terms and exclusivity, not haggling over ₦500. Come with your numbers: your monthly volume, your payment history, your growth plan. A distributor who sees you as a growing, reliable customer will invest in keeping you.

### Online and WhatsApp suppliers

Verify before you pay. Ask for a video call to see the goods. Ask for the business address and a CAC registration if the order is large. Start with a small test order before committing to a big one. Negotiate the same way you would in person, but be extra careful about delivery costs and return policies, which are often where online deals go wrong.

## When the Price Goes Up — And It Will

Prices in Nigeria rise often, driven by fuel costs, exchange rates, transport and seasonal scarcity. A price increase is not always a reason to change supplier. Sometimes it is genuine, and every supplier is raising prices at the same time. Your job is to tell the difference between a genuine increase and an opportunistic one.

When your supplier raises prices, ask three questions:

- What caused the increase?
- How much have other suppliers raised their prices?
- How long will the new price last?

If the cause is real — fuel went up, the naira fell, the harvest was poor — and other suppliers have raised prices by a similar amount, accept it, and look at whether you need to raise your own prices. If only your supplier has raised prices, and the reason is vague, push back and compare with competitors.

Protect yourself against sudden increases by keeping a buffer stock of your most important items when prices are stable, and by having a second supplier ready. A backup supplier is not disloyalty. It is good business. If your main supplier fails, your business should not stop.

Also, review your own prices when your costs rise. Many small businesses absorb cost increases for months, quietly losing money, because they are afraid to raise their prices. If your costs have risen by 15 percent, your prices probably need to rise too. Tell your customers honestly why, and most of them will understand — because their own costs have risen too.

## Your Monthly Supplier Routine

Make supplier management a routine, not a crisis response:

- **First week of the month:** Check prices from two other suppliers for your top five items. Record them.
- **Second week:** Review your stock. What is running low? What is not moving? What should you buy in bulk this month?
- **Third week:** Pay your suppliers on time, and confirm any outstanding balances. A clean payment record is your best negotiating tool.
- **Fourth week:** Review your costs against your sales. Has your margin improved or worsened? Which supplier gave you the best deal this month? Which one needs a conversation?

Keep a supplier list: name, phone, what they supply, price, payment terms, and a rating for reliability and quality. When you need a new supplier, you will not be starting from zero.

## A Real Example

A small supermarket owner in Surulere buys 50 cartons of a popular soft drink every week. Her supplier charges ₦12,000 per carton. She checks two other suppliers and finds the same drink for ₦11,200 and ₦11,500. She goes back to her supplier and says: "I have been buying 50 cartons a week from you for eight months, and I pay cash every time. Another supplier is offering ₦11,200. I would rather stay with you, but I need ₦11,400 to make it work."

Her supplier counters at ₦11,600. She agrees, but asks for free delivery, which was costing her ₦5,000 a week. The supplier agrees. Her saving is ₦400 per carton, or ₦20,000 a week, plus ₦5,000 on delivery — ₦25,000 a week, or about ₦100,000 a month. That is money she can use to stock a new product, or to pay a staff bonus. All of it came from one honest conversation, backed by real market prices.

## Your Action Plan This Week

1. **Today:** List your top 10 items, your current supplier, and what you pay for each.
2. **Tomorrow:** Get quotes from two other suppliers for the same items, in the same quantity and quality.
3. **Day 3:** Work out your walk-away price for your most important item.
4. **Day 4:** Visit your main supplier. Use the conversation structure in this guide. Ask for a better price, a bulk discount, or free delivery.
5. **Day 5:** Confirm whatever you agree in writing on WhatsApp, and record it in your supplier list.
6. **Day 6:** Set up a backup supplier for your most important item, even if you do not buy from them yet.
7. **Day 7:** Review your margins with the new prices. If your costs have fallen, decide whether to keep the saving as profit or to lower your prices and win more customers.

Negotiation is a skill, and like any skill, it improves with practice. The first conversation will feel awkward. The fifth will feel natural. And the savings will compound: a 5 percent saving on your costs, repeated every month, is the difference between a business that survives and a business that grows. Start with your biggest cost, prepare your numbers, and have the conversation. Your supplier expects it — and your profit depends on it.
