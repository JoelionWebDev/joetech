Snails are one of the quietest money-makers in Nigerian agriculture. They need little space, eat leaves and farm waste, make no noise, and produce meat that many Nigerians love — in pepper soup, stews and as a delicacy at parties and restaurants. A small snail farm can start in a backyard in Lagos, Ibadan, Enugu, Benin City or Port Harcourt, and grow into a steady side income or a full business.

This guide walks you through snail farming step by step: choosing the right species, building pens, feeding, breeding, keeping snails healthy, and selling them at a good price. It is written for a beginner with modest capital, not for a large commercial farm. Snail farming rewards patience and consistency more than big spending, so the plan here is to start small, learn the routine, and expand with money the farm has earned.

## 1. Understand Snail Farming Before You Start

Snail farming, also called heliciculture, is the rearing of edible land snails for meat. In Nigeria, the most popular species is the Giant African Land Snail, known scientifically as Archachatina marginata. It grows large, lays many eggs, and is the type most buyers ask for. Two other types you will hear about are Achatina achatina and Achatina fulica. Achatina achatina is also popular but can be harder to manage, while Achatina fulica grows fast but is smaller and less preferred by many buyers. For a beginner, Archachatina marginata is usually the safest choice.

Snails are slow growers. Depending on species, feed and care, a snail can take six months to over a year to reach a good market size. That is the first truth to accept: this is not a business that pays in four weeks. Your first real income often comes after several months, when your first batch reaches market weight and your breeders start laying eggs regularly.

Snails also need a calm, damp environment. They are most active at night and in cool, humid conditions, and they suffer in hot, dry, direct sunlight. That is why pens are built in shade, with moist soil, and why watering and feeding are usually done in the evening. If you understand those two facts — slow growth and a need for moisture and shade — you already understand half of snail farming.

## 2. Choose Your Farming System and Pen Type

There are three main ways to keep snails in Nigeria. Choose one that fits your space and budget, and build it properly before you buy any snails.

**Free-range or open pen:** a fenced area of land, usually with a low wall or netting buried into the ground so snails cannot escape, planted with leaves they can eat. This is cheap to set up and suits a rural compound with space, but it is harder to protect snails from predators, flooding and theft, and harder to count and manage them.

**Trench or hutch system:** shallow trenches or wooden boxes filled with loose, moist soil, covered with a lid or netting. This is the most common system for beginners. It is easy to build, easy to inspect, and easy to expand by adding another box. A wooden hutch about 1 metre by 1 metre can hold a reasonable number of snails, depending on their size.

**Concrete or block pen:** a low block wall pen with a concrete floor, soil inside, and a wire or net cover. This lasts longer, is easier to clean, and protects better against rats and other animals, but it costs more to build. Many farmers start with hutches and move to block pens once the business is earning.

Whatever system you choose, four things matter: shade, moisture, drainage and security. The pen must be shaded, the soil must stay damp but not waterlogged, rainwater must drain away rather than flood the pen, and the cover must keep out rats, lizards, snakes, soldier ants and thieves. A pen that fails on any of these will lose snails quietly, and you may not notice until it is too late.

## 3. Write a Simple Business Plan and Budget

Write your plan on one or two pages. Start with your goal: are you farming for meat sales, for breeding stock, or for both? Breeding stock — healthy adult snails sold to other farmers — often sells at a higher price per snail than meat snails, but it needs a reputation and healthy, well-kept stock. Meat sales bring steadier, smaller payments from households, restaurants and market traders.

List your start-up costs honestly. They usually include pen construction, point-of-lay or adult snails for breeding, feed and calcium supplements, soil and containers, a watering can, and transport. Prices vary by location and season, so get quotes in your own area before you fix your budget. A small backyard farm can often start with a modest sum, while a larger farm with several block pens will cost considerably more.

Set a realistic timeline. If you buy young snails, allow many months before they reach market size. If you buy mature breeders, they can start laying eggs within weeks, and those eggs hatch in about two to four weeks, giving you a new generation to grow. Work out how many snails you need to sell each month to cover feed, water, transport and your time. Keep a simple record book from day one: date, number of snails bought, feed given, eggs laid, snails sold, price and buyer. That book is what turns a hobby into a business you can measure.

## 4. Choose a Good Site and Prepare It

Snails do best in a cool, shaded, quiet place. A spot under trees, beside a building that blocks the afternoon sun, or under a simple shade of palm fronds or a tarpaulin works well. Avoid open ground where the sun hits directly all day, and avoid land that floods during heavy rain. In Lagos, Ibadan and the South-South, rain can be heavy for months, so drainage is not a small detail — a flooded pen can drown snails and wash away eggs.

The site should be close to water, because you will water the pens regularly to keep the soil moist. It should also be close enough to your house that you can check the snails daily, and secure enough that strangers cannot walk in and take them. A fenced compound is ideal. If you farm in a rented space, agree the arrangement in writing with the landowner before you build anything.

Clear the site of sharp stones, broken glass and rubbish. If you use the free-range system, plant food leaves around the pen — pawpaw, plantain, cocoyam and waterleaf are all useful. Keep the area free of chemicals: do not spray insecticide or herbicide near the pens, and do not use soil from a place where chemicals were recently used. Snails are sensitive, and a small amount of chemical residue can kill a whole pen.

## 5. Build Pens That Keep Snails Safe and Comfortable

Build the pen before you buy snails. A good pen is simple: a container or enclosure, loose soil, shade and a secure cover. For a wooden hutch, use strong wood and a fine wire mesh or net cover that lets air in but keeps snails in and predators out. Raise the hutch slightly off the ground or place it on blocks so rainwater does not sit under it.

Fill the pen with loose, loamy soil to a depth of about 15 to 20 centimetres. The soil should be free of stones and chemicals, and slightly sandy so it drains well. Snails burrow into soil to rest and to lay eggs, so hard, compacted soil will stress them. Keep the soil damp by sprinkling water in the evening, not by flooding it. If you squeeze a handful of soil and only a few drops come out, the moisture is about right.

Add hiding places: pieces of broken clay pot, short lengths of bamboo, or broad leaves. Snails like to hide during the day, and hiding places reduce stress and fighting. Do not overcrowd the pen. Young snails can be kept more closely together, but adult snails need space to move, feed and lay eggs. Overcrowding slows growth, spreads disease and leads to broken shells. Start with one or two pens, learn the routine, and add more pens as your stock grows.

## 6. Buy Healthy Stock and Start Breeding

Buy your first snails from a reputable farmer, not from a random roadside seller. Ask to see the farm, look at the snails, and ask how old they are and what they have been fed. Healthy snails have clean, unbroken shells, a firm body, and they withdraw quickly when touched. Avoid snails with cracked shells, a very light shell, or a body that looks dry and shrunken.

Buy a mix of mature breeders and younger snails. Mature breeders — snails that are already laying or close to laying — will give you eggs sooner, while younger snails are cheaper and will grow into breeders or meat stock later. A common starting point for a small farm is a few dozen breeders plus a larger number of young snails, but the exact number depends on your pen space and budget. Do not fill a pen on the first day and hope for the best; stock it gradually and watch how the snails settle.

When you bring snails home, place them gently in the pen in the evening, sprinkle water, and give them fresh leaves. Leave them alone for a day or two to settle. Snails mate and lay eggs in the soil, usually at night. A single snail can lay dozens of eggs at a time, several times a year, depending on species and care. Check the soil gently every week or two for eggs, and move the eggs to a separate hatching pen or a small container with damp soil so the adults do not damage them. Eggs usually hatch in two to four weeks. Keep the baby snails — called hatchlings — in a separate pen with soft soil, fine leaves and a shallow source of calcium, and handle them as little as possible, because their shells are very fragile.

## 7. Feed Your Snails Well and Cheaply

Feeding is where snail farming is most affordable, because snails eat leaves, fruits and farm waste that many farmers already have. Good everyday feeds include pawpaw leaves and fruit, plantain leaves, cocoyam leaves, waterleaf, cabbage, lettuce, banana, pawpaw and ripe plantain. Give fresh feed in the evening, when snails are active, and remove leftover feed the next day so it does not rot and attract flies.

Snails also need calcium to build strong shells. Without it, shells become thin, break easily, and growth slows. Provide calcium in the form of crushed eggshells, crushed snail shells, limestone powder or bone meal, placed in a small container in the pen. Dry eggshells from your kitchen, washed and crushed, are a free and useful source. Some farmers also give a formulated feed — a mix of maize, soya, wheat offal and calcium — in small quantities to speed growth, especially for snails being raised for market. If you use formulated feed, introduce it gradually and do not let it replace fresh leaves entirely.

Provide clean water. Snails drink and absorb moisture through their bodies, so a shallow dish of clean water, or regular sprinkling, helps. Do not use a deep container that hatchlings can drown in. Avoid salty food, spicy food, citrus in large quantities and any feed that has been sprayed with chemicals. Wash leaves from the market before feeding them, and never feed snails food you would not be comfortable handling yourself. A simple feeding routine — fresh leaves in the evening, calcium always available, water as needed — will carry most of the farm's growth.

## 8. Protect Snails from Pests, Predators and Disease

The biggest threats to a snail farm are not diseases you can see in a textbook — they are rats, soldier ants, lizards, snakes and theft, plus stress from heat, dryness and overcrowding. Prevention is cheaper than cure, and a secure pen is your first medicine.

Keep the pen covered at all times with fine mesh or netting, and bury the edges of the netting or wall a few centimetres into the ground so rats cannot dig under it. Clear grass and rubbish around the pen, because tall grass hides rats and snakes. Some farmers place the legs of a raised hutch in small containers of water or used engine oil to stop soldier ants climbing in — a simple, effective trick, provided the oil never touches the soil inside the pen.

Check the snails daily. Look for cracked shells, snails that have not moved for days, or a smell of decay, and remove dead snails immediately so they do not contaminate the pen. Handle snails gently and by the shell, never by pulling the body. Wash your hands before and after handling them. If a shell cracks slightly, keep the snail in a separate, quiet pen with plenty of calcium and moisture — small cracks can heal, but a badly damaged snail rarely recovers. Avoid overcrowding, keep the soil moist but not waterlogged, and change the top soil every few months. Clean routines prevent most problems a beginner will face.

## 9. Harvest, Price and Sell Your Snails

Harvest snails when they reach a good market size — usually when the shell is hard, thick at the edge, and the snail feels heavy for its size. Do not harvest all your breeders: keep your best, largest, healthiest snails for breeding, and sell the rest.

Snails are sold by size and by number, and sometimes by weight, depending on the market. Large snails fetch a higher price per piece than small ones, and prices rise around festive periods, weddings and December, when demand for pepper soup and party food is high. Prices also vary by city — a large snail in Lagos may sell for more than the same snail in a rural market — so check prices in your own market, from traders and other farmers, before you fix yours. Sell in the evening or early morning when snails are active and look their best, and keep them in a ventilated basket or container with damp leaves for transport, never in a sealed nylon bag in the sun.

Your buyers will include households, market traders, restaurants, hotels, caterers and other snail farmers who want breeding stock. Start by telling people around you: neighbours, church members, colleagues and WhatsApp contacts. Take clear photos of your snails and pens, and post them with your location and phone number. Supply a restaurant or caterer regularly, even in small quantities, and you will have a steadier income than selling one large batch to a stranger. Agree payment before delivery, and be wary of buyers who promise to pay "after the party" — a deposit, or payment on delivery, is safer.

## 10. Manage Money and Grow Slowly

Keep records from the first day. Write down every naira spent on pens, stock, feed, transport and water, and every naira earned from sales. At the end of each month, add them up. Snail farming has low running costs, but it also has slow returns, so knowing your real costs stops you from thinking you are making a profit when you are only recovering what you spent.

Reinvest your first profits into more pens and more breeders, not into a new phone or a bigger house. Growth in snail farming is compounding: more breeders mean more eggs, more eggs mean more snails to sell, and more sales mean more breeders. That cycle only works if you keep your breeders, keep your pens clean, and keep feeding consistently.

Avoid the common mistakes. Do not buy snails before the pen is ready. Do not overcrowd a pen to save space. Do not feed snails chemically sprayed leaves. Do not expect quick money — a farmer who gives up after three months because the snails are still growing has lost the investment, while the farmer who waits, feeds and protects them will harvest. And do not sell your best breeders for a quick gain.

Snail farming in Nigeria is a patient business, but it is a real one. Start with healthy stock, build a shaded, secure pen, feed fresh leaves and calcium, protect the snails from predators, and sell to people you can reach again. Do that consistently for a year, and a small backyard farm can become a steady source of income — quiet, low-cost, and built on work you can do yourself.
