A generator breaks down in the middle of a busy week. A supplier increases prices overnight. A loyal customer delays payment for a month. Your shop rent is due, and sales have been slow for two weeks. For a small business in Nigeria, any one of these events can be stressful. Two at once can be enough to close the business.

An emergency fund is money you set aside for those moments. It is not profit to spend, and it is not money for a new phone or a holiday. It is a cushion that lets your business survive a bad month, pay for an urgent repair, or cover rent while you wait for a late payment. Many Nigerian small businesses do not have one, not because they do not want it, but because they think they need a large income to start. You do not. You can build an emergency fund on a small income, one small transfer at a time.

This guide shows you how. It covers how much to save, where to keep the money, how to build the habit when income is irregular, what counts as a real emergency, and how to rebuild the fund after you use it.

## What a Business Emergency Fund Really Is

A business emergency fund is separate savings for your business, kept apart from your daily operating money and your personal money. It is for unexpected costs that you cannot plan for in a normal month. It is not for restocking, which is a regular cost. It is not for advertising, which is a planned cost. It is for the costs that arrive without warning.

Examples of real business emergencies in Nigeria include a fridge or generator breaking down, a phone or laptop used for business stopping work, a sudden rent increase or a landlord asking for payment earlier than expected, a supplier failing and you needing to buy stock urgently from a more expensive source, a courier losing a parcel and you needing to refund or replace it, or a slow month where sales do not cover rent and salaries.

A fund is not a loan. You do not pay interest on it, and no one can refuse to give it to you when you need it. That is why it is so powerful for small businesses that cannot easily borrow from a bank. It gives you time to solve a problem without panic.

Your fund also protects your personal life. Without it, a business emergency often becomes a personal emergency. You borrow from your house rent, your children's school fees, or your food money to save the business. With a fund, the business pays for its own problems, and your personal money stays safe.

## How Much Should You Save?

The common advice is to save three to six months of business expenses. For a small business in Lagos or Abuja, that can sound impossible. If your monthly expenses are ₦300,000, three months is ₦900,000. That figure can feel so large that you never start.

Start smaller. Your first goal is one month of essential expenses. Essential expenses are the costs you must pay even if you make no sales: rent, electricity, internet, transport to the market, and any staff salary you cannot delay. Add them up for one month. That is your first target.

Your second goal is two months. Your third goal is three months. You do not need to reach the third goal this year. You need to reach the first goal, and then keep going. A fund of ₦150,000 is better than a fund of zero. It can pay for a generator repair, a replacement phone, or a week of slow sales.

To find your monthly essential expenses, look at your last three months of spending. Write down rent divided by twelve if you pay yearly, or the monthly amount if you pay monthly. Add electricity, fuel for the generator, internet data, transport, packaging, and staff costs. Leave out restock costs for now, because you can pause restocking for a short time in an emergency, but you cannot pause rent. The total is your one-month target. If you do not know your expenses, track them for one month in a notebook or Google Sheet. You will be surprised where the money goes.

## Step 1: Open a Separate Account and Name It Clearly

The first step is to separate the fund from your spending money. If the emergency money sits in the same account you use for daily transfers, you will spend it. It is human nature. You will see a balance of ₦200,000 and think you can afford something, forgetting that half of it is your safety net.

Open a separate savings account or wallet for the fund. It can be a different bank account, a Moniepoint or Opay savings wallet, a Kuda or PiggyVest savings pocket, or a cooperative savings account. Choose an account that is safe, but not too easy to withdraw from on impulse. If you can move the money to your spending account in two taps, you will do it when you are tempted. If it takes a day or a deliberate step, you will think twice.

Name the account clearly: "Business Emergency Fund." Do not name it "Savings" or "My Money," because that is vague. A clear name reminds you of its purpose every time you see it. Tell your staff or your partner that this account is not for daily spending. If you have a business partner, agree together that withdrawals need both of you to approve.

Keep the fund in naira if your expenses are in naira, which they are for most small businesses. Do not lock the fund in an investment you cannot access quickly. An emergency fund must be available within a day or two. A fixed deposit that locks money for six months is not an emergency fund. A savings account that pays small interest and allows quick withdrawal is ideal.

## Step 2: Pay Yourself First, Even If It Is Small

The habit matters more than the amount. If you wait until the end of the month to save what is left, there will usually be nothing left. Instead, save first, as soon as money comes in.

Choose a simple rule you can follow every week. For example: save 5 percent of every sale, or save ₦5,000 every Friday, or save ₦1,000 a day. Pick an amount that is small enough to be realistic, but regular enough to grow. If you earn ₦50,000 in a good week, 5 percent is ₦2,500. If you earn ₦20,000 in a slow week, 5 percent is ₦1,000. The percentage method works well for irregular income because it adjusts to what you actually earned.

Set up an automatic transfer if your bank or app allows it. PiggyVest, Kuda, and some banks let you set a daily or weekly autosave. Automation removes willpower from the process. If you have to remember to transfer manually, you will forget on busy weeks. If it happens automatically, the fund grows without you thinking about it.

Treat the transfer as a business expense, like rent or data. It is not optional. When you receive a payment from a customer, move the savings portion immediately, before you pay for anything else. A vendor in Onitsha who sells ₦100,000 of goods in a week and saves ₦5,000 will have ₦20,000 in a month and ₦240,000 in a year, without feeling a large pinch at any one time.

If your income is very low, start with ₦500 a day. That is ₦3,500 a week and ₦14,000 a month. It is not a large fund, but it is a start, and it builds the habit. After three months, increase it to ₦1,000 a day if you can. Small, steady savings beat large, irregular savings that stop after two weeks.

## Step 3: Separate Business Money From Personal Money

Many Nigerian small businesses fail to save because business and personal money are mixed. The owner uses business sales to pay for food, school fees, and family needs, and then has nothing left for the business. The business looks profitable, but the money is gone.

Open a separate account for your business if you have not already. All business income goes into the business account. All business expenses come out of it. Pay yourself a regular salary or allowance from the business account to your personal account, even if it is small. For example, pay yourself ₦50,000 a month, or ₦15,000 a week, depending on what the business can afford. That is your personal money. The rest stays in the business.

This separation makes saving possible. You can see how much the business actually earned, how much it spent, and how much is left to save. Without it, you are guessing. With it, you can set a savings target and track it.

If you are a sole trader and this feels formal, start simply. Use one Moniepoint account for business and one Opay account for personal, or two bank accounts. Label them clearly. At the end of each week, move your salary to your personal account and your emergency savings to your fund account. Leave the rest for business costs. This simple routine will change how you see your money.

## Step 4: Find Money to Save Without Earning More

You may think you cannot save because your income is too small. In many cases, you can find small amounts by cutting waste, not by earning more. Review your spending for a month and look for leaks.

Common leaks for Nigerian small businesses include buying data in small, expensive bundles instead of a monthly plan, paying for delivery twice because of poor planning, buying packaging in small quantities at a high unit price instead of in bulk, eating out every day instead of cooking, and paying for subscriptions you no longer use. Each leak may be only ₦2,000 or ₦5,000 a month, but together they can be ₦20,000 or more — enough to fund your emergency savings.

Negotiate your costs. Ask your supplier for a better price if you buy in bulk or pay early. Ask your landlord if you can pay rent in two parts instead of one large payment. Compare courier prices for your busiest route. A small saving on each order adds up over a month.

Increase your income slightly to fund the savings. Add a small delivery fee that covers your true cost instead of absorbing it. Offer a complementary product that costs little but adds profit, like a small extra for a food order. Raise your price by a small amount on your bestseller if the market allows it. A ₦500 increase on a product you sell fifty times a month is ₦25,000 extra — more than enough to start a fund.

## Step 5: Decide What Counts as an Emergency — Before You Need It

A fund only works if you do not spend it on non-emergencies. Write down your emergency rules and keep them where you can see them. A real emergency is urgent, unexpected, and necessary for the business to continue. A new phone because you want a better camera is not an emergency. A replacement phone because your business phone stopped working and you cannot take orders is an emergency. A new dress for a party is not an emergency. Rent that is due tomorrow is an emergency if you have no other way to pay it.

Make a list of three to five situations that qualify, and stick to it. For example: equipment breakdown, urgent rent or salary, supplier failure, refund or replacement for a lost parcel, and a slow month where sales do not cover essential costs. Anything else should come from your normal budget, not from the fund.

If you are tempted to use the fund for something else, wait 48 hours. Most non-emergencies feel less urgent after two days. If you still need the money after 48 hours and it fits your rules, use it. If it does not fit, find another way.

Tell someone you trust about your rules. A partner, a friend, or a fellow business owner can help you stay accountable. When you are tempted to spend the fund on a non-emergency, talk to them first. A short conversation can save months of savings.

## Where to Keep the Fund So It Is Safe and Accessible

Your emergency fund needs to be safe from theft, safe from inflation as much as possible, and accessible within a day or two. Do not keep it as cash at home or in your shop. Cash can be stolen, lost, or spent easily. Keep it in a regulated bank or a reputable savings app.

A high-yield savings account or a savings app that pays interest is a good choice. PiggyVest, Kuda, and some banks offer savings pockets with interest that is better than a current account. The interest will not make you rich, but it helps the fund keep up with rising prices a little. Do not chase high returns with your emergency fund. The goal is safety and access, not investment growth. Keep your investments separate.

Avoid keeping the fund in crypto, in a loan to a friend, or in stock that you cannot sell quickly. An emergency fund that is locked or at risk is not a fund. It is a gamble. If you want to invest, do it with money beyond your emergency fund, after the fund is complete.

Check the fund balance once a month. Do not check it daily, or you will be tempted to use it. Once a month, confirm the balance, confirm that your automatic transfers are working, and update your target. If you have reached one month of expenses, celebrate briefly, then set the next target.

## How to Rebuild After You Use the Fund

Using your emergency fund is not failure. It is the fund doing its job. A business that uses its fund to survive a generator breakdown and keeps trading is a business that planned well. The key is to rebuild it as soon as you can.

After you use the fund, pause non-essential spending until it is rebuilt. Reduce your personal salary temporarily if you can. Increase your savings rate for a few weeks. For example, if you normally save 5 percent of sales, save 10 percent until the fund is back to its previous level. Tell your customers and staff nothing about the fund — just focus on selling and saving.

If the emergency was large and the fund is empty, start again with your first goal: one month of essential expenses. You have done it before, and you can do it again, faster this time because you have the habit. Do not borrow to rebuild the fund. Borrowing to save is not saving. Earn it back through sales and disciplined transfers.

Learn from the emergency. If your generator broke down, budget for regular servicing so it lasts longer. If a supplier failed you, build a relationship with a backup supplier. If a slow month drained the fund, plan a promotion or a new product for the next slow season. The fund buys you time, and time lets you fix the cause.

## Final Thoughts: Start Small, Stay Consistent

Building a business emergency fund in Nigeria on a small income is not about having a large salary. It is about having a clear target, a separate account, and a habit of saving a little every time money comes in. Start with one month of essential expenses. Save 5 percent of every sale, or a fixed amount every week. Keep business and personal money separate. Decide in advance what counts as an emergency, and keep the fund safe and accessible.

Open your separate account this week. Set up an automatic transfer, even if it is only ₦500 a day. Write down your one-month target and stick it on your wall or in your phone. In three months, check your balance. You will be surprised how much small, regular savings can grow. In a year, you will have a cushion that lets you sleep better, negotiate better, and survive the bad months that close businesses without savings.

Your business deserves that protection. Start today, start small, and keep going. The fund will not build itself, but it will build your confidence with every transfer you make.
