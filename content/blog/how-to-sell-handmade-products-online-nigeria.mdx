If you can make something with your hands — beaded bags, scented candles, crocheted baby clothes, handmade soap, Ankara accessories, baked treats, or custom gift boxes — you already have a business. The hard part is not making the product. It is finding buyers, convincing them your work is worth the price, and getting the order to them in good condition.

Selling online solves the biggest problem handmade sellers face: foot traffic. A stall in a market in Onitsha or a shop in Yaba only reaches the people who walk past it. An Instagram page, a WhatsApp catalogue, or a listing on Jumia reaches buyers in Lagos, Abuja, Port Harcourt, and even Nigerians abroad who want a piece of home.

But handmade products need more trust than factory-made goods. A buyer cannot touch your candle or try on your crochet top before paying. Your photos, your descriptions, and the way you reply to messages have to do the convincing.

## Why Handmade Products Sell Well Online

Buyers are tired of mass-produced items that everyone else owns. A handmade product feels personal. A beaded bag made by one person in Ibadan is not the same as a bag from a factory, and many buyers will pay more for that difference. They buy the story as much as the product: the maker, the materials, and the care that went into it.

Handmade products also suit online selling because they photograph beautifully. A well-lit photo of a handmade soap with dried flowers, or a short video of your hands weaving a bag, stops people from scrolling.

The market is wide. Nigerians buy handmade gifts for birthdays, weddings, baby showers, and Christmas hampers. Parents buy crocheted baby sets. Brides buy beaded accessories. Offices buy custom gift boxes for staff. And Nigerians in the UK, Canada, and the US often order handmade Ankara pieces and skincare as gifts or for themselves, because they cannot find the same thing where they live.

## Choose What to Make and Who to Sell To

Do not try to make everything. A seller who makes soap, candles, bags, and cakes all at once confuses buyers and stretches herself thin. Pick one product line, or two that fit together, and become known for it. "The candle woman in Lekki" is easier to remember and recommend than "the woman who sells many things".

Start with what you already make well. If your crochet baby hats get compliments every time, start there. If your shea butter mix clears skin, start there. Skill you already have beats a new skill you must learn from scratch, because your first customers will judge your quality harshly and your early reviews will follow you.

Check demand before you invest heavily in materials. Search Instagram and TikTok for your product and see what sellers are posting, what prices they charge, and what buyers ask in the comments. Visit a local market and ask traders what sells fastest. If ten sellers already make the same beaded earrings, you need a clear difference: a better finish, a lower price, faster delivery, or a design nobody else has.

Decide who your buyer is. A ₦3,000 handmade soap sells to a different person than a ₦45,000 custom gift box. Write down your buyer in one sentence: "Working women in Lagos aged 25 to 40 who want natural skincare". Every decision after that — your prices, your photos, your captions — should speak to that person.

## Price Your Handmade Products for Profit

Pricing is where most handmade sellers lose money. They charge what feels fair, or what a neighbour charges, without adding up their real costs. Then they work long hours and wonder why the business never grows.

Start with your costs. Add up the materials for one item: the wax, the wick, and the jar for a candle; the yarn and buttons for a baby set; the fabric, beads, and lining for a bag. Then add your packaging: the box, the ribbon, the thank-you card. Then add delivery to the customer, if you offer free delivery. That total is your cost price. Never sell below it.

Next, add your time. If a crochet top takes you six hours and you value your time at ₦1,500 an hour, that is ₦9,000 of labour. Many sellers skip this and effectively work for free. Your time is a real cost, and your price must cover it, or you will burn out and quit.

Then add your profit. A common approach is to set your price at two to three times your material cost, then check that it also covers your time. If your materials cost ₦4,000 and you price at ₦12,000, you have room for your time, your packaging, and a profit. If the price looks too high for your buyers, reduce your material costs or speed up your process instead of cutting your profit to nothing.

Study what others charge, but do not copy the cheapest seller. That seller may use cheaper materials, or may not be counting her time. Instead, compare your quality and your finish. If your work is neater and your packaging is better, you can charge more, and you should say why in your description: hand-poured, small batch, made to order, or premium materials.

Offer clear sizes and options. A candle in small, medium, and large at ₦5,000, ₦8,000, and ₦12,000 gives buyers a choice and lets you serve both small budgets and premium gift buyers.

## Set Up Your Online Shop

You do not need a website on day one. Most handmade sellers in Nigeria start on WhatsApp Business and Instagram, and that is enough to take real orders. What matters is that your shop looks organised and trustworthy wherever it lives.

Start with WhatsApp Business. Set up a business profile with your business name, a clear photo, your location, and your opening hours. Build a catalogue with your products, each with a photo, a price, and a short description. When a buyer asks "how much?", you can send the catalogue link instead of typing prices one by one. Turn on quick replies for common questions like delivery fees and payment details, so you answer fast even when you are busy making orders.

Add Instagram next. Use one account for your business, with a clear bio that says what you make, where you deliver, and how to order. Post your products regularly, show your hands at work, and share customer reviews. Your Instagram is your shop window. A page with three posts from six months ago tells buyers you may have closed down.

Keep your prices and product names the same everywhere. If your candle is ₦8,000 on WhatsApp and ₦10,000 on Instagram, buyers will notice and trust you less. Keep one simple price list, update it everywhere when it changes, and make sure your phone number is the same on every platform.

## Photograph and Describe Your Work Properly

Your photo is your product on the internet. A buyer who cannot see the quality will not pay your price, no matter how good your work is in real life. You do not need a professional camera. Your phone, daylight, and a clean background are enough.

Photograph near a window in daylight, not under a yellow bulb at night. Put your product on a plain background: a white sheet, a wooden table, or a clean wall. Take at least four photos of each product: the front, the back or side, a close-up of the detail, and one showing the size next to something familiar, like your hand or a phone. If you sell food or skincare, show the real product in its real pack, not a picture from the internet.

Show your product in use. A candle looks better lit in a cosy corner than sitting alone on a table. A crochet baby set looks better on a baby doll or a real baby than folded in a bag. A bag looks better carried on a shoulder than lying flat. These lifestyle photos help buyers imagine owning the product, and that imagination is what makes them buy.

Film short videos of your process. A thirty-second clip of you pouring a candle, stitching a bag, or packing an order builds trust faster than any caption, because it proves the product is genuinely handmade.

Write honest descriptions. State the materials, the size, the colours available, how long it takes to make, and how to care for the product. If an item is made to order and takes five days, say so before the buyer pays. If a soap contains shea butter and fragrance, list both. Honest descriptions reduce complaints and returns, and they attract buyers who value quality.

## Delivery, Payments, and Packaging

Delivery is where many handmade sellers lose customers. A beautiful product that arrives late, crushed, or smelling of petrol will not bring a repeat order. Plan delivery as carefully as you plan your product.

For Lagos orders, dispatch riders are the usual option. Build a relationship with one or two reliable riders, agree on their rates for different areas, and keep their numbers saved. A rider who knows your shop and your packaging will treat your orders better than a stranger you call for the first time. Always confirm the delivery fee with the buyer before you send the order, and share the rider's number and expected arrival time as soon as the parcel leaves.

For orders outside Lagos, use a logistics company with tracking, such as a courier that serves Abuja, Port Harcourt, Ibadan, and Enugu. Pack fragile items with bubble wrap or soft tissue, seal the box well, and write the buyer's name and phone number clearly on the outside. If the product is a candle or a food item, protect it from heat during transport. Tell the buyer how many days delivery will take before they pay, so there are no surprises.

Take payments before you start making a custom order. For made-to-order products, ask for at least half the payment upfront, and the balance before delivery. This protects you from buyers who order, make you spend on materials and hours of work, and then disappear. For ready-made stock, take full payment before dispatch. Use Paystack or Flutterwave payment links so buyers can pay by card or transfer easily, and always confirm payment before you pack the order.

Package like the product matters, because the unboxing is part of the experience. A clean box, tissue paper, a ribbon, and a short handwritten thank-you note cost very little and make buyers feel special.

Keep a simple record of every order: the buyer's name, what they ordered, the amount paid, the delivery address, and the date you sent it.

## Grow Your Handmade Business with Social Media

Social media is your shop, your advert, and your word of mouth all at once. The sellers who grow fastest are not always the ones with the best products. They are the ones who show up consistently and reply quickly.

Post regularly, even when you have no new product. Show your work in progress, answer a common question, share a customer review, or show how you pack an order. Three to five posts a week is a realistic target for a busy maker. Use your phone camera and natural light, and keep your captions short and friendly, with the price and how to order clearly stated.

Use customer photos and reviews. When a buyer sends you a photo of your product in their home, ask permission to share it, then post it with a thank-you. Real customer photos convince new buyers far more than your own photos, because they show the product in a real home with a real person.

Run small, honest promotions. A Christmas gift set, a Valentine's bundle, or a "buy two, save ₦1,000" offer gives people a reason to buy now instead of later. Announce promotions a few days early so your followers have time to plan, and set a clear end date so the offer feels real.

Answer messages fast. A buyer who messages you at 9am and gets a reply at 9pm has usually bought from someone else by then. Set up quick replies on WhatsApp Business for your prices, delivery fees, and delivery times. If you are busy making orders, say so honestly and give a time you will reply, then keep that promise.

## Common Mistakes

The first mistake is underpricing your work. Many makers charge only for materials and forget their time, their packaging, and their delivery costs. The result is a busy business that makes no money. Add up every cost, including your hours, before you set a price.

The second mistake is taking orders you cannot finish on time. If you can make five bags a week, do not accept twenty orders in one week. Late delivery damages your reputation far more than a polite "I am fully booked this week, the next slot is Monday" ever will. Learn to say no, or to give a realistic delivery date and keep it.

The third mistake is poor packaging. A handmade soap wrapped in a thin nylon bag looks cheap, even if the soap itself is excellent. Spend a little on boxes, tissue, and a thank-you card. Packaging is part of the product, and buyers judge it.

The fourth mistake is using another seller's photos. Buyers will notice when the product that arrives looks different from the photo, and the complaint that follows will cost you more than the sale was worth. Always photograph your own work, even if your photos are simple.

The fifth mistake is ignoring power and time planning. Handmade work needs light, and in Nigeria, NEPA supply is not reliable. If you sew, pour candles, or bake, plan your work around power availability, keep a rechargeable lamp and a power bank charged, and finish orders before the delivery date so a blackout does not make you late.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need a CAC-registered business to sell handmade products online?

No, you can start as an individual seller with a WhatsApp Business account and an Instagram page. Registering your business with the CAC helps with trust, with opening a business bank account, and with supplying shops or companies later, but it is not required to take your first online order.

### How much money do I need to start selling handmade products online?

You can start with the cost of materials for ten pieces of your product, plus packaging and a small amount for delivery. For many makers, that is between ₦20,000 and ₦50,000. Start small, sell those first pieces, and use the profit to buy more materials.

### Which platform is best for selling handmade products in Nigeria?

WhatsApp Business and Instagram are the best starting points for most makers, because that is where Nigerian buyers already shop and chat. Add Jumia or Konga later if you want to reach buyers who search for products, and consider Etsy if you want to sell to buyers abroad.

### How do I handle a customer who says the product is not what they expected?

Reply the same day, apologise, and ask what is wrong. If the product is genuinely faulty, offer a replacement or a refund. If the customer simply changed their mind, explain your policy politely. A calm, fast response protects your reputation and often turns a complaint into a repeat order.

### Can I sell handmade products to buyers outside Nigeria?

Yes. Many makers ship to Nigerians abroad through logistics companies or through friends and family travelling. For payments, use a Paystack or Flutterwave link, which accepts card payments. Always confirm the delivery cost and time with the buyer before they pay, because international delivery is slower and more expensive.

## What to Do Next

1. Choose one product line to focus on and write down who your buyer is in one sentence.
2. Make ten pieces, calculate your full cost including materials, packaging, and your time, and set a price that leaves a profit.
3. Set up WhatsApp Business with a catalogue, prices, and quick replies, and create or tidy up your Instagram business page.
4. Photograph each product in daylight from four angles, plus one photo or video showing it in use.
5. List your products with honest descriptions that state materials, sizes, colours, and how long a made-to-order item takes.
6. Take payment before you start a custom order, pack it well, and confirm the delivery fee and rider with the buyer before dispatch.
7. Post three to five times a week, share customer photos with permission, and reply to every message as fast as you can.
8. Keep a simple record of every order and every buyer, and message past buyers first whenever new stock arrives.
