Walk into a busy shop in Lekki, Ikeja, or Onitsha today and watch how customers pay. Many still carry cash, but more and more hold out a card or a phone and ask, "Do you have POS?" If your answer is no, a good number of them will walk to the next shop that does. Nigerians now expect to pay by card or transfer, and a shop without a way to accept electronic payment is quietly losing sales every single day.

A POS terminal — short for point of sale terminal — is the small machine that lets you accept card payments in your shop. The customer inserts or taps their ATM card, enters their PIN, and the money moves from their bank account into yours. This guide walks you through everything: what a POS terminal is, where to get one in Nigeria, what it costs, how to set it up, and how to use it every day without stress.

## What Is a POS Terminal and How Does It Work?

A POS terminal is a handheld electronic device that reads a customer's debit or credit card and processes the payment. When a customer pays, the terminal connects through a mobile network or Wi-Fi to your bank or payment provider, checks that the customer has enough money, deducts the amount, and prints or displays a receipt. The money usually lands in your account within minutes or by the next working day, depending on your provider.

In Nigeria, most POS terminals work on the Interswitch or NIBSS network, which connects the banks. When your customer uses a Verve, Mastercard, or Visa card, the terminal talks to their bank through this network, and the payment is confirmed almost instantly when the network is healthy. The terminal needs a SIM card and mobile data to connect, which is why network quality matters so much — a terminal with a weak MTN or Airtel signal in your shop will frustrate customers with slow or failed transactions.

## Why Your Shop Needs a POS Terminal

The first reason is simple: customers carry less cash than they used to. After the cash scarcity of early 2023, millions of Nigerians changed their habits permanently. Many now move around with little or no cash, paying for everything by card or transfer. A shop that only accepts cash is asking those customers to go and find an ATM or a POS agent first, and many will not bother. They will buy from your competitor instead.

Finally, a POS terminal protects you from fake cash. Counterfeit naira notes circulate in Nigerian markets, and a busy shop attendant can easily accept a fake ₦1,000 note in a rush. Card payments eliminate that risk entirely. You also reduce the risk of theft, because less cash sits in your drawer overnight.

## Types of POS Terminals Available in Nigeria

Not all POS terminals are the same, and the right one depends on your type of business and how much you sell each day.

The traditional bank POS is issued by a commercial bank such as GTBank, Access Bank, First Bank, Zenith Bank, or UBA. These are the classic machines you see in supermarkets and pharmacies. They are sturdy, reliable, and directly linked to your bank account with that bank. The downside is that getting one from a bank can be slow — you usually need an existing account, and the application can take days or weeks. Banks also sometimes charge a monthly rental fee or require a minimum monthly transaction volume.

The fintech POS is issued by companies such as Moniepoint, Opay, Palmpay, Kuda, and FairMoney. These have become extremely popular with small shops, market traders, and roadside businesses across Nigeria. They are usually much faster to get — sometimes within 24 to 48 hours — and the application is simpler. Many of them come with a dedicated account number, so customers can also pay you by transfer into that account. Fintech terminals often have competitive charges and no monthly rental fee, which is why small businesses love them.

The Android smart POS is a newer type that runs on Android, with a touchscreen and sometimes a built-in camera for scanning QR codes. They can do more, but they cost more and their batteries drain faster. For most small shops, a standard terminal is enough.

For a typical shop — a provision store, a boutique, a pharmacy, a restaurant, or a phone accessories shop — a fintech terminal from Moniepoint or Opay is usually the fastest and simplest starting point. If you already have a strong relationship with your bank and you process large volumes, a bank terminal is also a solid choice. Some businesses keep both: a bank terminal as the main machine and a fintech terminal as a backup for days when one network is down.

## Where to Get a POS Terminal in Nigeria

You have two main routes: your bank, or a fintech agent or office.

To get one from your bank, visit your branch and ask for a POS terminal application. You will usually need an account with the bank, a valid ID, your BVN, and your business details. If approved, the bank will deliver and configure the terminal at your shop, usually within a week or two. Some banks charge a refundable caution fee or a monthly rental of around ₦2,000 to ₦5,000.

To get one from a fintech company, the process is usually faster and more flexible. For Moniepoint, you can apply through the Moniepoint app or visit a Moniepoint agent or office in your area. You will need your BVN, a valid ID, and a phone number. The terminal itself is usually bought outright, with prices that have commonly ranged from about ₦30,000 to ₦65,000 depending on the model and current promotions. Opay works in a similar way: apply through the Opay app or an agent, provide your BVN and ID, and pay for the terminal. Palmpay also offers terminals through its agent network.

Be careful about who you buy from. Only get a terminal from an official agent, an official office, or the provider's own app. Never buy a POS terminal from a random person on the street or on social media. Ask for a receipt, and make sure the terminal is registered in your own name with your own BVN, not someone else's.

## What Does a POS Terminal Cost?

The cost of a POS terminal has two parts: the cost of getting the machine, and the charges on each transaction.

The machine itself costs money if you buy it from a fintech provider. Prices change with promotions and the naira exchange rate, but you should budget roughly ₦30,000 to ₦65,000 for a standard terminal, and more for an Android smart terminal. Bank terminals are often given free or for a refundable caution deposit, but the bank may charge a monthly rental fee instead.

The transaction charges are where the ongoing cost sits. In Nigeria, the standard charge for POS transactions is typically 0.5% of the transaction amount, capped at ₦1,000 per transaction. That means a ₦10,000 sale costs you ₦50, a ₦50,000 sale costs you ₦250, and a ₦500,000 sale costs you ₦1,000, not ₦2,500, because of the cap. Some providers charge slightly different rates, so always confirm the exact rate before you accept the machine. Ask the agent to show you the charge in writing.

On top of that, there is usually a small stamp duty charge of ₦50 on transfers and receipts above ₦10,000, which is a government charge that applies across Nigerian banking. Your customers do not pay the POS charge — you do, as the merchant. Some shop owners add the charge to the customer's bill, but the Central Bank of Nigeria has rules against surcharging card payments, and customers dislike it. Most successful shops absorb the charge as a cost of doing business, because the extra sales a POS brings in far outweigh the small percentage.

Other costs to budget for include the paper rolls for printing receipts, which cost around ₦500 to ₦1,500 for a pack depending on size, and the data for the terminal's SIM card. The terminal uses very little data, but make sure you keep the SIM topped up so the machine never goes offline in the middle of a sale.

## How to Set Up Your POS Terminal: Step by Step

Once your terminal arrives, setting it up takes less than an hour. Here is the process:

1. Unbox the terminal and check that everything is there: the machine itself, a charger, a battery, a SIM card or a slot for one, and at least one paper roll.
2. Charge the battery fully before first use. A full charge usually takes two to three hours.
3. Insert the SIM card that came with the terminal, or your own data SIM. If the provider gave you a SIM, use that one, because it is already configured for their network. Make sure the SIM has data and is active.
4. Switch the terminal on and wait for it to connect to the network. You should see signal bars and the provider's name on the screen. If it says "no network" or "no service", try moving near a window or outside briefly — some shops with thick walls or metal roofs block the signal.
5. The agent or bank officer who delivers the terminal will usually log in for you with a merchant ID and a default password or PIN. Watch them do it, and then change the default password to one only you know.
6. Run a test transaction. Ask a friend or family member to pay ₦100 with their card, then check that the money arrives in your account and that the receipt prints. If the test fails, call the provider's support line immediately, while the agent is still there if possible.
7. Set up your business name on the receipt. Most terminals let you set a merchant name that appears on the printed receipt and on the customer's bank alert. Make sure it shows your business name, not the agent's name or a generic name, because customers check this.
8. Train your staff. Show every attendant how to enter an amount, how to ask the customer to insert or tap their card, and what to do if a transaction fails.

## How to Use Your POS Terminal Every Day

A smooth daily routine makes the POS a help rather than a headache. Start each morning by switching the terminal on, checking the battery is charged and the signal is strong, and confirming it is working before your first customer arrives. There is nothing worse than discovering at 10 a.m. that the machine has been offline all morning.

When a customer wants to pay by card, enter the exact amount on the terminal first, and show the customer the screen so they can confirm the amount before they insert their card. Then hand the machine to the customer, or let them tap their card on the reader. Never take a customer's card behind the counter or out of their sight. Let them enter their own PIN, and never look at the keypad while they type it. When the transaction is approved, the terminal will print a receipt. Give one copy to the customer and keep the merchant copy for your records.

At the end of the day, settle or reconcile the terminal. Most machines have a "settlement" or "end of day" function that prints a summary of all the day's transactions. Do this every evening before you close, and compare the settlement total with the money that arrived in your account.

## Handling Failed Transactions and Network Problems

Failed transactions are the most stressful part of running a POS in Nigeria, and they will happen. The key is knowing what to do in the moment, so neither you nor your customer panics.

If the terminal says "transaction failed", "declined", or "network error", first check the receipt. If no receipt printed and the terminal says the transaction failed, the payment did not go through, and the customer can try again. Do not give them the goods until a transaction shows as approved.

If the terminal says the transaction was approved but no receipt printed, or the customer got a debit alert but your terminal shows a failure, the money may be hanging in the middle: deducted from the customer's account but not yet settled into yours. Stay calm. Write down the customer's name, phone number, the amount, and the time. Tell them you will check and call them back. Then check your account balance and your transaction history on the terminal. In most cases, the money arrives within a few hours, or the customer's bank reverses the debit automatically within 24 hours.

Never ask a customer to pay twice for the same item without proof. If their bank debited them and you cannot confirm that your account was credited, do not take a second payment. Instead, record the details and follow up with your provider's support line. The provider can trace the transaction using the reference number on the receipt or in the terminal's history.

If a terminal fails repeatedly, call the provider's support line and ask for a replacement or a swap. Do not keep struggling with a faulty machine for weeks. And always keep a backup way to collect money: a bank transfer to your account number, or a second terminal.

## Security: Protecting Your Money and Your Customers

A POS terminal handles other people's money, so security matters. The most important rule is simple: your merchant PIN and your login password are yours alone. Never share them with an agent, a staff member you do not trust, or anyone who calls you claiming to be from the bank or the provider. Real providers will never call and ask for your PIN or password. If someone calls asking for it, hang up. It is a scam.

Reconcile your account every day. Compare the terminal's settlement report with the money in your bank account. If money is missing, report it to your provider the same day.

Keep your terminal in a safe place at night. Do not leave it on the counter overnight. Put it in a drawer, a safe, or take it home. Terminals get stolen, and a stolen terminal registered in your name can be misused.

Finally, keep your receipts. Store the merchant copies in a box or a file for at least three months. If a customer disputes a payment, or a bank asks for proof, those receipts are your evidence.

## Common Mistakes Shop Owners Make with POS

The first mistake is buying a terminal from an unverified source. A cheap terminal from a stranger on WhatsApp may be cloned, registered to someone else, or locked to a provider that will not support you. Your money could go to the wrong account. Always buy from an official channel.

The third mistake is giving the goods before confirming payment. In a rush, an attendant may hand over a product when the terminal is still processing, and then the transaction fails. Train your staff: goods leave the counter only after the terminal shows "approved" and the receipt prints.

The fifth mistake is ignoring the settlement report. Some owners never settle the terminal or check the daily totals, and only discover missing money weeks later. Reconcile every evening. It takes five minutes.

## Your Checklist: Getting Your POS Running This Week

Day one: decide between a bank terminal and a fintech terminal. If you want speed and simplicity, visit a Moniepoint or Opay agent in your area and ask about their current price and charges. If you prefer your bank, visit your branch and ask for the application form.

Day two: prepare your documents — your BVN, a valid ID, and your business details.

Day three: pay for the terminal or submit the bank application, and confirm the transaction charges in writing. Ask for a receipt and the support phone number.

Day four: when the terminal arrives, charge it, insert the SIM, set your password, set your business name, and run a test transaction of ₦100.

Day five: train your staff, print your account number for transfers as a backup, buy spare paper rolls, and start using the machine with real customers.

Within a week, you will wonder how you ever ran a shop without one. Your customers will pay faster, your records will be cleaner, and you will stop losing sales to the customer who only has a card. A POS terminal does not fix a bad product or a rude attendant, but for a good shop, it removes one of the biggest reasons customers walk away.
