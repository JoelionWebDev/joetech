It is 11am on a Saturday at The Glow Chair, a salon on Admiralty Way in Lekki Phase 1. Amara, the owner, has just finished knotless braids for Ngozi, a first-time customer who found the salon by searching "braids near me" on her phone in the car. Ngozi is turning her head in the mirror, smiling at the edges, taking a photo for her status. Three doors down, another salon has an empty chair and a Google profile with two reviews from 2023. The Glow Chair has 47 reviews, most of them from this year, and Amara replies to every single one.

Ngozi did not choose The Glow Chair because of an advert. She chose it because strangers on Google said the braids last, the wash is gentle, and the owner answers her messages. That is what this article is about: the three things that decide whether reviews work for you once the profile exists — asking at the moment that matters, answering every review like a human being, and knowing what to do when a review is not fair or not real.

## The Moment Matters More Than the Script

Amara used to ask for reviews at the till, while the customer was paying. It barely worked. A woman counting out ₦25,000 for braids is thinking about her money, not about your Google profile. She would say "okay, I will do it" and then forget before she reached the car park. Amara moved the ask to a different moment, and the reviews doubled in six weeks.

The moment is the mirror. When a customer has just seen the finished work and she is smiling at her own reflection — that is the ask. Not a speech. One sentence, while she is still looking at the mirror: "You look good. If you have a minute, a Google review would mean a lot to us — I can send you the link on WhatsApp." Then Amara sends the link immediately, while the customer is still in the chair, still happy, still holding her phone.

There are other good moments, and they all share the same thing: the customer has just felt pleased, and you are catching the feeling before it cools. A bride who messages "I love my hair, thank you" on WhatsApp the morning of her wedding is a perfect moment — reply with thanks and the link in the same message. A regular who tells Blessing, the junior stylist, "this is the best my hair has looked in months" is a perfect moment. A customer who rebooks before she leaves is a perfect moment; she has just told you with her money that she is coming back.

And there are moments to leave alone. Never ask a customer who is unhappy, who has complained about the wait, or who is arguing about the price. Fix the problem first. If you fix it well, the ask comes next time, when she is happy again. Never ask while a customer is on a call, rushing to pick up her children, or standing in the rain outside. A review ask that annoys someone costs you more than the review was worth.

Amara also stopped asking only her favourites. For a while her reviews all sounded the same — the same five women, the same kind of praise — and new customers could tell. Now every happy customer gets asked, first visit or fiftieth.

## Getting Your Review Link and Putting It Where Hands Can Reach It

Every verified Google Business Profile has a direct review link — a web address that takes a customer straight to the box where they write a review, without searching for your business first. Amara found hers in the Business Profile dashboard, under the option to share the review form, and saved it in three places: her notes app, a WhatsApp Business quick reply so any staff member can send it in two taps, and her Instagram bio.

A long, ugly link puts people off, so she shortened it with a free link shortener and printed it as a QR code as well. The QR code sits on the mirror at each station — not on the wall by the door, where nobody looks, but on the mirror the customer is already staring into. It is also on the till, and on the small card Blessing hands over with every take-home hair treatment. A customer who has just admired her braids can point her phone at the mirror and be writing before Amara has swept the floor.

Before she shared it anywhere, Amara tested the link herself on two phones — an Android and an iPhone — with a friend's Google account. She counted the taps. Three taps to the review box, and the right business name at the top. She also checked that it did not open a general Google search, which is what happens when a link is copied wrong. A broken review link is a silent loss. You never hear about the reviews you did not get.

One warning from Amara's first month: never pay for reviews. Google removes paid and fake reviews, and a customer who spots a row of identical five-star reviews written in the same voice will trust you less, not more. The link is free. The ask is free.

## Replying to Good Reviews: Sound Like You Were There

Amara replies to every good review within a day, and she never copies the same reply twice. The formula is simple: thank them by name, mention one specific thing from their review, and invite them back. It takes thirty seconds, and it does more for the business than the review itself, because the reply is what the next stranger reads.

Here is a real one. A customer named Chidinma wrote: "Best braids I have had in Lekki. My scalp did not hurt at all and the braids still look fresh after three weeks." Amara replied: "Thank you, Chidinma. I am glad your scalp felt comfortable — we take our time with the parts so it does not pull. Come back when you are ready for a touch-up, and we will look after you again."

Compare that with "Thanks for your review." The first reply tells the next woman reading that the salon is gentle, careful, and remembers its customers. The second tells her nothing, and suggests the owner did not read what was written.

Two more examples from The Glow Chair's profile. To a short review that only said "Good service, neat work": "Thank you, Adaeze. Neat work is what we aim for every time — see you at your next appointment." Short review, short reply, but still personal. The rule underneath is the same: write back like the customer is standing in front of you, because in a way she is. The next stranger reading your replies is deciding whether she wants to stand in your shop too.

## Replying to Bad Reviews: Slow Down, Then Answer

A one-star review at The Glow Chair arrived on a Tuesday evening. A customer named Tunde — yes, a man, buying a wig for his wife — wrote that he waited an hour past his appointment time and that nobody explained the delay. Amara's first instinct was to defend herself in public. The salon had been short-staffed that day; a stylist called in sick. She typed a reply, deleted it, and waited until morning.

Her reply the next day read: "Thank you for telling us, Tunde. You are right — waiting an hour without an explanation is not good enough, and I am sorry. We were short-staffed that day, but that is our problem, not yours. Please send us a WhatsApp message so we can make it right, and your wife's next appointment is on us."

That reply did three things. It thanked him instead of arguing. It apologised without making excuses the customer had to wade through. And it moved the fix to WhatsApp, where it belongs, instead of negotiating in public. Two weeks later the wife came back, and the appointment went smoothly. The one-star review is still there. So is Amara's reply, and every woman who reads both comes away thinking the salon handles problems properly.

Amara's rules for a bad review are taped inside her office cupboard: never reply angry, never reply the same night if you can help it, never argue about who was right in public, never share private details, and always offer a concrete next step — a message, a call, a free fix.

## The Fake Review: What to Do When It Is Not Real

In September, a one-star review appeared from a name Amara did not recognise, claiming the salon "spoilt my hair and refused to refund me" and mentioning a style The Glow Chair does not even offer. No booking, no payment, no record of the person ever walking in. Amara suspected a competitor — there are four salons on the same stretch of Admiralty Way — but suspicion is not proof, and an accusation in public would have made her look worse than the review.

She did two things. First, she reported it. In the Business Profile dashboard, she found the review, tapped the three dots beside it, chose "Report review", and wrote a short, factual note: this person was never a customer, there is no booking or payment record, and the style mentioned is not a service the salon offers. Google reviewed the report. It took eleven days, and the review was removed.

Second, while she waited, she replied to it anyway. Short and factual: "We have no record of a booking or visit under this name, and the style mentioned is not one we offer. We would like to understand what happened — please contact us on WhatsApp so we can look into it." That reply did its job. Anyone reading it could see the review might not be genuine, without Amara sounding defensive or angry.

Not every report succeeds. Google keeps many reported reviews up, especially when it cannot be sure. If yours stays up, do not write a second, angrier reply, and do not ask friends to flood the profile with five-star reviews to bury it. One strange review among many good ones does very little damage.

## Keeping It Going, Week After Week

The Glow Chair does not run campaigns for reviews. It runs a routine. Blessing and the other stylist know the mirror line by heart. Amara checks the profile every Sunday night and replies to anything new. She aims for about five new reviews a week — not a hard target, just a number that tells her whether the asking has gone quiet.

She also shares the best reviews instead of letting them sit on Google. A screenshot of a kind review goes on her WhatsApp status on a quiet Tuesday, with a simple thank-you. A real review, shared honestly, is the strongest advert a Lekki salon can run — because it comes from a customer, not from the owner.

Start where Amara started. Find your link, test it on two phones, put the QR code on the mirror your customers already look into, and ask at the moment they smile. Reply to every review within a day, good or bad, like the person is standing in front of you. And when a review is not real, report it, answer it calmly, and let your honest ones do the talking.
