Every Nigerian small business owner knows this feeling. You supplied goods, you delivered the service, the customer smiled and said "I will pay you next week" — and next week became next month, then three months, then silence. Your money is sitting in somebody else's pocket while you struggle to pay your own suppliers, your rent, and your staff.

Unpaid customer debt is one of the biggest silent killers of small businesses in Nigeria. It is rarely one huge default. Instead, it is twenty customers owing ₦20,000 each, ten customers owing ₦50,000 each, and a few owing ₦200,000 or more. Added together, that is money that should be in your bank account funding your next order, your next advert, or your next hire.

The good news is that collecting money does not have to mean shouting, threatening, or losing a customer forever. Most customers who owe you are not criminals. They are busy, they forgot, they are facing their own cash flow problem, or your own system made it too easy for them to delay. A calm, structured approach collects far more money than aggression ever will — and it keeps the relationship alive so the customer can buy from you again.

This guide gives you that system: how to prevent debt before it happens, how to follow up in stages, what to say at each stage, how to offer payment plans that actually work, and when to escalate or walk away.

## Why Customers Owe You in the First Place

Before you fix collections, understand why the debt exists. The cause decides the cure.

**There was no clear agreement.** Many Nigerian businesses sell on trust and a verbal promise. "Pay me by Friday" is not an agreement. Which Friday? How much exactly? By bank transfer or cash? Without those details written down, the customer genuinely may not feel a deadline has passed.

**You made paying difficult.** If the only way to pay you is to come to your shop in Lekki with cash, or to ask you for your account number again, many customers will postpone. Every extra step is a reason to delay.

**The customer has a genuine cash problem.** Salaries are delayed, a business deal fell through, school fees came due. These customers often intend to pay but cannot pay everything at once. Treating them like fraudsters pushes them away; a plan brings the money in.

**The customer is testing you.** Some people pay late because nobody has ever chased them firmly. A first reminder the day after the due date teaches them your terms are firm.

**There is a dispute you do not know about.** Perhaps the goods arrived late, the quantity was short, or the service did not meet expectations. The customer stays silent instead of complaining, and silence looks like refusal to pay. Always ask whether they are satisfied before you assume bad faith.

**The sale was never supposed to be on credit.** This is the most common cause. You gave credit because you wanted the sale, because the customer is a friend, a church member, a family member, or a regular. Credit given to protect a relationship often ends up damaging that same relationship.

## Step 1: Stop the Bleeding — Set Clear Payment Terms

You cannot collect effectively if your terms were never clear. From today, every sale should have a payment term, even a one-line term, stated before money or goods change hands.

For most small businesses in Nigeria, these terms work well:

**Payment on delivery or before delivery.** This is the safest option for new customers, online orders, and one-off jobs. For a food vendor in Surulere, a fashion seller on Instagram, or a printer in Ikeja, payment before dispatch removes the entire collection problem. Customers in Nigeria are now used to paying before delivery for online orders, so do not apologise for it.

**50 percent upfront, 50 percent on delivery.** This is the standard for services and custom work: websites, branding, tailoring, catering, event planning, and consulting. The upfront payment covers your materials and commits the customer. The balance is due on delivery, not "after the event" or "when I have money." State the balance due date in writing before you start the work.

**Net 7 or Net 14 for trusted business customers.** If you supply offices, schools, hotels, or corporate clients in Lagos or Abuja, they may genuinely need a few days to process payment. Agree a specific number of days — seven or fourteen — and put the due date on the invoice itself: "Due: 2 December 2026." Never write "pay soon" or leave the date blank.

**No credit for new customers.** Until a customer has paid on time at least three times, they pay upfront. This is not rude; it is business policy. Banks do not lend to strangers, and neither should you.

Put your terms in writing on your invoice, in your WhatsApp Business catalogue, and on your price list: "Payment is due within 7 days of delivery."

Also decide your credit limit per customer. A customer who has always paid ₦50,000 on time can be trusted with ₦100,000 of credit. A customer who has never paid on time should have a credit limit of zero until their behaviour changes. Credit is earned, not assumed.


## Step 2: Build a Simple Debt Tracker

You cannot collect what you cannot see. Many business owners know they are owed money but cannot say exactly how much, by whom, or since when. That vagueness is why the money stays unpaid.

Create a simple tracker. A notebook, a Google Sheet, or an Excel file all work. For each debt, record the customer name and phone number, invoice number and date, what was supplied, total amount, amount paid, balance outstanding, agreed due date, number of days overdue, date of your last follow-up and what the customer said, and the next follow-up date.

Update it every Friday. In thirty minutes you will see who owes the most, who is longest overdue, and who broke a promise this week.

Sort your debts into three groups: Group A (1 to 14 days overdue) is easiest to collect with a polite reminder; Group B (15 to 60 days) needs phone calls and a payment plan; Group C (over 60 days) needs a final demand, a lawyer's letter, or a write-off decision. Act on Group A first, before it becomes Group B or C.

## Step 3: The Follow-Up Sequence That Works

Most debts are collected by a calm sequence of reminders that get gradually firmer, so the customer is never surprised by your tone.

**Reminder 1: one day before the due date.** This is a friendly heads-up, not a demand. Send it by WhatsApp or SMS: "Good morning, Mrs Adeyemi. This is a friendly reminder that your balance of ₦85,000 for the catering service is due tomorrow, 3 December. You can transfer to our GTBank account, 0123456789. Thank you so much." Most good customers will pay after this message alone. It removes the excuse of forgetting, and it is easy to send.

**Reminder 2: on the due date or the day after.** Short, warm, and clear: "Hello Mr Okafor, your payment of ₦120,000 was due today. Kindly confirm when you will be transferring it so I can update our records. Thank you." If the customer replies with a new date, accept it — once. Write it in your tracker and confirm it back in writing: "Thank you, I have noted 10 December. I will check in with you then."

**Reminder 3: one week overdue — the phone call.** Stop messaging and call. A phone call is harder to ignore than a text, and you will learn more in two minutes of conversation than in ten messages. Be polite, be specific, and listen: "Good afternoon, Mrs Adeyemi, this is Igono from Joetech. I am calling about the balance of ₦85,000, which was due on 3 December. I wanted to check if everything is fine with the service, and when you will be able to pay." Then listen. If there is a complaint, solve it. If there is a cash problem, move to the payment plan in Step 4. If the customer promises a date, confirm it back to them in writing immediately after the call.

**Reminder 4: two to three weeks overdue — the firm written message.** Now the tone changes. Still respectful, but no longer casual. State the amount, the original due date, the promises made, and what happens next: "Dear Mr Okafor, your balance of ₦120,000 for the website project delivered on 18 November remains unpaid. It was due on 25 November, and payment was promised by 5 December. Kindly pay the full amount by 12 December to avoid further action on this account, including suspension of further services." Send this by WhatsApp and by email if you have one. Keep a copy — it creates a written record that you chased the debt properly.

**Reminder 5: 30 days overdue — the final demand.** This is your last message before escalation. Be brief and formal: "Dear Mrs Adeyemi, this is a final demand for the outstanding balance of ₦85,000, now 32 days overdue. Please pay within 7 days, failing which we will hand this matter to our lawyer for recovery. We value your patronage and would prefer to resolve this directly." If the customer pays, welcome them back warmly and restore normal service. If they do not, you must follow through — an empty threat destroys your credibility for every future collection.

## Step 4: Offer a Payment Plan — And Make It Stick

When a customer genuinely cannot pay in full at once, a payment plan is your best friend — half the money now beats all the money never — but only if it is specific, written down, and enforced.

Get a part payment immediately. Before you agree any plan, ask for something today — even ₦10,000 or ₦20,000. A customer who pays something today is serious. A customer who promises to start paying next month often is not.

Split the balance into two or three payments, not ten. A ₦150,000 debt becomes ₦50,000 a week for three weeks, or ₦75,000 in two weeks. Short plans get completed; long plans get forgotten. Avoid plans longer than six weeks unless the amount is very large.

Put the dates in writing. Send a WhatsApp message listing each payment and its date, and ask the customer to confirm with a simple "Agreed." For example: "Thank you, Mr Okafor. As agreed, the balance of ₦150,000 will be paid as follows: ₦50,000 on 10 December, ₦50,000 on 17 December, and ₦50,000 on 24 December. Please confirm this plan. Thank you."

Enforce the first missed instalment immediately. If the first payment is missed, call the same day. Do not wait for the second one. A plan that is enforced at the first slip usually survives; a plan that is ignored at the first slip usually collapses.

## Step 5: What to Say — and What Never to Say

Your words decide whether the customer pays you or blocks your number.

Do say: "I understand things are tight right now. Let us find a way you can pay." Do say: "When exactly will you be able to pay? Let us agree a date." Do say: "I value your business and I want to keep working with you." Do say: "Can you pay part of it today, and the rest by Friday?" Do say: "Is there anything about the goods or service that you are not happy with?"

Never call a customer a thief or a fraudster. Even if you believe it, saying it ends the relationship and makes payment less likely, not more. Never threaten to disgrace a customer on social media or to embarrass them at their office. Threats like that can backfire badly and may expose you to legal trouble. Never threaten to send anyone to their house. Never demand "pay me today or else" without a realistic path to payment. And never call or message in anger — if you are furious, wait an hour. A calm voice collects money; an angry voice collects enemies.

Keep every conversation private. Never discuss a customer's debt with other customers, with their friends, or in a group chat. Debt is a private matter between you and the customer. Public shaming destroys trust, invites a defamation claim, and makes other customers afraid to buy from you on any terms.

## Step 6: Special Situations Nigerian Businesses Face

**The friend or family member who owes you.** This is the hardest debt to collect, because the relationship matters more than the money — until the money starts to matter more than the relationship. Treat friends and family like customers from the start: agree the amount, the date, and the payment method before you supply anything, and put it in a WhatsApp message. "Bro, no problem, I will do it for ₦80,000. Please transfer ₦40,000 now and ₦40,000 when I deliver on Saturday." If they are offended by clear terms, they were never going to pay comfortably anyway. If the debt already exists, have an honest, private conversation: "I value our relationship, but this money is affecting my business. I need it by the end of the month. Can we agree a plan?"

**The customer who keeps promising "tomorrow".** After the third broken promise, stop accepting new dates. Say: "I appreciate your assurances, but I need a part payment today to show commitment. How much can you transfer right now?" If the answer is nothing, move to the final demand stage. Endless promises without payment are a decision — the customer has decided not to pay — and you should act on that information.

## Step 7: When to Escalate

Escalation does not mean aggression. It means using formal, legal tools in the right order.

**Send a lawyer's letter.** A letter from a lawyer on their letterhead, demanding payment within seven or fourteen days, resolves a surprising number of stubborn debts. It signals that you are serious and that the matter is now formal. In Lagos, a lawyer's letter typically costs between ₦20,000 and ₦50,000, so use it for debts large enough to justify the cost — usually ₦100,000 and above.

**Use the Small Claims Court.** Lagos State has a Small Claims Court designed for debts up to ₦5,000,000, and it is faster and cheaper than the regular courts. You do not always need a lawyer, filing fees are modest, and cases are meant to be resolved within weeks rather than years. Bring your invoice, delivery note, WhatsApp messages, and bank statements showing no payment. Many other states have similar small claims procedures — ask a lawyer in your state. This is a realistic option for a small business, not just for big companies.

**Know when to write it off.** Some debts will never be paid. The customer has relocated abroad, the business has closed, or the amount is so small that chasing it costs more than it is worth. Writing it off is not defeat; it is a business decision. Record it, learn from it, close that customer's credit permanently, and focus your energy on customers who pay. The lesson is the real value: change the policy that allowed the loss.

## Step 8: Prevent the Next Debt

Collection is hard. Prevention is easy. Build these habits into your business.

Take deposits on every custom job. No deposit, no work. This single rule prevents most service-business debt. Use payment links: Paystack and Flutterwave links let a customer pay with a card, transfer, or USSD in seconds, so send the link with the invoice. Send invoices immediately and set reminders at the moment you issue the invoice — if you use accounting software like Wave or QuickBooks, or even a simple spreadsheet with calendar reminders, the follow-up dates should be set on day one.

Review your debtors weekly. Every Friday, spend thirty minutes on your tracker. Who is due this week? Who missed a promise? Who needs a call on Monday morning? This habit alone will cut your overdue debts dramatically.

Keep records of everything: save WhatsApp chats, invoices, delivery notes, and bank statements. If a dispute ever reaches a lawyer or a court, these records are your evidence.

## Conclusion

Collecting money customers owe you is not about being harsh. It is about being clear, consistent, and professional. Set your terms before the sale. Send the invoice on time. Remind politely before the due date. Call when the message is ignored. Offer a plan when the customer is struggling. Escalate firmly when promises keep breaking. And prevent the next debt with deposits, payment links, and a weekly review of your tracker.

Start today: open your tracker, list the five most recent debts, and send the first friendly reminder before the day ends.
