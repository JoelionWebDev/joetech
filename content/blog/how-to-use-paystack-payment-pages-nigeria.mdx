Funke sells ready-to-wear Ankara in Yaba, from a market table three days a week and an Instagram page with about 4,000 followers. For two years her routine was the same: post a dress, wait for the DMs, agree a price, send her GTBank account number, and wait again. Some customers paid in ten minutes. Some never paid, and she had already kept the dress aside while another buyer asked her table for the exact same size.

The breaking point came in September. A customer in Abuja sent a screenshot of a ₦22,000 transfer for a bubu gown. Funke packed it, paid a rider ₦3,500 to take it to Jibowu, and learned the next morning that the money never arrived. The screenshot was fake. She lost the dress, the delivery money and a day of trading — and decided her payment method had to change.

What she built instead was not a website. It was a Paystack payment page, set up like a small shop. One link, one page, and everything a customer needs to buy without messaging her first.

## A Payment Link Takes Money. A Payment Page Runs a Shop.

People mix these two up, and the mix-up costs them sales. A payment link is a checkout for one thing at one price. You send it, the customer pays that amount, and that is the end of it. It is perfect when someone has already agreed to buy a specific item and you just need their money.

A payment page is bigger. It holds several products, each with its own photo, price, description and stock count. A customer opens it and sees what you sell, the way they would see clothes hanging on a rail. They pick what they want, choose a quantity, fill in their delivery details, and pay — on a page carrying your business name, logo and colours, so it looks like your shop rather than a blank form.

A payment link is the POS machine on a counter. A payment page is the shop itself.

## Inside Funke's Page: Three Dresses, Real Prices, Real Stock

Funke did not put everything she owns on the page. She picked the three designs that sell fastest and photographed them properly, one afternoon, on her balcony in daylight, against a plain white bedsheet pinned to the wall.

The first is the Ankara midi dress, her best seller, at ₦18,500. The photo shows it on a mannequin, front and back. The description is plain: cotton Ankara, sizes 8 to 16, fully lined, delivery within Lagos in two days. She had nine in stock, four in size 12, three in size 14, two in size 10, and she typed those numbers in as the stock for each size.

The second is the bubu gown at ₦22,000, free size, in two prints. She photographed both prints side by side so nobody would pay and then ask which print they were getting. Stock: six pieces, three of each print.

The third is a two-piece Ankara set, top and wide-leg trousers, at ₦25,000. This one sells slower, so she only put four on the page. When those four are gone, the page shows it as sold out until she sews more.

That stock count is the part most sellers skip, and it is the part that saves the most trouble. Before the page, Funke sold the last size 12 midi dress to two different women on the same Saturday, one on Instagram and one at her market table, because neither sale was recorded anywhere. With stock on the page, the fourth size 12 order simply cannot go through. The customer sees it is gone and picks another size. No apologies, no refunds, no quarrel in the DMs.

## Building the Page, One Product at a Time

Funke built the page on her phone, sitting at her table between customers. She logged into her Paystack dashboard, went to Payments, then Payment Pages, and tapped Create Payment Page. The first thing Paystack asked for was a name, and she learned quickly that the name is for the customer, not for her. "Midi dress" tells a buyer nothing. "Ankara Midi Dress — Sizes 8 to 16" tells her everything before she even opens the page.

Each product needed a short description, and Funke wrote the way she talks to a customer at her table. Fabric, lining, sizes, delivery time. Two or three sentences, no more. Nobody reads a brand story while holding their card.

The photos mattered more than anything else she typed. A blurry photo taken under a yellow bulb looks like a gamble; a clear photo in daylight looks like a shop. Funke's rule is simple: if a photo is not good enough for her Instagram feed, it is not good enough for her payment page. The first photo shows the full dress, and the second, where she has one, is a close-up of the fabric.

For each product she also set the details Paystack should collect: name, phone number, delivery address, and size. That is all. Every extra box on a checkout page is a small reason to close the tab, so she collects the delivery day on WhatsApp after payment, when the customer is already committed.

## The Settings That Make It Look Like Her Shop

First, the custom URL. Paystack gives every page a link, but you can shape the ending. Hers is paystack.com/pay/funke-ankara. It is short enough to say out loud at her market table, easy to type from a flyer, and it carries her name. A customer who sees that link knows whose shop she is walking into. Compare that with a long string of random letters, which looks like something a scammer would send.

Second, the branding. She uploaded her small logo and picked her colours, so her page header is now the deep orange she uses on all her market flyers, with her business name across the top. A customer who has seen her flyer at a trade fair recognises the page instantly, and that recognition matters, because the biggest fear of a first-time online buyer in Nigeria is paying a stranger.

Third, the storefront setting. Paystack lets you show your payment pages together, so one link shows everything you sell. Funke turned this on, and her Instagram bio now carries that single link, with her WhatsApp status pointing to it every time she posts a new design.

Fourth, the quantity limit. For the two-piece set, where she only has four pieces, she set a maximum of one per order, so one buyer cannot sweep up all four while others are still deciding. For the midi dress she allows up to two, because a customer buying for herself and her sister is common.

Fifth, the redirect after payment. When a customer finishes paying, Paystack can send them to a page you choose, and Funke set hers to her WhatsApp number. The buyer lands straight in a chat with her, already paid, asking when the dress will be dispatched. The sale is closed before the conversation even starts.

## What the Money Looks Like After Paystack Takes Its Cut

Funke sells the midi dress for ₦18,500. Paystack's fee on a Nigerian card is 1.5 percent plus ₦100, capped at ₦2,000, so on that dress the fee is about ₦378, and she receives roughly ₦18,122. On the bubu at ₦22,000 the fee is about ₦430, leaving her about ₦21,570. On the two-piece set at ₦25,000 the fee is about ₦475, leaving her about ₦24,525.

She did the maths the evening she set the prices. Before the page, she lost far more than ₦378 on some sales: the fake alert that cost her a ₦22,000 dress and ₦3,500 in delivery, the customers who promised to pay and vanished. The fee is visible and predictable. The losses from her old method were invisible and much bigger.

She prices with the fee already inside the number. Her page never shows a separate charge for payment: the customer sees ₦18,500 and pays ₦18,500. A customer who sees an extra charge at checkout feels tricked, and a customer who feels tricked rarely comes back.

The money lands in her Paystack balance first, then settles into her GTBank account, usually the next working day. She checks her dashboard every morning, the way she used to check her bank alerts, except now each payment already has a name, a product, a size and a delivery address attached to it.

## Running the Shop Day to Day

A page is not something you build once and forget. Each morning Funke opens the dashboard and looks at yesterday's orders, then messages each customer on WhatsApp to confirm the size, the address, and the delivery day. If the address is outside Lagos, she confirms the state first, because delivery to Enugu costs more than delivery to Ibadan.

When a size sells out, she updates the stock that same day. When she sews a new batch, she adds the pieces to the page before she posts about them on Instagram. She learned this the hard way in her second week, when she posted a new print before updating the page and three customers paid for a dress she had not yet listed. She refunded all three, and she has not made that mistake since.

## When a Page Is Enough, and When You Need a Real Store

A page is enough while your range is small, say under fifteen products, and your customers mostly find you on WhatsApp and Instagram. It is enough while every product has one price and a handful of sizes or colours. It is enough while you handle delivery yourself, quoting dispatch fees in the chat after payment, and while your customers are comfortable messaging you to sort out the details. Funke is in this stage now, and the page does everything she needs.

You start to outgrow it when the page itself becomes the bottleneck. If you have forty products and customers cannot search or filter them, they will give up scrolling. If you sell the same dress in six sizes and four prints, the custom fields turn into a long form and buyers abandon it halfway. If you run discounts or promo codes, a payment page has nowhere to put them, and if you want delivery fees for Lagos, Abuja and Port Harcourt calculated automatically instead of negotiated in a chat, you need a real store.

Funke already knows her own trigger. When she starts selling more than fifteen designs at once, or hires someone to sew full time and her range doubles, she will build a proper online store, with a catalogue, a cart and automatic delivery fees. The page will not be wasted then. It will become the checkout for her best sellers, while the store handles the browsing. The page was never meant to be the final destination. It was the first shop she could afford, and it is doing that job well.

Her September self would not recognise her November routine. She still has her table in Yaba, and she still loves the haggling and the laughter of the market. But now, when a customer in Abuja asks how to pay, Funke sends one link, paystack.com/pay/funke-ankara, and the customer picks a dress, pays, and lands in her WhatsApp with the order already confirmed. The dress still travels to Jibowu on a rider's bike — except now, the money is already in her account before the bike leaves.
