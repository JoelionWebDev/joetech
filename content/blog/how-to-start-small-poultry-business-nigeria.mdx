Eggs and chicken are eaten every single day in Nigeria. From the woman frying eggs at a roadside stall in Ikeja to the family buying a live chicken for Sunday rice in Enugu, the demand never stops. That is why poultry remains one of the most reliable agribusinesses in the country. You do not need a large farm to begin. Many successful poultry farmers in Nigeria started with 50 or 100 birds in a backyard pen and grew from there.

This guide explains how to start a small poultry business properly: choosing the right type of birds, building a pen, budgeting your startup costs, feeding and vaccinating your flock, and selling at a profit. The prices quoted are realistic Nigerian figures as of late 2026.

## Why Poultry Farming Works in Nigeria

Poultry has three advantages that most other small businesses do not have. First, the product sells itself. Nigerians eat eggs for breakfast and chicken at every celebration. A crate of eggs is one of the fastest-moving items in any market, and a healthy broiler will always find a buyer. Second, the production cycle is short. Broilers are ready for sale in six to eight weeks, so your money does not stay locked up for months. Layers start producing eggs at about 18 to 20 weeks, and then they keep laying for over a year. Third, you can start small. Unlike crop farming, which needs acres of land, poultry needs only a pen, feed and water.

The risks are real too. Disease can wipe out a flock in days. Feed prices rise without warning, and a bad batch of day-old chicks can ruin a whole cycle. Every risk in this guide has a practical way to manage it. The farmers who succeed are not the ones with the most money. They are the ones who keep clean pens, vaccinate on schedule and keep proper records.

## Step 1: Choose Layers, Broilers or Noilers

Your first decision is what type of bird to raise, because it determines your income pattern, your costs and your daily work.

**Layers** are hens kept for egg production. They start laying at 18 to 20 weeks and produce eggs for 72 to 80 weeks. A good layer produces about five to six eggs a week. Layers give you steady daily income because you sell eggs every day or every few days. The downside is the long wait before the first income, and layers eat a lot of feed before they start laying. Layers suit a farmer who wants regular income and can wait five months for the first sale.

**Broilers** are chickens raised for meat. They grow fast and are ready for sale in six to eight weeks, weighing 2 to 2.5 kilograms. A broiler sells for ₦6,000 to ₦9,000 in Lagos, depending on size and season, with prices rising sharply around Christmas, Easter and Sallah. Broilers give you fast cash, but the income comes in batches. You sell a whole batch, rest the pen, clean it and start again. Broilers suit a farmer who wants quick turnover and can handle the work of selling a full batch at once.

For a first-timer, broilers are usually the best starting point. The cycle is short, so you learn fast and see your money back quickly. Start with 100 broilers. If that cycle goes well, your second batch can be 200, and you can add layers later for steady egg income. Do not start with 1,000 birds. A big flock with a beginner's mistakes is a big loss.

## Step 2: Write a Simple Plan and Budget

Before you buy a single chick, write down your plan. How many birds will you start with? What will you sell, and to whom? How much money do you have, and how much do you expect to make?

Here is a realistic startup budget for 100 broilers in Lagos or Ogun State:

- 100 day-old chicks at ₦1,200 each: ₦120,000
- Feed for 8 weeks (about 10 bags): ₦450,000
- Vaccines and medication: ₦25,000
- Drinkers, feeders and heat source: ₦40,000
- Wood shavings and litter: ₦15,000
- Pen construction or renovation: ₦150,000
- Transport and miscellaneous: ₦30,000
- **Total: roughly ₦830,000**

If you already have a pen, the cost drops to about ₦680,000. If your budget is smaller, start with 50 birds. The cost roughly halves, and you still learn the same lessons. Never borrow money for your first batch. Use your own savings so that a bad first cycle does not leave you in debt.

## Step 3: Build a Good Pen

The pen is your birds' home for their whole life. A bad pen causes disease, slow growth and losses. A good pen is dry, well ventilated, secure from thieves and predators, and easy to clean.

Choose a location that is slightly raised so rainwater drains away. A pen that floods in the rainy season will kill birds and breed disease. Keep the pen away from your kitchen and living rooms, but close enough that you can check on the birds easily. If you are in a residential area, check with your neighbours and your estate or local government rules first. Some estates do not allow poultry, and a complaint can shut you down.

Build with blocks for the lower walls and wire mesh for the upper part so air flows freely. A pen for 100 broilers needs about 10 to 12 square metres of floor space, roughly 3 metres by 4 metres. Overcrowding is one of the biggest causes of disease. The roof should be high enough for hot air to escape and strong enough to survive a storm. Use zinc or asbestos roofing sheets, and make sure rain cannot blow in.

The floor should be concrete or well-compacted earth covered with dry litter. Wood shavings are the most common litter in Nigeria, and a bag costs around ₦2,000 to ₦3,000. Spread the litter 5 to 8 centimetres thick. Change it when it becomes wet or smelly. Wet litter breeds coccidiosis, a disease that kills chicks fast.

Inside the pen you need feeders and drinkers. For 100 birds, provide at least four feeders and four drinkers so that every bird can eat and drink without fighting. Hang the feeders at the birds' back height so feed does not spill. Clean drinkers every day. Dirty water is one of the fastest ways to spread disease through a flock.

## Step 4: Buy Healthy Day-Old Chicks

The quality of your chicks decides the quality of your whole batch. Buy only from a reputable hatchery. In the South West, well-known hatcheries include Obasanjo Farms, Zartech, Amo Farm, CHI Farms and Agrited. Ask other poultry farmers in your area which hatchery they trust, and buy from that one.

A healthy day-old chick is alert, dry, active and has clean eyes and a closed navel. Avoid chicks that are wet, weak, sleepy or have swollen bellies. Buy chicks that are all the same age and the same breed, so they grow evenly and can be sold at the same time.

Common broiler breeds in Nigeria include Arbor Acre, Ross 308, Cobb 500 and Hubbard. All are good. The breed matters less than the hatchery's quality and the feed you give them. Order your chicks in advance and confirm the delivery date. Have the pen ready, warm and clean before the chicks arrive. The first 48 hours are the most delicate period of a chick's life, and a cold or dirty pen on arrival can cause losses that haunt the whole cycle.

## Step 5: Feed Your Birds Properly

Feed is your biggest cost, taking 70 to 75 percent of your total spending. It is also the biggest factor in how fast your birds grow. Get feeding right and the business works. Get it wrong and no amount of good luck will save you.

Buy feed from a reputable mill or brand. In Nigeria, popular feed brands include TopFeed, Hybrid Feeds, Animal Care, Livestock Feeds and New Hope. A 25kg bag of broiler starter feed costs around ₦22,000 to ₦28,000, and finisher feed is slightly cheaper. Prices vary by location and season, so confirm before each batch.

Broilers need three feed stages. Starter feed for weeks 1 to 3, grower feed for weeks 4 to 5, and finisher feed for weeks 6 to 8. Each stage has a different protein level matched to the bird's growth. Do not feed finisher to day-old chicks. Their small bodies cannot use it properly and they will grow slowly.

For layers, the stages are chick mash, grower mash and layer mash. Layers need extra calcium for strong eggshells, so layer mash contains limestone. You can also offer crushed oyster shells or limestone grit separately. A layer eats about 110 to 120 grams of feed a day once it starts laying.

Feed your birds twice a day, morning and evening, and make sure feed is always available during the day for broilers. Clean water must be available at all times. A bird that has no water will stop eating, and a bird that stops eating stops growing. In the hot season, birds drink more water, so check drinkers twice a day.

Keep feed records. Write down how many bags you buy, the price, the date and how long each bag lasts. This tells you your real feed cost per bird, which is the number that decides your profit.

## Step 6: Vaccinate and Protect Against Disease

Disease is the single biggest threat to a poultry business in Nigeria. One outbreak of Newcastle disease can kill an entire flock in a week. Vaccination is cheap. Treatment after an outbreak is expensive and often too late.

Follow a standard vaccination schedule for your birds. For broilers, the usual schedule is Newcastle disease vaccine (La Sota) at day 18, Gumboro vaccine at day 10 and day 24, and a second Newcastle dose around day 28. For layers, the schedule is longer and includes fowl pox and fowl typhoid vaccines. Buy vaccines from a licensed veterinary pharmacy, keep them cold in a cooler with ice, and use them the same day you open them. A vaccine that has been left in the sun is useless.

Keep the pen clean. Remove droppings and wet litter regularly. Disinfect the pen between batches with a poultry disinfectant, and leave it empty for at least two weeks before bringing in new birds. This rest period breaks the disease cycle. Many beginners skip it and pay for it with sick birds.

Control who enters the pen. Visitors, feed sellers and other farmers can carry disease on their shoes and clothes. Keep a footbath with disinfectant at the pen entrance and use it every time. Do not borrow equipment from another farmer's pen, and do not visit another poultry farm and then walk straight into your own pen.

Watch your birds every day. Healthy birds are active, eat well and have bright eyes. Sick birds are dull, eat little, sit apart from the flock, or have drooping wings and watery droppings. If you notice sick birds, separate them immediately into a sick bay and call a veterinarian. Early action saves flocks. Find a poultry vet in your area before you need one, not after your birds start dying.

## Step 7: Keep Records and Manage Money

A poultry farm without records is a gamble. You need to know what you spent, what you earned and what went wrong. Keep a simple record book or a spreadsheet on your phone and update it every day. It takes five minutes.

Record the date you bought the chicks, the number, the hatchery and the price. Record daily feed use, daily deaths, vaccines given and any medication used. For layers, record daily egg production. At the end of each batch, add up the total cost and the total income. This gives you your profit per bird, which is the single most important number in your business.

Open a separate account for the poultry business. Money from egg and chicken sales goes in, and feed, vaccines and transport come out. At the end of each month, you can see whether the business made money. Many farmers feel busy and assume they are profitable, then discover at the end of the year that feed costs ate everything. Records stop that from happening.

## Step 8: Sell Your Eggs and Birds for Profit

Selling is where many poultry farmers lose money, not because their birds are bad but because they sell in a rush. Plan your sales before the birds are ready.

For broilers, the best prices come around festive seasons. Christmas, Easter, Sallah and New Year push broiler prices up by 20 to 30 percent. If you can time a batch to finish a week before Christmas, you earn significantly more. Work backwards from the festive date: if Christmas is December 25 and broilers take seven weeks, place your chicks around the first week of November.

Sell through several channels. Sell live birds to neighbours and walk-in customers at your farm gate. Supply local markets and chicken sellers. Supply restaurants, bukas and event caterers who buy regularly. Offer home delivery in your estate for a small fee. The more channels you have, the less you depend on any single buyer.

For eggs, sell in crates of 30. A crate of eggs sells for ₦4,500 to ₦6,500 depending on the season and your location, with prices higher when supply is low. Sell to households, shops, bakeries, schools and restaurants. Bakeries are excellent customers because they buy in bulk every week. Offer a small discount to a bakery that buys ten crates a week, and you have a steady income.

Use WhatsApp to sell. Take clean photos of your birds and eggs, post them on your status, and keep a broadcast list of customers. When a batch is ready, send a message: "Fresh broilers available, 2.2kg average, ₦7,500 each. Delivery available in Lekki and Ajah." Customers who have bought from you before will order again if you remind them.

## Step 9: Mistakes That Kill Small Poultry Farms

**Starting too big.** A first batch of 1,000 birds is a big risk for a beginner. Start with 50 or 100, learn the routine, then scale up.

**Skipping vaccination.** Vaccines cost a few thousand naira per batch. An outbreak costs the whole flock. Never skip a vaccine to save money.

**Buying cheap, unknown feed.** Bad feed means slow growth, weak birds and poor egg production. Buy from a brand you trust, even if it costs slightly more.

**Overcrowding.** Too many birds in a small pen means stress, fighting, disease and slow growth. Follow the space rule: about one square metre for every eight to ten broilers.

**No records.** If you do not know your cost per bird, you do not know whether you are making money. Keep records from day one.

**Selling on credit.** Do not give birds or eggs on credit to customers who may never pay. Cash on delivery protects your business. If a corporate customer insists on credit, get a written agreement and a clear payment date.

## Step 10: Growing the Business

Once your first two or three batches run smoothly, you can grow in several ways. Increase your flock size gradually, from 100 to 200 to 500 birds. Add layers alongside broilers so you have daily egg income plus batch meat income. Start producing your own feed if you have the space and the knowledge, because feed is your biggest cost and milling your own can save 15 to 20 percent.

You can also add value. Dressed and packaged chicken sells for more than live birds, especially to busy professionals and supermarkets. Smoked chicken is another value-added product that stores well and sells at a premium. If you have layers, consider selling fertilised eggs or point-of-lay birds to other farmers.

## Conclusion

A small poultry business in Nigeria is a real opportunity, not a get-rich-quick scheme. It rewards patience, cleanliness and consistency. Start with 50 or 100 broilers, buy healthy chicks from a trusted hatchery, feed them well, vaccinate on schedule, keep clean records and sell through several channels. Your first batch will teach you more than any book. Your second batch will be better, and your fifth will run like a routine.

The demand for eggs and chicken in Nigeria is not going away. Families eat them every day, and the population keeps growing. The farmers who earn from it are the ones who treat it as a business from day one. Buy your chicks, build your pen and start. Your first crate of eggs or your first batch of broilers is closer than you think.
