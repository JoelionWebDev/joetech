Running a small business in Nigeria without a budget is like driving from Lekki to Abuja at night with no fuel gauge. You might arrive, but you will spend the whole journey guessing and hoping the tank does not run dry halfway. Many Nigerian business owners work hard every day — selling, delivering, answering WhatsApp messages late into the night — yet at the end of the month they cannot say where the money went. The shop was busy and orders came in, but the bank balance tells a different story.

A budget changes that. It is not a punishment, and it is not only for big companies with accountants. A budget is simply a plan for your money before the month begins: how much you expect to come in, how much you plan to spend, and how much you intend to keep. When you write it down, you stop guessing and start deciding on purpose. This guide shows you how to build one that fits a Nigerian small business, whether you run a fashion store in Lekki, a food business in Surulere, a phone accessories shop in Ikeja Computer Village, a salon in Abuja, or an online business selling on Instagram and WhatsApp.

## Why Your Small Business Needs a Budget

First, a budget helps you survive. In Nigeria, costs move fast. Fuel prices change, data subscriptions go up, and your supplier raises prices without warning. A business without a budget absorbs every increase silently until one day there is no money left to restock. A budget forces you to notice rising costs early, while you still have time to adjust your prices or find a cheaper supplier.

Second, it brings clarity. When you know your numbers, you can answer real questions. Can I afford an assistant this month? Should I buy that new freezer, or wait? Is my Instagram advert bringing in more money than it costs? Without a budget, these are guesses. With one, they become calculations you can trust.

Third, a budget shows your real profit. Many owners confuse revenue with profit. Selling goods worth ₦800,000 in a month is not the same as making ₦800,000. After stock, transport, rent, data, and packaging, the real profit might be ₦120,000 — or nothing at all. Knowing the true figure is the only way to plan expansion or decide whether a second location makes sense.

## What a Business Budget Actually Is

A business budget is a written plan with three parts: your expected income, your planned expenses, and the profit you want to keep. That is all. It does not need to be a long document or a complicated spreadsheet. A table in a notebook, a Google Sheet on your phone, or a neat note can work perfectly.

The budget covers one month. At the start of the month you write the plan. During the month you record what actually happens. At the end of the month you compare the plan with reality and learn from the difference. That monthly review is where the power lies, because it turns your budget from a guess into a tool that gets smarter every month.

Income is every naira the business earns: sales, service fees, delivery charges, and anything else the business brings in. Expenses are everything it spends: stock, rent, transport, salaries, data, electricity, packaging, advertising, and bank charges. Profit is what is left after expenses are taken from income. If the number is negative, the business spent more than it earned, and the budget shows you exactly where the leak is.

## Step 1: Know How Much Money Comes In

Start with income, because everything else depends on it. Look at your last three months of sales and work out the average. If you sell through WhatsApp, go through your chats and bank alerts. If you use Paystack or Flutterwave, check your dashboard. If you take cash, check your sales book. Add up each month separately, then divide by three.

Take a small fashion business in Lekki that sold ₦650,000 in September, ₦720,000 in October, and ₦580,000 in November. The total is ₦1,950,000, so the average is ₦650,000 a month. Use that average — not your best month, and not the month you are hoping for. Budgeting with your best month is a common mistake. It makes the plan look comfortable on paper and impossible in real life.

If your business is new and you lack three months of records, use an honest, conservative estimate. It is better to plan for ₦400,000 and earn ₦500,000 than to plan for ₦700,000 and earn ₦450,000. Also list your income sources separately — product sales, delivery fees, a training class — so that if one source dries up, you can see at once how much income you lose.

## Step 2: List Your Fixed Costs

Fixed costs stay roughly the same every month, whether you sell a lot or a little. Rent is the classic example: ₦150,000 a month for a shop in Ikeja does not change because sales were slow. Salaries are the same. If you pay your sales assistant ₦60,000 a month, that is fixed until you change the arrangement.

List every fixed cost in your business: rent, salaries, internet and data, electricity and generator fuel set aside monthly, software subscriptions, loan repayments, and retainers such as an accountant's fee. Do not forget the small ones — the ₦5,000 business phone line, the ₦10,000 security levy in your plaza. Together, they add up quickly.

Add them all up. That total tells you the minimum your business must earn before it makes any profit at all. If your fixed costs are ₦280,000 a month, you must earn more than that, plus the cost of your goods, before you have made a single naira of profit. Many owners are shocked the first time they add up their fixed costs. The number is almost always bigger than they expected.

## Step 3: List Your Variable Costs

Variable costs change with how much you sell. The biggest one for most Nigerian small businesses is the cost of goods — stock you buy to resell, or raw materials for what you produce. A food vendor who sells more plates in December spends more on ingredients in December. A phone accessories seller who sells 200 chargers spends more on stock than one who sells 80.

Other variable costs include packaging, delivery and logistics, fuel for dispatch bikes, bank charges, Paystack or Flutterwave transaction fees, advertising, and commissions. Pay a rider ₦1,500 per delivery and make 100 deliveries, and that is ₦150,000. Make 40 deliveries and it is ₦60,000.

Budget variable costs as a percentage of income where you can. If your cost of goods is usually about 55 percent of sales, then on ₦650,000 of expected income your stock budget is about ₦357,500. Percentages keep the budget flexible: when sales rise, the stock budget rises with them, and when sales fall, it falls too. Be honest about transaction fees as well. At around 1.5 percent, fees on ₦650,000 of sales come to nearly ₦10,000 a month. Small, but ignoring it month after month is how leaks grow.

## Step 4: Separate Business Money from Personal Money

Open a separate bank account for your business, even if it is just a second savings account used only for business. All business income goes in. All business expenses come out. Personal spending comes from your personal account. The two should not mix.

Then pay yourself a fixed salary from the business. Choose an amount — say ₦100,000 a month — and transfer it to your personal account on a fixed day, like an employee's payday. That salary is your personal money. But do not dip into the business account for personal needs outside that salary, and do not quietly fund the business from your personal account without recording it.

This does three things. It tells you whether the business can actually afford you. It stops the slow drain where business cash disappears into daily life. And it keeps your records clean, which matters when you apply for a loan or work with an accountant. If a full salary feels impossible now, start with ₦40,000 or ₦50,000 and raise it as the business grows. The amount matters less than the discipline of a fixed sum, on a fixed day, from a separate account.

## Step 5: Decide Your Profit Target First

Most owners treat profit as whatever is left at the end of the month. That is backwards. Decide your profit target first, then build the rest of the budget around it. Suppose your expected income is ₦650,000 and you want to keep ₦100,000 as profit and savings. That leaves ₦550,000 for all expenses. If fixed costs are ₦280,000, that leaves ₦270,000 for variable costs, including stock. If your stock normally costs more than that, you have a problem to solve before the month begins: sell more, raise prices slightly, or find a cheaper supplier. That is exactly the decision a budget exists to force.

Your target does not need to be large. Even ₦30,000 or ₦50,000 a month builds up — ₦50,000 a month is ₦300,000 in six months, enough for new equipment, a shop upgrade, or surviving a slow month without borrowing. Keep it in a separate savings account if you can. Money sitting beside daily spending gets spent. Money in a separate account feels different, and that small friction often protects it.

Add a small emergency line too, even ₦20,000 or ₦30,000 a month. Generators break down, suppliers raise prices, and heavy Lagos rain damages shops. Without room for surprises, every surprise becomes a crisis. Over a year, ₦25,000 a month becomes ₦300,000 — a cushion that can carry your business through a bad month. Aim, over time, for an emergency fund that covers one to three months of fixed costs.

## Step 6: A Simple Monthly Budget Template You Can Copy

Here is a complete monthly budget for a small Nigerian business. Change the numbers to match your own.

Income: product sales ₦550,000; service fees ₦70,000; delivery charges collected ₦30,000. Total expected income: ₦650,000.

Fixed costs: shop rent ₦150,000; staff salary ₦60,000; internet and data ₦25,000; electricity and generator fuel ₦30,000; software and subscriptions ₦10,000; miscellaneous fixed levies ₦5,000. Total fixed costs: ₦280,000.

Variable costs: cost of goods or stock ₦200,000; packaging ₦15,000; delivery and logistics ₦25,000; advertising ₦20,000; bank and payment charges ₦10,000. Total variable costs: ₦270,000.

Profit and savings: business savings ₦70,000; emergency fund contribution ₦30,000. Total set aside: ₦100,000.

Total expenses plus savings come to ₦650,000, so the budget balances exactly. Notice that profit and savings were planned first, not left to chance, and that every category has a number. If advertising costs ₦35,000 instead of ₦20,000, you will see it at once and can ask why. Copy this structure into a notebook or spreadsheet and fill in your own figures. Do not worry about perfection the first time. Your first budget is a draft, and your third will be far more accurate because you will have two months of real data behind it.

## Step 7: Track Spending Weekly and Cut Waste

A budget you write and never look at again is useless. The habit that makes it work is a short weekly check-in. Pick a day — Sunday evening suits many owners — and spend fifteen minutes reviewing the week. How much came in? How much went out? Are you on track, or already over budget somewhere?

Record every expense, even small ones. The ₦2,000 for nylon bags, the ₦3,500 for fuel to deliver an order, the ₦1,000 for a receipt book — these are exactly the amounts people forget, and they are why budgets fail. Use whatever you will actually stick to: a notebook, a notes app, a simple Google Sheet, or a free expense tracker. The best method is the one you use every day. If staff spend business money, insist on a receipt for everything, kept in one envelope or one WhatsApp group. Review them together weekly. It protects your staff as well as your money.

When expenses run too high, cut waste, not value. Drop the second data subscription you do not need, the software tool you have not opened in months, or packaging bought in small quantities when bulk buying from a Lagos market costs far less per unit. Negotiate with suppliers too — regular bulk orders and prompt payment often earn a better price.

## Step 8: Plan for Slow Months and Festive Seasons

Nigerian business is seasonal. December is busy for fashion, food, events, and gifts. January is famously slow — customers have spent their money, schools are resuming, and everyone is tightening their belt. Ramadan, Easter, and the rainy season all shift buying patterns. A good budget plans for this instead of being surprised by it.

When you have a strong month, resist spending all the extra profit. Put a bigger share into savings during good months so slow months are covered. Think of December's extra income as January's rent. That is how businesses survive the January slump without borrowing or panic-selling stock at a loss.

Plan ahead for known big expenses too. If rent is due every January, set aside a portion monthly from July. If you need new stock for Christmas, start saving in September. If the business needs a new freezer or machine, save over three months instead of buying on credit at a high interest rate. List yearly expenses on a calendar — rent renewal, equipment replacement, festive stock, annual subscriptions — divide each by twelve, and add that amount to your monthly budget as a savings line. Big bills become small monthly habits.

## Mistakes to Avoid and Tools That Help

The first mistake is budgeting only in your head. A budget in your mind is a wish, not a plan. Write it down. The second is forgetting irregular costs. Phones break and annual subscriptions renew, so look at a full year of expenses, not just last month, and divide yearly costs by twelve. The third is making the budget too complicated. With forty categories you will abandon it by week two. Start with ten to fifteen categories at most. A simple budget you use beats a perfect one you ignore.

The fourth mistake is treating the budget as fixed in stone. If fuel prices jump mid-month, adjust. If a big order comes in, raise the stock budget. A budget that never changes has stopped reflecting reality. The fifth is giving up after one bad month. Every business has months where the plan falls apart — an emergency, a supplier problem, a slow season. One bad month means the budget did its job by showing you what went wrong.

You do not need expensive software. A notebook and pen work fine — many successful Nigerian traders have run businesses on notebooks for decades. A Google Sheet on your phone is the next step up: it adds numbers automatically and you can share it with an accountant. Keep your receipts in an envelope, or photograph them into a dedicated WhatsApp group, as a backup if your notebook is lost. ## Your First Budget Starts This Week

You do not need to wait for the first of the month or a perfect moment. Open a notebook or a fresh spreadsheet today and work through these steps: list your income, list your fixed costs, list your variable costs, set a profit target, and write it all down. Give yourself one hour. That single hour will give you more control over your business than any amount of extra hustle without a plan.

Then commit to the weekly check-in: fifteen minutes every Sunday evening. Record what came in, what went out, and compare it with your plan. At the end of the month, do the full review, note one lesson, and make one change. Repeat for three months, and you will know your business in a way you never did before.

A budget will not make your business rich overnight. What it will do is stop the silent leaks, show you your real profit, protect you in slow months, and give you confidence to make bigger decisions — hiring, expanding, raising prices, buying equipment — based on facts instead of feelings. In a business environment as challenging as Nigeria's, that clarity is one of the most valuable assets your business can have. Start this week, keep it simple, and let your budget grow with your business.
