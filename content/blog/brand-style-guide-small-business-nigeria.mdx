A customer sees your flyer on Instagram, then visits your website, then chats with you on WhatsApp. On Instagram your business uses one colour, on your website it uses another, and the WhatsApp display picture is a photo from two years ago. The customer is not sure it is the same business, and that small doubt is enough to make them buy from someone else.

That is the problem a brand style guide solves. It is a short document that records how your business should look and sound everywhere it appears. Big companies have had these for years, but small businesses in Nigeria need them just as much, because most of your customers meet you in several places at once: your website, Instagram, WhatsApp, a flyer, and a shop sign. When all of those look like they belong to the same business, customers trust you faster and remember you longer.

You do not need a design agency or a large budget to create one. A clear, practical style guide can be written in a day. This guide walks through each part, step by step.

## Start with your brand foundations

Before you choose colours or fonts, write down three short foundations: what your business does, who it serves, and how it should feel to customers. These three sentences guide every other decision in the guide.

What your business does should be one clear sentence. For example: "We sell ready-to-wear clothes for working women in Lagos." Who it serves is equally specific: "Busy professionals who want good quality without spending a whole day shopping." How it should feel is the personality: "Friendly, neat, and reliable, never loud or pushy."

Keep this section short. Three sentences are enough.

## Set the rules for your logo

Your logo is the most recognised part of your brand, so it needs clear rules. Start by saving your logo in the formats you actually use: a full-colour version for your website and flyers, a single-colour version for stamps and receipts, and a version with a transparent background for placing on photos.

Then write down the rules for using it. State the minimum size it should appear at, so it never becomes a tiny unreadable mark on a business card. State the clear space around it, meaning nothing else should sit too close to it. State what nobody should do to it: do not stretch it, do not change its colours, do not add effects or shadows, and do not place it on a busy photograph where it disappears.

If your logo includes your business name, write down the exact spelling and spacing of that name. Many Nigerian businesses lose consistency here, with one spelling on the website, another on the shop sign, and a third on Instagram. One spelling, used everywhere, is part of your brand. If you are working with a designer on your logo or other brand materials, read our guide on [how to brief a graphic designer in Nigeria](/blog/brief-graphic-designer-brand-nigeria) before you hand over the job, so the designer gets the details right the first time.

## Choose your colours and write them down

Colour is what customers remember first. Choose one main colour, one or two supporting colours, and one neutral colour for backgrounds and text. Your main colour should appear on your logo, your website buttons, and your packaging. Your supporting colours add variety in flyers and social media posts. Your neutral colour, usually white, black, or a soft grey, keeps everything readable.

The important step is writing the exact colour codes down. A colour described as "blue" can be produced in dozens of shades by different designers and printers. Write the hex code for each colour, for example a blue might be #1B4F72, and keep a note of it in your guide. When a designer, printer, or social media handler asks for your colours, you send the codes, and the result matches every time.

Choose colours that suit your business and your customers, and check they stay readable on a phone screen in bright sunlight, because that is where most of your customers will see them.

## Pick fonts and a tone of voice

Your fonts should be as consistent as your colours. Choose one font for headings and one for body text, and use them everywhere. A heading font can have character, but the body font must be easy to read in long paragraphs.

Your tone of voice is how your business sounds when it writes. Decide whether you speak formally or casually, whether you use "you" or "we" in your captions, and which words you avoid. A fashion business might sound warm and playful. A logistics company might sound direct and reliable. Write three or four example sentences in your guide, such as how you announce a new product or reply to a customer complaint. When anyone writes for your business, those examples show them the voice to copy.

## Add rules for photos and everyday templates

Customers judge your business by your photos, so your guide should cover them too. Decide on a photo style: bright and natural, or warm and moody. State that product photos should be taken in good daylight, against a plain background, and that the product should fill most of the frame. If you use stock photos on your website, choose ones that look like real Nigerian life, not generic images that could belong to any business in any country.

Then create the templates your business uses every day. A price list template, a WhatsApp status template, an invoice header, and a social media post template are enough to start. Each template should already contain your logo, colours, and fonts, so anyone who uses it produces work that looks like your brand without having to think about design. Free design tools make this easy, and once the templates exist, even a staff member with no design experience can create a post that looks professional.

## Keep the guide short, shared, and updated

A style guide that sits in a drawer is useless. Keep yours to a few pages, save it as a PDF, and share it with everyone who creates anything for your business: your designer, your social media handler, your printer, and any staff who post on your behalf. When a new person joins, the guide is the first document they should read.

Review the guide once or twice a year. Businesses change, and a guide written when you had one product may not fit when you have five. Update the colours, the logo, or the templates when they change, and send the new version to everyone who has the old one. A guide that grows with your business keeps working for years.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How long should a brand style guide be?**

For a small business, three to five pages is plenty. It should cover your brand foundations, logo rules, colours, fonts, tone of voice, and photo style. Longer guides are for large companies with many teams. A short guide that people actually read is far more useful than a long one nobody opens.

**Do I need a professional designer to create one?**

Not necessarily. You can write the foundations, choose your colours and fonts, and build your templates yourself using free design tools. A designer is helpful for creating or refining your logo and for setting up the first templates, but the decisions in the guide belong to you, because you know your business and your customers best.

**What if I already have a logo and colours I like?**

Then your guide is mostly a matter of writing down what you already use. Record the exact colour codes, the font names, and the logo rules. That alone will make your business look more consistent, because everyone working for you will finally be using the same details instead of guessing.

**How much does it cost to create a brand style guide?**

It can cost nothing but your time if you write it yourself and use free design tools for templates. If you hire a designer to create the logo and the first templates, that is the main cost, and it is a one-time investment. The guide itself saves money afterwards, because you stop paying for designs that have to be redone when they do not match.

## Make your business look like one business

A brand style guide is not decoration. It is the document that makes every part of your business look like it belongs together, so customers recognise you instantly and trust you faster. Start with your foundations, set the rules for your logo, colours, fonts, and voice, and build the templates you use every day. Share it with everyone who works on your brand, and update it as your business grows.
