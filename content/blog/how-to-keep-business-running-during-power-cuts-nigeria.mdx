Power cuts are not an emergency in Nigeria. They are a normal part of running a business. If you wait for stable public power before you serve customers, you will spend many days each month closed, losing sales, losing stock, and losing customers to competitors who have found a way to keep going.

The businesses that survive power cuts are not always the ones with the biggest generators. They are the ones with a plan. They know what must stay on, what can wait, and what to tell customers when the lights go out. They have backup power that matches their real needs, not power that tries to run everything at once. They protect their cold stock, keep their phones charged, keep their payments working, and keep selling even when the neighbourhood is in darkness.

This guide is for small business owners in Lagos, Abuja, Port Harcourt, Ibadan, Onitsha, Kano, and every other Nigerian city where power supply is unreliable. Whether you run a shop in Balogun Market, a salon in Lekki, a cold room in Mile 12, a pharmacy in Surulere, a cyber cafe in Ikeja, a restaurant in Yaba, or a tailoring shop in Aba, the same principles apply. Plan for the cut before it happens, spend your backup budget where it protects income, and build simple habits that keep the business open.

## Start With What Must Stay On

The first mistake many owners make is trying to power everything. That is expensive and it fails. A small generator cannot run a freezer, two air conditioners, a water pump, a television, and a fridge at the same time. It will trip, it will overheat, and it will burn more fuel than you can afford.

Start by listing everything in your shop that uses electricity. Then divide the list into three groups. The first group is essential: things that protect income or stock if they stop. The second group is useful: things that make work easier but can wait for a few hours. The third group is nice to have: things you can switch off during a cut without losing money.

For a cold room owner in Mile 12, the freezer is essential. The lights and the fan are useful. The television is nice to have. For a hair salon in Lekki, the clippers, the dryer, and the lights over the chairs are essential. The air conditioner is useful. The music system can wait. For a pharmacy in Surulere, the fridge that holds vaccines and insulin is essential, along with the lights, the fan, and the point-of-sale terminal. The television in the waiting area is not.

Write the list on paper and stick it near your switchboard. When power goes off, everyone in the shop should know what stays on and what goes off. That one habit alone can save you fuel, protect your generator, and stop arguments during a busy day. Adaeze, who runs a frozen food shop in Oshodi, used to run her generator with everything switched on. When she separated her loads, she found two of her four freezers were nearly empty most days. She now runs only the two full freezers and one light during a cut, and she moves stock into the full freezers first. Her fuel use dropped sharply, and her stock still stays frozen.

## Know Your Real Power Needs Before You Buy Anything

Before you buy a generator, an inverter, or solar panels, you need to know how much power your essential items actually use. This is measured in watts. Every appliance has a watt rating on a label at the back or in its manual. Add up the watts of the essential items you want to run at the same time. That total tells you the minimum size you need.

A small freezer may use 150 to 250 watts when running. A fan uses 60 to 80 watts. An LED bulb uses 10 to 15 watts. A laptop uses 45 to 65 watts. A point-of-sale terminal and a small printer use very little. A hair dryer, on the other hand, can use 1,500 to 2,000 watts. A water pump can use 750 watts or more when it starts. An air conditioner can use 1,000 watts or more. These big items are the ones that kill a small backup system.

If your essential load is 800 watts, a 1,000-watt generator is not enough, because appliances draw extra power when they start. You need headroom. A good rule is to buy a backup system that can handle at least 25 percent more than your normal essential load. If you are not sure, ask the seller to test the system with your actual appliances before you pay, or ask an electrician to measure your load with a clamp meter. Do not buy backup power on guesswork. It is cheaper to spend a little time measuring than to buy the wrong system twice.

## Choose the Right Backup: Generator, Inverter, or Solar

Most Nigerian small businesses need a mix, not a single solution. Each type of backup has a strength and a weakness. The right choice depends on your business, your budget, and how long your power cuts usually last.

A petrol generator is the most common choice. It is relatively cheap to buy, it can be repaired almost anywhere, and it can run heavy loads if it is big enough. Its weakness is fuel. Petrol prices change, fuel queues waste time, and a generator that runs all day can eat your profit. Generators are also noisy, they produce fumes, and they need regular servicing: oil changes, spark plugs, and cleaning. Never run a generator inside a shop or in a closed room. The fumes can kill. Keep it outside, in a ventilated place, away from customers and stock.

An inverter with batteries is quiet, clean, and automatic. When public power goes off, the inverter switches over in seconds. It is excellent for lights, fans, laptops, phones, point-of-sale terminals, routers, televisions, and small fridges. It is not good for heavy loads like air conditioners, water pumps, and big dryers unless you buy a very large and expensive system. Batteries also wear out. A good battery may last two to four years if it is used properly. A battery that is drained completely every day will die much faster. Ask the seller how long the system will run your essential load, and test that claim on the first day.

Solar panels with an inverter and batteries are the most reliable long-term option in Nigeria because we have strong sunshine in most parts of the country. The upfront cost is higher than a generator, but the running cost is very low. Solar is excellent for shops that need steady light power through the day: phone charging shops, pharmacies, small offices, tailoring shops, and provision stores. It is less suitable as a sole backup for a cold room with several large freezers unless the system is sized properly and the panels have enough roof space. Get a proper site assessment before you pay for solar. A system that is too small will disappoint you, and a system that is too big will waste money you could have used for stock.

## Protect Your Cold Stock First

If you sell frozen food, drinks, ice cream, meat, fish, milk, yoghurt, vaccines, or any product that needs cold storage, power cuts are a direct threat to your stock. A freezer that stays closed can keep food cold for several hours, but a freezer that is opened every few minutes loses cold fast.

Keep your freezers full. A full freezer stays cold longer than a half-empty one because the frozen items help keep each other cold. If you do not have enough stock to fill a freezer, fill plastic bottles with water, freeze them, and keep them inside as ice blocks. They act as a cold reserve and they cost almost nothing.

Do not open the freezer unless you must. When a customer wants something, open it once, take out what is needed, and close it immediately. Train your staff to do the same. Put a sign on the freezer if it helps: "Please ask the attendant, do not open and leave open." That small discipline can save thousands of naira in spoiled stock.

## Keep Your Phones, Payments, and Internet Working

In modern Nigerian business, power is not only about lights. It is about phones, payments, and internet. If your phone is dead, customers cannot reach you on WhatsApp. If your point-of-sale terminal is dead, you cannot take card payments. If your router is off, you cannot confirm transfers. Many sales are lost during power cuts not because the shop is dark, but because the business cannot communicate or take payment.

Charge everything before the cut. Keep a power bank fully charged at all times, and keep a second one as backup. Power banks are cheap compared with a lost sale. Charge your phone, your point-of-sale terminal, and your power banks every night and every morning when public power is on. Do not wait until the battery is low.

Put your router and your point-of-sale terminal on your inverter if you have one. They use very little power, and keeping them on means you can still confirm transfers, print receipts, and stay online. If you do not have an inverter, a small power bank can keep a router running for several hours with the right cable. Ask a technician to set this up for you properly.

Keep a written record of payments during a cut. If your network is slow, do not assume a transfer has failed. Ask the customer to show you the debit alert or the transaction receipt on their phone. Write down the name, amount, and time. If you cannot confirm immediately, take the customer's phone number and confirm as soon as the network returns. Do not release expensive goods on an unconfirmed transfer. At the same time, do not accuse a genuine customer of lying because your own network is down. A calm process protects both sides.

## Serve Customers Well When the Lights Go Out

Customers understand power cuts. They live with them too. What they do not forgive is being ignored, being kept in the dark about what is happening, or being treated as if the cut is their problem. How you talk to customers during a cut matters as much as your backup power.

Tell customers what is happening and what you can still do. A simple sentence helps: "We are on backup power now, so we can still serve you, but the air conditioner is off. Please bear with us." Customers will stay if they know you are still working. They will leave if they stand in a dark shop with no explanation.

Keep the shop safe and comfortable. Open doors and windows for ventilation if you are running a generator outside. Use rechargeable lamps and emergency lights so customers can see products and prices. Keep walkways clear. If the shop is hot, offer water if you can. Small courtesies keep people patient.

## Cut Your Fuel Bill Without Cutting Your Service

Fuel is often the biggest cost of backup power. A generator that runs all day can consume more profit than the sales it protects. You need to control fuel use deliberately, not just hope it will be enough.

Run the generator in blocks, not all day. For many businesses, two hours on and one hour off is enough to keep freezers cold and to serve customers, if the freezers stay closed during the off period. Test this pattern and watch your stock temperature. If the temperature stays safe, you have found a pattern that saves fuel.

## Build Simple Habits and a Written Plan

Backup power works best when it is a habit, not a panic response. The businesses that cope best have a short written plan that anyone in the shop can follow. The plan does not need to be long. One page is enough.

Your plan should answer five questions. What goes off first when power cuts? Who starts the generator or switches the inverter? How long do we run backup power before we close or reduce service? Who do we call if the generator fails? What do we tell customers? Write the answers down and keep the sheet where staff can see it.

Train your staff. Show them how to start the generator safely, how to switch loads, how to check the freezer temperature, and how to record payments when the network is slow. Do not assume they know. A staff member who panics during a cut can damage equipment or lose a sale. A staff member who knows the plan can keep the shop running while you are away.

## What to Do Next

1. List every electrical item in your shop and divide it into essential, useful, and nice-to-have, then stick the list near your switchboard.
2. Add up the watts of your essential items and check whether your current backup can handle that load plus headroom.
3. Decide your backup mix: generator, inverter, solar, or a combination, based on your real load and your budget.
4. Protect your cold stock: keep freezers full, keep them closed, use frozen water bottles as a cold reserve, and check temperatures daily.
5. Charge your phone, power banks, and point-of-sale terminal every day, and put your router on backup power so payments and WhatsApp keep working.
6. Write a one-page power-cut plan, train your staff on it, and keep the phone numbers of your technician and electrician where everyone can see them.
7. Track your fuel use and costs for one month, then adjust your generator hours so staying open during a cut still makes a profit.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How long can a freezer stay cold without power?**
A full freezer that stays closed can keep food frozen for several hours, often four to eight hours depending on the size, the amount of stock, and the temperature outside. A half-empty freezer warms up faster. Keep the door closed, keep the freezer full, and use frozen water bottles as a cold reserve to extend the safe time.

**Is solar better than a generator for a small shop?**
Solar is better for steady, low-power needs like lights, fans, laptops, and phones, because the running cost is very low after installation. A generator is better for short, heavy loads like a large freezer or a water pump. Many small businesses do best with a combination: solar and inverter for daily use, and a generator for heavy loads when needed.

**How can I reduce my generator fuel costs?**
Reduce your load first by switching off non-essential items. Run the generator in blocks instead of all day, keep freezers closed during off periods, service the generator regularly, and track how many hours each litre gives you. If fuel costs are higher than the profit from staying open, it is better to close for a short period and reopen when power returns.

**What should I do if my generator fails during a busy day?**
Stay calm and tell customers what is happening. Move cold stock to a working freezer if you can, keep phones and payments working with power banks, and call your technician immediately. If you have a relationship with a nearby trader, ask if you can store urgent stock with them for a fee. After the crisis, find out why the generator failed and fix the cause so it does not happen again.

**Can I run my whole shop on an inverter?**
You can run lights, fans, laptops, phones, routers, televisions, and small appliances on an inverter if it is sized properly. You cannot run heavy loads like air conditioners, water pumps, and large dryers on a small inverter. Ask an electrician to measure your load before you buy, and do not believe a seller who promises that a small inverter can run everything.

**How do I keep taking payments during a power cut?**
Keep your phone and point-of-sale terminal charged, keep a power bank ready, and put your router on backup power if possible. Record payments carefully when the network is slow, ask customers to show debit alerts, and confirm transfers as soon as the network returns. Do not release expensive goods on an unconfirmed transfer.
