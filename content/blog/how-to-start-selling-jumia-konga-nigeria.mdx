Every day, thousands of Nigerians open Jumia or Konga to buy phones, shoes, kitchen items, baby clothes and electronics. They search, compare prices, read reviews, and pay — often without ever visiting Instagram or WhatsApp. If you sell physical products and you are not on at least one of these marketplaces, you are invisible to a large group of buyers who are ready to spend.

Selling on Jumia and Konga is not magic, and it is not free money. Both platforms charge commission on each sale, they have rules about product quality and delivery times, and competition is fierce. But they solve the hardest problem for a small seller: finding customers. Instead of paying for adverts and hoping someone finds your page, you put your products where buyers are already searching.

This guide walks you through the whole process: choosing between Jumia and Konga, registering as a seller, preparing your products and photos, writing listings that convert, pricing for profit after fees, handling fulfilment and returns, and growing from your first ten orders to a steady income. Everything here is written for a Nigerian seller starting with modest capital, a phone, and products they can source reliably.

## Jumia or Konga — or Both?

Start by understanding the difference. Jumia is the larger marketplace in Nigeria, with more traffic, more product categories, and a bigger advertising system. It also has more sellers competing in popular categories like phones, fashion and electronics, and its commission and fulfilment fees can be higher. Konga is smaller but still significant, with a strong presence in Lagos and Abuja, its own logistics arm, and a reputation among buyers for electronics and home appliances. Konga also runs physical stores, which gives some buyers extra confidence.

For most new sellers, the practical answer is to start on one platform, learn the system, and add the second once your first store is running smoothly. If your products are fashion, beauty, home items or general goods, Jumia's larger traffic usually gives you faster first sales. If you sell electronics, appliances or higher-value items and you are based in Lagos, Konga is a strong starting point. If you have the time to manage two listings, selling on both doubles your visibility and protects you if one platform changes its fees or policies.

## What You Need Before You Register

Registration is straightforward, but it goes faster if you prepare your documents first. Both platforms will ask for similar things, and having them ready avoids a rejected application and a week of back and forth.

You will need:

1. A business name. This can be your registered business name if you have a CAC certificate, or your trading name if you are starting as an individual seller. A clear, memorable shop name matters — buyers remember "Ada's Kitchen Store" more easily than a string of random words.
2. A valid means of identification. Your National Identity Number slip or card, international passport, driver's licence or voter's card. Make sure the name on the ID matches the name on your seller account.
3. A bank account in the seller's name. This is where your sales payouts go. Use a business account if you have one. If you use a personal account, keep it separate from your everyday spending so you can track marketplace income clearly.
4. A phone number and email address you actually check. Order notifications, payout alerts and customer messages arrive here. A number you rarely answer will cost you sales and lower your seller rating.
5. Your CAC documents if you are registering as a business. A sole proprietor can often start with an ID and a bank account, but a registered business with a CAC certificate and a Tax Identification Number looks more credible and unlocks some seller programmes. If you have not registered your business yet, you can still start; upgrade your account when your CAC registration is complete.
6. Product photos and a price list. You cannot list products without clear photos and prices, so prepare these before you register, not after.

## Step 1: Create Your Seller Account

For Jumia, go to the Jumia seller centre website and click on the option to become a seller. You will create an account with your email and phone number, then fill in your shop details: shop name, business type, address, and bank details. Jumia will ask you to upload your ID and, if applicable, your CAC documents. Read the seller agreement carefully, especially the sections on commission, returns and prohibited items. Submit the application and wait for approval, which usually takes one to three working days if your documents are clear.

For Konga, go to the Konga seller page and follow a similar process. Create an account, provide your business and bank details, upload your ID and CAC documents, and choose your store name. Konga may also ask for your product categories and your warehouse or pickup address. Approval times are similar — a few working days.

While you wait, prepare your first ten listings — titles, descriptions, prices and photos — so you can list the same day approval comes.

## Step 2: Choose Products That Actually Sell

The biggest mistake new sellers make is listing what they like instead of what buyers search for. On a marketplace, you are not building a brand first. You are answering a search. A buyer types "wireless earbuds under 15000" or "ankara fabric 6 yards" into the search bar. If your product matches that search and your price and reviews are competitive, you get the sale.

Start with products that meet four tests:

- Demand: people are already searching for them. Check the marketplace itself — search your product idea and see how many sellers and reviews appear. Many reviews mean demand, but also competition. Few reviews with steady sales can mean an opening.
- Reliable supply: you can restock the same product at a similar cost. A product you can only source once will give you one sale and then a dead listing. Build relationships with two suppliers where possible, so one stockout does not stop your shop.
- Sensible size and weight: small, light products are cheaper to ship and less likely to be damaged. A phone case ships for far less than a standing fan, and the return cost is lower too.
- A margin after fees: the price must cover your cost, commission, delivery contribution, packaging, and still leave a profit. Test this with real numbers before you list anything.

Good starter categories for Nigerian sellers include phone accessories, beauty and skincare, fashion items like sneakers and handbags, kitchen tools, baby items, Ankara and ready-to-wear clothing, and small home appliances. Avoid categories you do not understand. If you cannot tell a genuine product from a fake, do not sell it — a single counterfeit complaint can close your shop.

Start with ten to twenty products, not two hundred. Ten well-photographed, well-described listings will outsell a hundred rushed ones. Once the first ten are selling, add more in the same category so your shop starts to look like a specialist store, not a random collection.

## Step 3: Take Photos That Sell

On Jumia and Konga, your photo is your shop window. Buyers cannot touch the product, so the photo has to do all the convincing. Poor photos are the single biggest reason a good product does not sell.

Use a clean, plain background. A white wall, a white bedsheet, or a plain table works well. Natural daylight near a window is better than a yellow bulb at night. Take photos from several angles: front, back, side, close-up of important details, and the product in use or being worn where that makes sense. For clothing, a photo on a person or a mannequin sells far better than a flat photo on a bed.

Make sure the photo shows the true colour. If a dress is wine and not red, the buyer must see wine. Wrong colours are a leading cause of returns in Nigeria, and returns cost you money and hurt your seller rating. If a product comes in several colours, photograph each colour separately and upload them as variants, not as a single confusing image.

Keep your phone steady and clean the lens before you shoot. Use the marketplace app or a free editing app to crop the image, brighten it slightly, and remove clutter from the background. Do not add heavy filters that change the colour, and do not put your WhatsApp number or "DM for price" on the image — marketplaces prohibit directing buyers off the platform, and the listing can be removed.

## Step 4: Write Listings That Convert

A listing has three parts that matter: the title, the description, and the price. Each one has a job.

The title should contain the words a buyer actually searches for. "Men's Leather Sneakers — Black, Size 42" works better than "Quality Sneakers for Men!!!" Include the brand where relevant, the key feature, the colour, and the size or capacity. Avoid shouting with capital letters and exclamation marks. Marketplaces rank clear, keyword-rich titles higher in search, and buyers trust them more.

The description should answer the questions a buyer would ask before paying: What is it made of? What is in the box? What sizes or colours are available? How do I use or care for it? Write in short paragraphs or bullet points. Be honest about what the product is and what it is not. If a power bank is 10,000mAh, say so. If a wig is synthetic, say so. Honest descriptions reduce returns and build reviews that help your next sale.

Include the key details in the specification fields the platform provides: brand, model, colour, size, material, weight, and warranty where applicable. Fill in every relevant field. Listings with complete specifications rank better and get fewer questions from buyers.

Set your price after fees, not before them. Work it out like this: start with your cost price, add packaging and your share of delivery, add the platform commission for your category, and then add your profit. If a phone case costs you ₦2,500, packaging is ₦200, commission is around 10 percent, and you want ₦1,000 profit, your selling price needs to be around ₦4,100 to ₦4,200. Price below that and you are working for the platform. Check competitor prices for the same product, but do not copy the cheapest seller if their price leaves you no profit. Compete on photos, description and reviews, not only on price.

## Step 5: Understand Fees, Commissions and Payouts

Fees are where many new sellers lose money without realising it. Read the fee schedule for your category before you list, and check it again every quarter because platforms change it.

The main costs are:

- Commission: a percentage of the selling price, deducted on every completed sale. It varies by category — electronics often attract a lower percentage than fashion or beauty. Know your category rate exactly.
- Fulfilment or delivery fee: charged when the platform handles delivery, or the cost you pay a dispatch rider if you deliver yourself. Factor it into your price.
- Payment processing: already included in most commission structures, but check whether pay-on-delivery orders carry an extra handling charge.
- Advertising: optional, but many sellers use sponsored listings to appear at the top of search results. Start with a small daily budget, such as ₦1,000 to ₦2,000, test it on your best product, and stop it if it does not produce sales.
- Returns: when a buyer returns a product, you usually bear the return delivery cost, and the commission may or may not be refunded depending on the reason. Price with a small allowance for returns — a realistic rate is 3 to 5 percent of orders in fashion, lower in electronics.

Payouts do not arrive the day a customer orders. The platform holds the money until delivery is confirmed and the return window passes, then pays into your bank account on a weekly or twice-weekly cycle. Plan your cash flow around this delay. Do not spend money from an order until the payout has actually landed. Keep a simple record of orders, payouts and fees in a spreadsheet so you can see your true profit each week.

## Step 6: Fulfilment — Getting Orders to Buyers on Time

When an order arrives, speed matters. The platform gives you a short window to confirm and hand the item to the courier. Late dispatch lowers your seller rating, and a low rating pushes your listings down in search results.

Set up a simple fulfilment routine. Keep your best-selling products packed or ready to pack. Store them in labelled boxes or bags so you can find the right size and colour in a minute. Keep packaging materials in stock: mailer bags, boxes, bubble wrap, tape, and a thank-you card if you use one. Weigh and measure your packed products once, and save the figures, so you are not guessing delivery costs for each order.

If you use the platform's logistics, drop off orders at the nearest hub before the daily cut-off time. Find out that cut-off time on your first day and build your day around it. If you deliver yourself within Lagos or Abuja, use a reliable dispatch rider and confirm the delivery with the buyer on WhatsApp after the platform confirms it. Never ask a buyer to pay you directly outside the platform, and never cancel an order and ask the buyer to reorder. Both actions can get your account suspended.

## Step 7: Handling Returns and Difficult Customers

Returns are part of marketplace selling. A buyer may receive the wrong size, change their mind, or receive a damaged item. How you handle the return matters more than the return itself.

Accept that returns will happen, and build them into your pricing and your attitude. When a return request arrives, respond politely and quickly, follow the platform's return process exactly, and inspect the returned item the day it arrives. If the item is in good condition, restock it. If it is damaged, photograph it and keep the evidence for any dispute.

Never argue with a buyer in the platform's message system. Keep messages short, factual and polite. "We are sorry the item did not fit. Please follow the return steps and we will process a refund once the item is received" resolves the issue and protects your rating. A rude message can be screenshotted and reported.

## Step 8: Growing From Your First Sales to a Steady Shop

Your first ten orders are the hardest. After that, focus on the numbers that actually move your shop: your seller rating, your response rate, your delivery speed, and your review count.

Ask every satisfied buyer for a review, in the way the platform allows. A short message after delivery — "Thank you for your order. If you are happy with the product, a review would really help our small shop" — is enough. Do not offer payment for reviews, and do not write fake reviews for your own products. Platforms detect this, and one penalty can erase months of work.

Reinvest your early profits into more stock of your best sellers, not into ten new product lines. Look at your seller dashboard each week and identify your top three products by sales and profit. Keep those in stock, improve their photos, and consider a small advertising budget for them. Drop products that have not sold in 60 days, or cut their price to clear them and free up cash.

## Your First-Week Checklist

Use this checklist in your first week as a seller:

1. Register on your chosen platform with clear documents and correct bank details.
2. Photograph your first ten products in daylight on a plain background.
3. Write clear titles and honest descriptions, and set prices after fees.
4. List the ten products and check that each one appears correctly in search.
5. Prepare packaging materials and confirm your drop-off hub and cut-off time.
6. Set a daily routine: check orders in the morning, dispatch before cut-off, reply to messages in the evening.
7. Record every order, fee and payout in a simple spreadsheet from day one.

Selling on Jumia and Konga rewards sellers who are organised, honest and consistent. Start small, learn the fees, protect your rating, and grow one product at a time. Your first sale will teach you more than any guide can — list your first ten products this week and let the marketplace start working for you.
