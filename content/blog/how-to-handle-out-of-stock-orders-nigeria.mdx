Nothing tests a small business in Nigeria quite like a customer who is ready to pay for something you no longer have. A dress in size 12 sells out on Instagram before you update your highlights. A food vendor runs out of chicken at 2 pm on a busy Saturday. A phone accessories shop in Computer Village sells the last power bank, then gets three more WhatsApp orders for the same item within the hour. A skincare brand in Lekki restocks on Monday, and by Wednesday the bestseller is gone.

Stockouts are not a sign that your business is failing. In many cases, they are a sign that demand is strong. What damages a business is not the empty shelf. It is the silence after the order, the slow reply, the vague promise, and the refund that takes days to arrive. Customers in Nigeria have many options. If buying from you feels stressful, they will buy from the next vendor on their timeline, and they will remember the experience.

This guide shows you how to handle out-of-stock orders in a way that protects your reputation, keeps the money in your business where possible, and turns a disappointed customer into a repeat buyer. It covers what to do in the first minutes after you discover a stockout, what to say, what alternatives to offer, how to run pre-orders properly, how to handle refunds without drama, and how to build simple inventory habits that reduce stockouts over time.

## Why Stockouts Hurt More in Nigeria

In Nigeria, trust is the currency of small business. Most customers buy from Instagram, WhatsApp, TikTok, or a small website they found through Google. They cannot walk into your shop to see the product. They send money based on photos, reviews, and your replies. When that trust is broken by a stockout handled badly, the damage spreads fast.

A customer who pays ₦25,000 for a wig, a pair of shoes, or a food tray and then hears nothing for two days will assume the worst. Scam stories are common, and every delayed reply feeds that fear. Even when you are honest and hardworking, a slow response looks the same as a scam from the customer's side of the screen.

There is also the social cost. A disappointed customer in Lagos or Abuja does not just quietly move on. They tell their sister, their group chat, and their followers. A single screenshot of a rude or careless reply can travel far. On the other hand, a customer whose stockout was handled with speed, honesty, and a good alternative often becomes your loudest supporter. People love to share stories of businesses that treated them well when things went wrong.

Stockouts also cost you real money. You lose the sale. You may lose the delivery fee you already quoted. You spend time answering angry messages instead of selling. If you refund slowly, the customer may open a dispute with their bank or payment provider. All of this is avoidable with a clear process that you follow every time.

## Step 1: Find Out Fast — Before the Customer Finds Out First

The worst way for a customer to learn that an item is out of stock is to discover it themselves. That happens when they pay and wait, when they ask for a delivery update and you go quiet, or when they arrive at your pickup point and the item is not there.

Build a habit of checking stock before you confirm any order. This sounds obvious, but many Nigerian sellers confirm first and check later, especially during busy periods like December, Black Friday, Valentine's Day, or a viral TikTok moment. During a rush, slow down for ten seconds and verify.

If you sell on WhatsApp, keep a simple stock sheet. It can be a notebook, a Google Sheet, or a note on your phone. Update it every time something sells. If you have staff, make one person responsible for updating stock at the end of each day. If you sell on a website, use a platform that reduces stock automatically when an order comes in. Paystack Storefront, Flutterwave Store, and most e-commerce website setups do this if you enter the correct quantity at the start.

When you receive a new order, follow this order of checks. First, confirm the exact variant the customer wants — size, colour, flavour, or model. Many stockouts are not about the product in general, but about one specific variant. Second, check physical stock, not just what your Instagram caption says. Third, check if a supplier can supply it quickly, and at what price, before you promise anything.

If you discover a stockout after the customer has already paid, act within the hour. Do not wait until the next morning. Do not wait until you have found a replacement. Send a message immediately. Speed is the single biggest factor in whether the customer stays calm.

## Step 2: Tell the Customer Quickly, Honestly, and With a Solution

Your first message after a stockout sets the tone for everything that follows. Customers forgive a stockout. They rarely forgive being ignored or misled.

A good stockout message has four parts. Apologise briefly. State the fact plainly. Offer a clear choice. Give a time for your next update.

Here is a template you can adapt for WhatsApp:

> Hello Ada, thank you so much for your order. I am sorry, but the black size 38 sandals you ordered just sold out this morning before I could update the page. I completely understand this is disappointing. I can offer you the same sandals in brown size 38, which is available now, or I can order the black pair for you and deliver on Thursday. If neither works, I will refund you in full today. Please let me know which you prefer. Thank you for your patience.

Notice what this message does. It does not blame the customer. It does not say "we are out of stock" without context. It gives the customer control. It promises a same-day refund if they choose that option. It also gives a specific delivery day for the reorder, not a vague "soon."

What not to say matters just as much. Avoid "Sorry for the inconvenience" with no follow-up. Avoid "We will get back to you" with no time. Avoid telling the customer to wait while you "confirm from supplier" for three days. Avoid offering a completely different product at a higher price without explaining the price difference. And never tell a customer the item is available when you know it is not, hoping a supplier will save you. That hope turns a small problem into a refund, a dispute, and a bad review.

If the customer is angry, do not match their energy. Stay calm, polite, and specific. Repeat the options. Confirm the refund timeline in writing. Many customers calm down once they see you are not hiding.

## Step 3: Offer Alternatives That Actually Help

An alternative is only useful if it solves the customer's real need. Offering a random product because you want to keep the sale will frustrate them further.

Think about why the customer wanted the item. Was it the colour, the size, the price, the brand, or the timing? A customer who wanted a red dress for a wedding on Saturday does not want a blue dress for next week. A customer who wanted a 20,000 mAh power bank does not want a 10,000 mAh one at the same price. A customer who wanted a particular skin cream because it suits sensitive skin does not want a different cream with fragrance.

Good alternatives follow three rules. First, they are close in price. If the alternative costs more, say so clearly and offer to cover the difference or give a discount. If it costs less, refund the difference immediately. Second, they are available now, or you can state an exact delivery date. Third, they respect the customer's original purpose.

Here are practical alternatives that work for Nigerian small businesses:

- **Same product, different variant:** Offer another colour, size, or flavour at the same price. Send clear photos and confirm before you send it out.
- **Upgrade at no extra cost:** If you have a slightly better model in stock, offer it for the same price. The small loss protects the relationship and often leads to a repeat order.
- **Bundle instead of a single item:** If the exact item is gone, offer a bundle that includes a similar item plus a small extra, like free delivery. Make sure the customer agrees before you charge or ship.
- **Pre-order with a clear date:** Offer to source the exact item and deliver on a specific day. Only offer this if you are sure your supplier can deliver. Add a small discount or free delivery as a thank-you for waiting.
- **Store credit with a bonus:** Offer store credit worth slightly more than the order value, for example 10 percent extra, if the customer is happy to shop again.

Always let the customer choose. Do not decide for them and ship a replacement without asking. That is the fastest way to get a "this is not what I ordered" message and a request for a return.

## Step 4: Run Pre-Orders the Right Way

Pre-orders are one of the best tools for handling stockouts in Nigeria, but only when they are run honestly. A pre-order means the customer pays now, or pays a deposit, for an item you will supply later. Done well, it helps you plan stock, reduce waste, and serve more customers. Done badly, it looks like you collected money for goods you never had.

Set clear pre-order rules and share them before the customer pays. State the item, the variant, the price, the deposit amount, the balance due date, and the exact delivery window. For example: "Pre-order closes Friday. Delivery is Tuesday to Thursday next week in Lagos, and Friday in Abuja and Port Harcourt."

Take a deposit, not always full payment, when the wait is longer than a few days. A 50 percent deposit is common and fair. It shows commitment from the customer and protects you if they change their mind. For high-value items, like a ₦180,000 phone or a ₦350,000 lace fabric, a deposit also reduces your risk if the customer disappears.

Keep a pre-order list separate from your regular orders. Write down the customer's name, phone number, item, amount paid, balance, and promised date. Send a reminder two days before delivery. On delivery day, send a message in the morning confirming that the item is ready. If there is a delay, inform the customer before the promised date, not after.

Never use pre-order money to cover other business expenses and then struggle to buy the stock. Keep pre-order funds in a separate account or wallet if you can. Moniepoint, Opay, and a separate business bank account can help you ring-fence this money. If you mix it with daily spending, you will find yourself short when it is time to order from your supplier.

If a pre-order item becomes unavailable from your supplier, inform the customer immediately and offer a full refund plus the choice of an alternative. Do not keep the customer waiting while you search for another supplier for weeks.

## Step 5: Handle Refunds Fast and Without Drama

Refunds are where many Nigerian small businesses lose customers permanently. A customer who waits five days for a refund will not order again, even if the product was good. A customer who gets a refund the same day will often order again next week.

Make your refund policy clear before the customer pays. Put it in your Instagram bio link, your WhatsApp Business catalogue, and your website. A simple policy works best: "If your item is out of stock after payment, you can choose a replacement, a pre-order, or a full refund the same day."

When a customer chooses a refund, process it the same day. If you received payment by bank transfer, send it back to the same account and share the receipt. If you received payment through Paystack or Flutterwave, use the refund function in your dashboard so the customer gets an email confirmation. Do not ask the customer to send their account details three times. You already have them from the payment.

If your bank has a delay, tell the customer honestly and give them proof, like a screenshot of the transfer. Do not say "I have sent it" when you have not. In Nigeria, customers check their alerts quickly, and a false claim destroys trust instantly.

Build a small float for refunds so you are never stuck. Keep ₦50,000 to ₦100,000 aside in your business account for refunds and small emergencies. It is not profit to spend. It is a trust fund for your customers. If a customer paid for delivery and you cannot fulfil the order, refund the delivery fee too. Keeping a ₦3,500 delivery fee for an order you did not deliver feels unfair, and customers remember it.

## Step 6: Fix the Cause — Simple Inventory Habits That Prevent Repeat Stockouts

Handling a stockout well is important. Preventing the next one is even better. You do not need expensive software to manage inventory as a small business. You need consistent habits.

Start with a reorder point for your bestsellers. A reorder point is the stock level at which you order more. If you sell five units of a cream every week, and your supplier takes three days to deliver, reorder when you have seven units left, not when you have one. Write the reorder point next to each product in your stock sheet.

Track your bestsellers weekly. Every Sunday evening, spend twenty minutes reviewing what sold out, what sold slowly, and what you need to order on Monday morning. This short review prevents most Monday morning surprises. If you sell in a physical shop in Lekki, Ikeja, or Onitsha, do the same check before you open on Monday.

Separate fast movers from slow movers. Fast movers are the items that sell every week. Keep more of them. Slow movers tie up your money. Do not reorder them in large quantities just because a supplier offers a discount. That discount is useless if the stock sits for three months.

Use your phone to keep stock visible. A Google Sheet with columns for product, variant, opening stock, sold, remaining, and reorder point is enough for most small businesses. Update it daily. If you have staff, make stock updates part of closing duties, like counting cash. If you sell on Instagram and WhatsApp, update your highlights and catalogue the same day an item sells out. Mark it "Sold Out" clearly. Customers would rather see "Sold Out" than order and be disappointed.

Build relationships with two suppliers for your key products, not just one. If your main supplier in Lagos is out of stock, or increases the price suddenly, a backup supplier in Onitsha, Aba, or Abuja can save you. Keep their numbers saved and check prices regularly. During festive periods, order earlier than usual. December demand in Nigeria is different from February demand. Plan for it.

If you run a website, use low-stock alerts. Many website platforms can send you an email when stock falls below a number you set. If you sell through WhatsApp Business, use the catalogue feature and update quantities there too.

## Final Thoughts

Every growing business in Nigeria will run out of stock at some point. It is a normal part of selling products people want. What separates businesses that grow from businesses that shrink is how they respond in that moment.

Respond fast. Be honest. Give the customer a real choice. Refund quickly if they want a refund. Learn from the stockout and fix the inventory habit that caused it. Follow up, and turn a disappointed buyer into a loyal one. Customers do not expect you to be perfect. They expect you to be reachable, honest, and fair. If you can do those three things consistently, a stockout becomes a small bump, not a lost customer.

Start today by checking your stock, updating your sold-out items, and setting reorder points for your top five products. Your future self, and your customers, will thank you.
