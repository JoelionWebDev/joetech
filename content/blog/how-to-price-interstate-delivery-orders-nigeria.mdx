A customer in Abuja orders a ₦45,000 dress from your Lagos shop. You quote ₦5,000 for delivery because that is what you charge in Lagos. The courier charges ₦9,500. You have just lost ₦4,500 on a sale you celebrated. A week later, a customer in Port Harcourt orders food items worth ₦30,000. You add ₦7,000 for waybill, but the park charges ₦6,000 plus ₦1,500 for handling, and the customer refuses to pay the balance on arrival. You absorb it.

This is how many Nigerian small businesses lose money on interstate orders without realising it. Delivery feels like a small detail next to the product price, but across ten or twenty orders a month, a ₦3,000 loss per order becomes ₦30,000 to ₦60,000 gone. That is money that should have been profit, restock money, or savings.

Pricing interstate delivery correctly is not about charging the highest fee you can. It is about knowing your true cost, choosing the right delivery method for each order, and communicating the price clearly before the customer pays. This guide walks you through that process step by step, with real Nigerian examples, courier and waybill options, and a simple pricing formula you can use today.

## First, Know Your True Delivery Cost

Most delivery losses start with guessing. A seller guesses ₦5,000 because it sounds reasonable, or copies a competitor's fee without knowing what that competitor pays. Your cost is specific to your product, your location, and your courier.

Your true delivery cost has several parts. The courier or transport fare is the biggest one. Then there is packaging: a sturdy box, bubble wrap, tape, and a branded bag can cost ₦800 to ₦2,500 per order, depending on the product. Fragile items like glass, cakes, or electronics need extra protection, and that costs more. There is also the cost of getting the parcel to the park or courier office — bike fare, fuel, or your own time. If you pay a dispatch rider ₦1,500 to take parcels to the park in Lagos, that is part of the cost.

Do not forget failed deliveries and returns. If a customer in Enugu does not pick up a waybill parcel, the park may charge storage, and you may pay again to bring it back to Lagos. If a courier attempts delivery twice in Kano and the customer is unavailable, you may be charged for a second attempt. Build a small buffer for these cases, even if it is only a few hundred naira per order.

To find your true cost, track ten interstate orders. For each one, write down the destination, the courier or park used, the weight, the fare charged, packaging cost, and any extra fee. After ten orders, you will see a pattern. Lagos to Abuja by road courier may average ₦7,000 to ₦10,000 for a small parcel. Lagos to Port Harcourt may be similar or slightly higher. Lagos to Kano or Maiduguri will cost more because of distance. Use your own records, not a guess, to set your prices.

## The Simple Formula: Product Price Plus Delivery, Never Mixed

Keep product price and delivery fee separate in your mind and in your quote. When you mix them, you hide a loss. A dress that costs you ₦28,000 to buy and sells for ₦45,000 makes ₦17,000 before delivery. If delivery costs ₦9,000 and you charge only ₦5,000, your real profit is ₦13,000, not ₦17,000. That is still profit, but you need to know the true figure to make decisions about discounts and ads.

Use this simple formula for every interstate order:

**Customer delivery fee = courier fare + packaging + transport to park or office + small buffer**

Set the buffer at ₦500 to ₦1,000 for small parcels, and more for fragile or high-value items. Round the total up to the nearest ₦500 so the price is clean and easy to communicate. For example, if your cost is ₦8,700, charge ₦9,000. If it is ₦6,200, charge ₦6,500.

Never offer free interstate delivery unless you have built the cost into the product price on purpose. Free delivery inside Lagos is common as a promotion, but free delivery to Abuja, Jos, or Uyo on every order will drain your profit. If you want to offer free delivery as a promotion, limit it: free delivery to Abuja on orders above ₦100,000, for one weekend only, or for your first twenty interstate customers. Set the limit in writing before you announce it.

## Choose the Right Delivery Method for Each Order

Nigeria has three main interstate options for small businesses: courier companies, park waybill, and bus waybill through a trusted driver. Each suits different orders.

Courier companies like GIG Logistics, DHL, and local Lagos couriers with interstate routes are best for small, valuable, or fragile parcels that need tracking and doorstep delivery. They cost more, but the customer gets a tracking number and delivery to their address. Use couriers for electronics, skincare, wigs, shoes, and orders above ₦50,000 where the customer expects careful handling.

Park waybill is best for larger, heavier, or less fragile goods: bags of foodstuff, Ankara fabric in bulk, cartons of goods, or items going to a city where the customer can easily pick up from the park. Waybill through parks in Lagos like Iyana Ipaja, Ojota, or Jibowu is often cheaper than a courier for the same weight. A carton that costs ₦12,000 by courier may cost ₦6,000 to ₦7,500 by waybill. The trade-off is that the customer must go to the park to collect it, and tracking is a phone call, not an app.

Bus waybill works well when you have a regular driver or transport company you trust on a specific route, for example Lagos to Onitsha or Lagos to Abuja. It can be fast and affordable, but it depends on trust. Only use a driver you or a fellow seller has used several times. Always get the driver's name, phone number, bus plate number, and a receipt.

For food and perishables, speed matters more than price. A cake, fresh fish, or frozen food going from Lagos to Ibadan needs same-day delivery by road, not a two-day courier. Quote a realistic price for speed, and explain to the customer why it costs more. Most customers will pay for food that arrives fresh.

## Set Delivery Zones and Publish Them

Customers hate surprises. If they only learn the delivery fee after they have chosen a product and are ready to pay, many will abandon the order. Publish your delivery zones and prices where customers can see them before they order.

Create three or four zones based on distance from your base. If you are in Lagos, your zones might look like this:

- **Zone 1 — Lagos and nearby:** Lekki, Ikeja, Surulere, Ajah, plus Ogun towns close to Lagos. Same-day or next-day delivery by rider.
- **Zone 2 — South-West and South-South close cities:** Ibadan, Abeokuta, Benin City, Warri, Port Harcourt. Delivery in one to three days.
- **Zone 3 — Abuja, Enugu, Onitsha, Owerri, Calabar:** Delivery in two to four days.
- **Zone 4 — North and far cities:** Kano, Kaduna, Jos, Maiduguri, Sokoto. Delivery in three to five days, higher fee.

For each zone, set a fee for a small parcel, a medium parcel, and a large parcel. A small parcel might be under 2 kg, like a dress or a phone case. A medium parcel might be 2 to 5 kg. A large parcel might be above 5 kg or bulky, like a carton of shoes. Weigh a few typical orders on a kitchen scale so your categories are real, not guessed.

Put this table in your WhatsApp Business catalogue description, your Instagram highlights under "Delivery," and your website delivery page. Update it when courier prices change. Fuel prices and courier rates change often in Nigeria, so review your zones every two or three months. When you increase a fee, announce it politely and explain briefly: "Due to increased courier charges, our Abuja delivery is now ₦9,000."

## Weight, Size, and Packaging: The Hidden Price Drivers

Two parcels going to the same city can cost very different amounts. A light dress in a small poly bag is cheap to send. The same dress in a large rigid box with tissue paper and a gift bag is bulky and costs more. A pair of shoes in their original box is heavier than a pair folded in a mailer.

Couriers charge by weight or by size, whichever is greater. This is called volumetric weight. A large, light box can be charged as if it were heavy. Learn this rule and pack smart. Use the smallest box that protects the product safely. Do not use a large box for a small item just because it looks premium. If you sell shoes, ask your courier whether removing the shoe box and using a mailer reduces the fee, and tell the customer in advance if you do that.

Weigh your packed parcel before you quote. A ₦2,000 kitchen scale pays for itself in a week. Write the weight on the parcel and in your order book. Over time, you will know without weighing that a particular product in its standard pack weighs about 1.5 kg and costs a certain amount to each zone.

Packaging also protects you from damage claims. A broken bottle of perfume or a crushed cake box going to Abuja will cost you a refund and a customer. Spend on bubble wrap, strong tape, and a "Fragile" label for delicate items. If the item is very fragile, add a handling surcharge of ₦1,000 to ₦2,000 and explain it to the customer. It is cheaper than a refund.

## How to Quote Delivery Without Losing the Sale

The way you present the delivery fee matters as much as the amount. Customers accept a fair fee when they understand it early and see the value.

Quote delivery early. When a customer in Abuja asks "How much is delivery?" do not say "We will confirm." Give the zone price immediately: "Delivery to Abuja is ₦9,000, and it takes two to three days. Your total is ₦54,000." The customer can then decide with full information. If the fee is too high for them, they may choose a cheaper product, a pickup option, or a waybill collection that costs less. That is better than a lost sale after payment.

Offer two options when you can. For example: "Courier to your doorstep in Abuja is ₦9,000, or waybill to the park is ₦5,500 and you pick it up." Many customers will choose the cheaper waybill if they live near the park. Giving a choice makes the higher price feel fair, because the customer chose it.

Do not apologise for a fair delivery fee. Saying "Sorry, delivery is expensive" makes the customer think you are overcharging. Instead, state it plainly and link it to speed and safety: "Delivery to Port Harcourt is ₦8,500 by courier, tracked, and delivered to your door in two days." That is a service, not a penalty.

If a customer complains that delivery is almost as expensive as the product, listen. For low-value orders, interstate delivery can indeed cost more than the product. A ₦5,000 item with ₦7,000 delivery does not make sense for the customer. In that case, suggest they order more items to share the delivery cost, or wait until you have a promo. It is better to lose one small order honestly than to subsidise delivery and lose money.

## Payment Before Dispatch: Protect Yourself on Interstate Orders

Never send an interstate order on the promise that the customer will pay on delivery unless you have a trusted payment-on-delivery arrangement with your courier. Many small businesses have lost goods and delivery fees this way. The customer changes their mind, is not available, or refuses the parcel, and you pay for the return.

For interstate orders, collect payment for the product plus delivery before you dispatch. Use Paystack or Flutterwave payment links so the customer can pay by transfer, card, or bank. Share the link on WhatsApp and confirm payment before you book the courier. For customers who prefer transfer, confirm the alert and the amount before you send the parcel.

If you offer pay-on-delivery through a courier, check the courier's terms. Some couriers remit the money after a few days and charge a fee for the service. Factor that fee and the delay into your cash flow. Do not promise a courier pay-on-delivery option if you have not tested it with a small order first.

Keep proof of dispatch. Take a photo of the parcel, the waybill receipt, and the courier tracking number. Send all three to the customer on WhatsApp the same day. This simple habit prevents most "I did not receive it" disputes. If the parcel is delayed, you can track it and update the customer before they start to worry.

## When Delivery Goes Wrong

Even with good planning, deliveries go wrong. A courier van breaks down. A waybill parcel is delayed at a park. A customer gives the wrong address in Kano. What you do next decides whether you keep the customer.

Track every parcel. Check the tracking status the morning after dispatch and again before the expected delivery day. If there is a delay, message the customer first. Do not wait for them to ask. A short message works: "Hello, your parcel to Enugu is delayed by a day because of the courier's schedule. It should arrive tomorrow. I am monitoring it and will update you."

If a parcel is lost or damaged, act fast. Contact the courier, file a report, and tell the customer what you are doing. Offer a replacement or a refund while the courier investigates, especially if the amount is small. Waiting for the courier to pay compensation can take weeks, and the customer should not wait that long. Choose a courier with a clear compensation policy and keep records of your claims.

If a customer refuses a parcel or is not available, call them before the courier returns it. Many failed deliveries happen because the customer was at work or the phone number was wrong. A quick call can save the delivery fee and the sale.

## Review and Adjust Your Prices Every Quarter

Delivery prices in Nigeria change. Fuel goes up, courier companies adjust rates, and park fees rise. If you set your delivery fees in January and never review them, you will be subsidising deliveries by June.

Set a reminder every three months to check your courier and waybill rates. Call your regular courier and ask for the current price to Abuja, Port Harcourt, Enugu, and Kano for a 2 kg parcel. Check park waybill prices on your busiest routes. Update your zone table and tell your customers if a price has changed.

Also review which routes make you money and which lose money. If you rarely sell to Maiduguri and every order there loses money, consider pausing delivery to that city or charging the full cost plus a buffer. If Abuja orders are frequent and profitable, consider negotiating a bulk rate with a courier. If you send ten parcels a week to Abuja, a courier may offer you a discount. Ask. Many sellers never negotiate, and they pay the walk-in price forever.

Keep a simple delivery log in a notebook or Google Sheet. Record the date, destination, method, cost, fee charged, and profit or loss on delivery. At the end of the month, add it up. If delivery is losing money overall, raise your fees by ₦500 to ₦1,000 per zone. If delivery is making a small profit, keep it as a buffer for failed deliveries. Do not treat delivery profit as business profit to spend. Keep it as a cushion.

## Final Thoughts: Charge Fairly, Deliver Reliably

Interstate delivery does not have to eat your profit. It becomes a problem only when you guess the price, hide the fee until the last minute, or absorb costs you should have charged for. Know your true cost, use a simple formula, choose the right method for each order, publish your zones, and collect payment before dispatch.

Start this week by tracking your next ten interstate orders in detail. Weigh your parcels, record every cost, and compare it with what you charged. You will likely find one or two routes where you are losing money. Fix those first. Then publish your zone prices on WhatsApp and Instagram so customers see them before they order. Clear pricing builds trust, and trust brings repeat orders from Abuja, Port Harcourt, Enugu, and beyond.

A business that delivers reliably across Nigeria can grow far beyond its home city. Price it right, and every interstate order becomes profit, not a lesson in losing money.
