You have probably heard the word API. A developer mentions it in a meeting. A payment company says you need one to accept money on your website. A job advert lists it as a skill. And if you are not a developer, it can sound like one of those words that everyone uses and nobody explains.

Here is the good news: an API is a simple idea, and you already use APIs every single day without noticing. Once you understand it, a lot of technology makes more sense — and you can talk to developers and choose tools for your business with confidence.

## What Is an API, in Plain English?

API stands for Application Programming Interface. Forget the full name — what matters is what it does. An API is a way for two different pieces of software to talk to each other and share information, following agreed rules.

Think of a restaurant. You sit at a table and you want food. You do not walk into the kitchen, open the fridge, and cook it yourself. Instead, you read the menu, you tell the waiter what you want, and the waiter carries your order to the kitchen and brings the food back. The waiter is the middleman who knows how to talk to the kitchen on your behalf.

An API is the waiter. One app (the customer) asks another app (the kitchen) for something, and the API carries the request across, then brings the answer back. The two apps never need to know how each other works inside. They only need to agree on the menu — what can be asked for, and what the answer will look like.

That is all an API is: an agreed way for software to ask for things and get answers.

## Everyday Examples You Already Use

You do not need to look far to find APIs at work in your own life.

**Paying online.** When you buy something on a Nigerian online store and pay with your card, the store does not handle your money itself. It asks a payment company, such as Paystack or Flutterwave, to process the payment. The store sends the amount through an API, the payment company checks the card, and the answer comes back: payment successful, or payment failed. The whole thing takes seconds.

**Maps inside apps.** When a delivery app shows you where your rider is on a map, the app did not build its own map. It asked Google Maps for the map and the location, through an API, and displayed the result inside the app.

**Log in with Google.** When a website offers a "Continue with Google" button, it uses Google's API to confirm who you are. You do not create a new password, and the website never sees your Google password. Google simply tells the website: yes, this is the right person.

Every one of these is two systems talking through an API, with you in the middle enjoying the result.

## How an API Works, Step by Step

Behind the scenes, an API conversation follows a simple pattern:

1. **A request goes out.** One app asks for something specific — for example, a website asks a payment company: "Charge this card ₦25,000."
2. **The other app checks the request.** The payment company checks the card, the amount, and whether the request is allowed.
3. **A response comes back.** The answer is usually short and clear: success or failure, plus useful details such as a transaction reference number.

That is the whole cycle: ask, check, answer. Developers call the specific thing being asked for an "endpoint" — it is simply the address for one item on the menu. The menu itself is called the "documentation", and a good API has clear documentation that explains every request it can handle.

## Why APIs Matter for Your Business

APIs are not just a developer topic. They are the reason small businesses can now use tools that used to belong only to big companies.

**You can accept payments on your website** without building a payment system from scratch. A payment API does the hard work, and your customers pay with card, transfer, or USSD.

**You can connect the tools you already use.** Your website can send new orders to your WhatsApp, your email list, or your accounting sheet automatically, instead of someone copying details by hand.

**You can save hours of manual work.** If your business takes bookings, an API can put them straight into your calendar. If you sell products, it can update your stock the moment an order comes in.

**You can offer a better experience.** A customer who can track a delivery, pay in two clicks, and get an instant receipt is a customer who comes back.

When a developer tells you a feature needs an API, what they are really saying is: this feature connects your website to another service, and that connection has to be set up properly.

## Common Words You Will Hear

A few terms come up again and again around APIs. Here is what they mean in plain language:

- **API key.** A secret password that identifies who is making the request. It proves that your website is allowed to use the service. Treat it like a bank PIN — never share it publicly or paste it into a chat.
- **Documentation.** The instruction manual for an API. It lists everything the API can do and how to ask for it. If a service has poor documentation, developers will struggle to work with it.
- **Rate limit.** A cap on how many requests you can make in a given time. For example, a free plan might allow 100 requests per hour. If your business grows past the limit, you may need a paid plan.
You do not need to memorise these. But recognising them will help you follow conversations with developers and service providers without feeling lost.

## What to Ask Before Paying for an API Service

If someone offers you a service that relies on an API — a payment gateway, an SMS service, a delivery integration — ask these questions first:

- **What does it cost as I grow?** Many APIs are free for small use and charge as volume grows. Understand the pricing before your business depends on it.
- **Is there a Nigerian option?** Local payment and SMS providers often understand Nigerian realities better — bank transfers, USSD, and local networks — and their support is easier to reach.
- **What happens if it goes down?** Every service fails sometimes. Ask what your website will show customers if the connection fails, so they see a clear message instead of a blank page.
## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need to know how to code to use an API?

No. As a business owner, you do not need to build APIs yourself. You need to understand what they do so you can choose the right tools, ask a developer the right questions, and know what you are paying for. Many services also offer simple setup screens that connect an API for you with a few clicks.

### Are APIs free?

Some are free, some charge based on usage, and some need a paid plan. A small business can often start free — for example, a payment gateway that takes a small percentage per transaction. Always check the pricing page before you commit.

### Is it safe to share an API key?

No. An API key is like a password for your business — anyone with it can make requests on your behalf, including charging customers or reading your data. Share it only with a developer you trust, through a secure channel, and never post it in a public chat or forum.

### Can my small business use APIs?

Yes — that is exactly who they are for. A small shop can accept online payments, send automatic SMS receipts, and sync orders to a spreadsheet using APIs, without hiring a large technical team. Start with one useful connection, such as payments, and add more only when you need them.

## Start With One Connection
