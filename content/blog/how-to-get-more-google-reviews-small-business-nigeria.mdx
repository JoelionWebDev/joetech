Emeka runs Mama Ejiro's Kitchen, a restaurant on Admiralty Way in Lekki Phase 1. Jollof rice, ofada, catfish pepper soup, and a small chops tray that sells out most Saturdays. Forty or so customers a day, and on a good weekend the dining room is full by 1pm. People tell him the food is good. And in two months, his Google profile collected exactly three reviews.

The food was not the problem. The asking was. Emeka never asked, because he never knew what to say. So he built himself a toolkit: exact words for every situation, a QR code where customers cannot miss it, and a follow-up sequence for the people who said yes and then forgot. Six weeks later, the profile had 31 reviews. Nothing about the food changed. Only the asking did.

This is that toolkit: not setting up a Google profile, and not replying to reviews once they arrive. Just the ask itself — the words, the card, and the nudge.

## Why Your Customers Say Yes and Still Never Post

Before the scripts, understand the gap they are closing. When Emeka started asking properly, he found that almost nobody refused. Out of ten customers asked, eight or nine said "sure, no problem" with a smile. But only two or three actually posted. The rest were not lying. They meant it in the moment, and then the day swallowed them: traffic, a call from work, a child to pick up. By evening, the review was gone from their mind.

The face-to-face words get the yes. The QR code and the link make the yes take thirty seconds instead of five minutes. The follow-up catches the people whose yes got lost in traffic. None of these steps is begging. Begging is asking four times. This is asking once, making it easy, and reminding once.

## Face-to-Face Scripts: Say Them Exactly Like This

The hardest part of asking is the awkwardness, and the cure for awkwardness is knowing your lines before the moment comes. Emeka printed these three scripts on a small card and taped it behind the till, where only his staff can see it.

**Script one: at the table, after a compliment.** A customer says "this jollof is very good" or finishes the plate and leans back, satisfied. The waiter says: "Thank you, I am glad you enjoyed it. If you have a minute, a quick Google review would really help our small kitchen. I can show you the code at the till — it takes less than a minute." Short, grateful, and it hands the customer something to do right now, not a homework assignment.

**Script two: at the till, while packing a takeaway.** The customer is waiting for the bag, phone in hand, with a minute to spare. The cashier says: "Thank you for coming. Before you go, would you mind leaving us a quick review on Google? Just point your phone at this code here and it takes you straight there." Then she points at the QR card on the counter. Pointing matters. Customers do not read signs; they follow a pointed finger.

**Script three: for a regular.** A man who eats there every Thursday does not need the full speech. The waiter says: "Chief, you are here every week — you know our food better than we do. If you can put that in a Google review for us, it would help other people find us."

Three rules sit under all three scripts. Ask while the customer is happy, never while they are complaining or waiting too long for a bill. Ask once per visit, not once per dish. And if a customer says no, smile and let it go.

## WhatsApp Scripts: Copy, Paste, Send

Most of Mama Ejiro's orders now come through WhatsApp, and the delivery customer never stands at the till. For them, the ask travels by message — and the message has to be short enough to read quickly and warm enough not to feel like a broadcast.

Emeka saved three templates in WhatsApp Business, so his staff send them in two taps.

**After a delivery, same evening:** "Hello Ada, thank you for ordering from Mama Ejiro's Kitchen today. We hope you enjoyed the ofada. If you have a minute, please leave us a quick Google review here: [link]. It helps other customers find us. Thank you."

**After a customer sends a compliment:** When a customer messages "the pepper soup was fire" or "my husband loved the small chops", the reply is: "Thank you so much, we are glad you loved it. Would you mind putting that in a Google review for us? Here is the link: [link]. It takes less than a minute." The compliment is the opening. Strike while the food is still warm in their memory.

**For a regular, once in a while:** "Hello Mr. Okonkwo, thank you for ordering with us again this week. Your support keeps our small kitchen going. If you have a moment, a Google review from a regular like you would mean a lot: [link]."

A few details make these work. The customer's name is in the first line. The thanks comes before the ask, never after it. The link is in the same message, so the customer never has to ask for it. And there is no promise of anything in return — no discount, no free drink. Google's rules forbid paying for reviews, even with a discount, and a customer who feels bribed writes a review that reads like a bribe.

Send the message once for each order or visit. Not once a day, not after every small purchase from the same person. One order, one ask.

## SMS Scripts: For Customers Who Are Not on WhatsApp

Some of Emeka's older customers do not use WhatsApp, or they ordered by phone call. For them, SMS still works — but an SMS has to be even shorter, because nobody reads a long text from a business.

**After a first visit or a big order:** "Thank you for eating at Mama Ejiro's Kitchen, Lekki. We are glad you enjoyed it. A quick Google review would help us a lot: [short link]. Thank you."

Use a shortened link in SMS, because a long Google link eats the whole message and looks like spam. And send it the same day, in the evening, when the customer is home and has a minute — not at 2pm when they are back at work.

## The QR Code: Where It Goes, and What the Card Should Say

The QR code is the part of the toolkit that works while nobody is asking. Emeka printed it in three places: on the till, on each table as a folded tent card, and as a sticker on the takeaway bag. The rule for each place is the same: put it where the customer's eyes and hands already are, while they are still happy.

The card says very little. At the top: "Enjoyed your meal?" Then the QR code, big — at least the size of a bottle cap, so an old phone can scan it from across the table. Underneath, one line: "Scan to leave us a Google review. It takes less than a minute." That is all. A QR card with too many words is a card nobody reads.

Before printing, Emeka tested the code with three phones: his Android, his wife's iPhone, and a waiter's old Tecno. All three opened the review page for Mama Ejiro's Kitchen, not a search page, and the business name was correct at the top. He also reprinted the code after the first batch, because the first print was too small and customers had to hold their phones inches from the card.

One more place people forget: the delivery bag. A customer who ordered on WhatsApp never sees the till. A sticker on the bag is the only prompt they get, and it arrives at the exact moment they are opening the food. Emeka's sticker says: "Enjoyed it? Scan and tell Google."

## The Follow-Up Sequence: Two Nudges, Then Stop

Here is where most of the extra reviews came from. Emeka keeps a simple notebook by the till — or a spreadsheet on his phone — with four columns: customer name, date, asked, reviewed. Every time a customer says yes to a review, their name goes in the book with the date.

**Day zero: the ask.** Face to face, or the WhatsApp message with the link — the day the customer is happiest, and the review is most likely.

**Day two: the first nudge.** If the name is still in the book with no review after two days, one short message: "Hello Ada, thank you again for eating with us on Saturday. Just a gentle reminder — if you still have a minute, our Google review link is here: [link]. No worries at all if you are busy." Most of the people who post, post after this message.

**Day five: the last nudge.** If there is still nothing after five days, one final message, even shorter: "Hello Ada, quick one — if you get a moment this week, a Google review would mean a lot to our small kitchen: [link]. Thank you." Then the name gets crossed out of the book. Done.

**After day five: stop.** No third message, no call, no begging. A customer nudged twice who has not posted has either forgotten again or decided not to, and a third message turns a friendly restaurant into a pest. Emeka's rule is firm: two nudges, then silence. One visit, one ask, two nudges, done.

Two situations need a special line. If a customer replies "I don't have a Google account", say "No problem at all, thank you for coming," and cross the name out. If a customer says "I tried but the link did not work", check the link yourself that same day and send a fresh one.

## Teaching Your Staff in Ten Minutes

A toolkit only works if the people on the floor use it. Emeka trained his four staff in a ten-minute huddle before the lunch rush. He read each script out loud once, had each waiter say it back once, and told them who asks when: the waiter asks at the table, the cashier asks at the till, and nobody asks a customer who is unhappy. He set a target for the team, not for reviews but for asks: ten asks a day across the whole restaurant. Asks are in their control. Reviews are not.

He also gave them one line for the moment a customer asks "what do I write?" The answer is: "Just write what you liked — the food, the service, anything. A sentence is enough."

## What Never Goes in the Toolkit

Three things stay out. No paying for reviews, no discounts or free food in exchange for five stars — Google can remove them, and customers can smell a bought review. No asking friends who have never eaten there. And no begging: the follow-up stops at two nudges, the ask stops at one per visit, and a "no" is the end of it.

Start tomorrow at lunch. Print the three face-to-face scripts behind the till. Save the three WhatsApp templates. Put the QR card on the counter and a table tent on each table. Open the notebook, and write down the first ten names of people who say yes. Nudge them on day two. Cross them out on day five if they never post. Emeka counted 31 after six weeks, from a standing start of three. The jollof did not change. The asking did.
