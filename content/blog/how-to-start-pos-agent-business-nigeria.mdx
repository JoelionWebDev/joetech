In almost every neighbourhood in Nigeria today, there is a small kiosk or a table with a POS machine and a handwritten sign: "POS Available Here." Behind that table is a POS agent, earning money every time someone withdraws cash, transfers money, or pays a bill. It is one of the easiest small businesses to start in the country. It needs no shop, no university degree, and no large capital. It needs a machine, some starting cash, and a good location.

But easy to start does not mean easy to succeed. Many new agents buy a terminal, sit in a poor location, run out of cash by noon, and close within three months. Others succeed and grow to two or three terminals, add other services, and earn a steady daily income. The difference is planning: choosing the right provider, the right location, the right charges, and managing cash and risk properly.

This guide shows you how to start a POS agent business in Nigeria the right way, with honest numbers and the practical lessons that experienced agents wish they had known on day one.

## What a POS Agent Actually Does

A POS agent is a person who offers basic banking services to people in their area, using a POS terminal and a mobile app provided by a bank or a fintech company. The agent is like a small, human ATM that also does transfers and bill payments.

The most common service is cash withdrawal. A customer who needs cash gives the agent their ATM card, the agent runs a withdrawal on the terminal, and hands the customer cash. The agent charges a small fee for the service. This is the service that brings the most customers, especially in areas far from a bank branch or a working ATM.

The second service is money transfer. A customer who wants to send money to someone else gives the agent cash, and the agent transfers the same amount from their own account or wallet to the recipient's account. The agent charges a fee for the transfer.

The third group of services includes deposits, bill payments, and airtime. Some customers want to deposit cash into their own bank account, pay for electricity, cable television, or buy airtime and data. Each of these earns the agent a small commission.

The agent earns in two ways: the fee charged to the customer, and the commission paid by the provider on each transaction. The fees look small, often ₦100 to ₦500 per transaction, but they add up. An agent who serves fifty customers a day can earn a meaningful daily income. The business is a volume business: many small transactions, done reliably, every day.

## How Much Money Do You Need to Start?

One of the attractions of the POS business is the low startup cost, but you should still count every naira before you begin, because running out of cash is the most common reason new agents fail.

The terminal itself is the first cost. Many fintech providers, such as Opay, Palmpay, and Moniepoint, give you a terminal after a deposit or a one-time payment. The cost is typically between ₦20,000 and ₦60,000, depending on the provider and whether the terminal is Android-based or a simpler machine. Some providers offer the terminal free if you commit to a transaction target. Do not choose a provider only because the terminal is free. The charges and the settlement terms matter more over time.

Your float is the second and more important cost. Float is the cash you keep on hand to give to customers who withdraw, and the money in your wallet or account to fund transfers. If you open with only ₦20,000 in cash, you will run out by mid-morning on a busy day, and every customer after that is lost. Most experienced agents recommend starting with at least ₦100,000 to ₦200,000 in total float, split between cash in your drawer and money in your wallet. In a busy market or a high-traffic area, you may need ₦300,000 or more. This is the real capital of the business, and you must protect it.

## Choosing the Right Provider

Your provider is your business partner. You will depend on them for the terminal, the network, the settlement of your money, and support when things go wrong. Choose carefully.

Compare the charges first. Providers charge the agent a small fee on each transaction, and the agent passes a charge on to the customer. If your provider's charge is high, your profit is low. Ask for the full charge list in writing: withdrawal charges, transfer charges, and any daily or monthly fees. Some providers charge a flat rate per transaction, others a percentage. Do the maths with your expected daily volume before you choose.

Compare the settlement speed. When a customer withdraws cash from you, that money leaves your wallet or account. You need your own money replenished quickly so you can serve the next customer. Providers that settle instantly or within a few hours are far better for an agent than providers that settle the next day. Ask other agents in your area which provider settles fastest, because their experience is more honest than any advert.

Compare the network reliability. A terminal that fails in your location is useless. Test the terminal in your exact spot before you commit. If the provider's terminal uses a network that is weak in your area, ask whether there is a model that works on a different network, or whether the terminal can connect to Wi-Fi.

Compare the support. Save the support number and test it. A provider whose support line never answers will leave you stranded when a transaction fails or a terminal breaks. Providers with agents or offices near your area can help faster, and that help is worth paying a slightly higher charge for.

## Picking a Location That Brings Customers

Location is the single biggest factor in a POS agent's success. A good location can make an average agent successful. A bad location can make a hardworking agent fail.

Look for places with heavy foot traffic and no nearby ATM. Markets, bus stops, junction corners, university gates, hospital entrances, and busy residential streets are all strong locations. The best spot is one where people already need cash: near a market where traders sell for cash, near a transport park where passengers need money for fares, or near a school where parents pay fees.

Look at the competition. If three agents already operate on the same street, a fourth will struggle unless they offer something better, such as lower charges, longer hours, or extra services. It is often better to find a busy street with only one agent, or none, than to join a crowded one.

Consider safety. You will be handling cash every day, so your location must be safe. A spot inside a shop, beside a trusted trader, or in a busy, well-lit area is safer than a lonely corner. Tell the people around you what you do, and build relationships with neighbouring traders. They will watch out for you, and they will send customers to you.

If you already run a shop, a provision store, or a barbing salon, your own shop is the best location of all. You already have customers, you already have a safe space, and the POS service brings in people who may also buy your other goods. Many provision store owners have doubled their daily income by adding a POS terminal to the counter.

## Setting Your Charges Fairly

Your charges are your income, and setting them wrong can either drive customers away or leave you working for nothing.

Start by finding out what other agents in your area charge. Walk around and ask, or watch what customers pay. In most Nigerian neighbourhoods, a withdrawal of ₦5,000 costs the customer about ₦100, a withdrawal of ₦10,000 costs about ₦200, and larger withdrawals cost more, often on a sliding scale. Transfers usually cost between ₦100 and ₦300 depending on the amount. These figures vary by area, and you should charge what your area accepts, not what you wish you could charge.

Do not undercharge to attract customers. An agent who charges half the going rate will attract a crowd, but will earn too little to sustain the business, and will run out of float faster because the volume is too high. Customers respect a fair price more than a cheap one, as long as the service is fast and reliable.

Do not overcharge either. A customer who feels cheated will not come back, and will warn their neighbours. In a business built on repeat customers, one overcharged customer can cost you ten future transactions.

Display your charges clearly on a board where customers can see them before they transact. A clear price list prevents arguments, builds trust, and shows that you are organised. When a customer knows the charge before they hand over their card, there is nothing to argue about afterwards.

Review your charges every few months. If your provider raises their charges, or if the cost of your float rises, you may need to adjust. Small, explained adjustments are accepted. Sudden, unexplained increases are not.

## Daily Operations: Running the Point Well

A POS point is a simple business, but it needs discipline. The agents who last are the ones who treat it like a real job, not a side hustle they do when they feel like it.

Open early and close late. The busiest times for withdrawals are early morning, when traders need cash to buy goods, and evening, when workers return home. An agent who opens at 7am and closes at 8pm will serve far more customers than one who opens at 10am. Consistency matters too: customers who find you closed twice will stop coming.

Keep your float balanced. Start the day with a plan: enough cash for withdrawals, and enough money in your wallet for transfers. During the day, when your cash runs low, deposit some of it into your wallet through the provider's app or at a nearby agent, so you can keep doing transfers. When your wallet runs low, withdraw cash from it to keep serving withdrawal customers. Managing this balance is the core skill of the business, and you will get better at it with practice.

Record every transaction. A simple notebook is enough: date, type of transaction, amount, charge, and the customer's name or phone number for larger amounts. At the end of the day, add up your charges. That is your income. Without records, you will never know whether you are making a profit or slowly losing your float.

## Staying Safe: Fraud, Theft, and Failed Transactions

Handling cash and other people's cards every day brings real risks. A few habits will protect you and your customers.

Never let a customer walk away with your terminal, and never let them enter their PIN where you can see it. Stand the terminal so the customer can see the amount on the screen before they enter their PIN. If the amount is wrong, cancel and start again. This protects the customer and protects you from accusations.

Verify every transaction before handing over cash. Wait for the success message on the terminal and, when possible, check your app for the transaction. A customer who shows you a debit alert on their phone may be showing a fake alert. Your own terminal and your own app are the only proof that counts.

Keep your cash safe. Do not keep all your float in one place. Keep a small amount in your drawer for the next few customers, and the rest in a safer place, such as a locked box or a bag you keep close. At the end of the day, take your cash home or deposit it, and do not walk home late at night with a large amount if you can avoid it.

When a transaction fails but the customer's account was debited, stay calm and explain the situation. Write down the transaction reference, the date, the amount, and the customer's phone number. Report it to your provider immediately. Most failed transactions reverse automatically within 24 hours, and your customer will appreciate that you followed up rather than dismissing them.

## Mistakes That Sink New Agents

The most common mistake is starting with too little float. An agent who runs out of cash by 11am loses the rest of the day's customers and earns a reputation for being unreliable. Start with enough float, and grow it as your volume grows.

The second mistake is mixing business money with personal money. When your float and your feeding money sit in the same pocket, you will spend your float without noticing, and one day you will open your drawer and find nothing to serve customers with. Keep the float separate, and pay yourself a fixed amount from your daily charges.

The third mistake is choosing a provider on the promise of a free terminal alone. A free terminal from a provider with high charges, slow settlement, and no support will cost you far more in a month than a paid terminal from a good provider. Compare the total cost, not the sign-up gift.

The fourth mistake is poor record keeping. Agents who do not record their transactions cannot tell whether they are making a profit, cannot spot theft, and cannot resolve disputes. A notebook and five minutes at the end of the day is all it takes.

The fifth mistake is ignoring the law and the rules. Register your business when you can, keep your identification documents ready, and follow your provider's rules about transaction limits and customer verification. Agents who operate without any records or registration find it hard to get help when a problem arises.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much can a POS agent earn in a day in Nigeria?

It depends on location and volume. An agent in a quiet street serving twenty customers a day might earn ₦2,000 to ₦5,000 daily. An agent in a busy market serving eighty to a hundred customers a day can earn ₦10,000 to ₦20,000 or more. Your income is your total charges minus your provider's fees and your costs. Location and consistency matter more than anything else.

### Do I need a shop to start a POS business?

No. Many agents start with a table and a chair under a shade, or from inside an existing shop. What matters is a safe, busy location and a reliable terminal. A shop helps, because it gives you shelter and security, but it is not required. Some agents start from their provision store counter.

### Which is better for a POS agent: a bank or a fintech provider?

For most new agents, a fintech provider is faster and easier. Registration is quicker, the terminal arrives sooner, and the charges are often lower. Banks offer POS terminals too, but the process is slower and the requirements are stricter. Many experienced agents hold terminals from both a bank and a fintech provider, so they have a backup when one network fails.

## What to Do Next

1. Walk around your area this week and note the busiest spots with no nearby ATM and no POS agent, then ask three agents in nearby areas which provider they use and what they earn on a good day.
2. Compare two or three providers on paper: the cost of the terminal, the charge per transaction, the settlement speed, and the quality of support. Choose the best total package, not the cheapest machine.
3. Set aside your float, get your terminal, and open on a Monday morning with a clear price board and a notebook. Serve every customer well, record every transaction, and review your earnings at the end of the first week.
