Every home, office, school, hotel, restaurant, church and mosque in Nigeria buys cleaning products. Liquid soap, disinfectant, bleach and multipurpose cleaner are weekly and monthly necessities, bought again and again by the same customers. That repeat purchase makes a cleaning products business one of the most practical small manufacturing businesses you can start in Nigeria today.

You do not need a factory or a degree in chemistry. Many successful producers in Lagos, Abuja, Port Harcourt, Ibadan, Enugu and Onitsha started in a room in their house with plastic drums, a mixing stick and a modest stock of chemicals. What they had was a reliable recipe, clean production habits, honest measurements and a plan for selling every batch they made. This guide walks you through the whole journey in order, so you avoid the mistakes that sink most beginners — especially producing first and looking for customers later.

## Step 1: Understand the Market Before You Mix Anything

Cleaning products sell because people need them constantly. A family that buys a litre of liquid soap this week will need another one in a few weeks. A hotel that orders ten litres of disinfectant this month will order again next month. Your job is to find a small corner of that demand and serve it better than the next person.

Start by deciding who your first customers will be. Households buy small sizes — 500 ml and one-litre bottles — and care about smell, thickness and price. Offices, schools, hotels and restaurants buy in bulk, usually five litres and above, and care about consistency and reliable supply. Retail shops buy to resell, so they care about your wholesale price and whether your product moves. Laundries, car wash operators and cleaning companies are heavy users who buy regularly and in volume.

Pick one group to start with — a beginner who tries to serve everybody usually serves nobody well. If you live in a residential area, households and nearby shops are the natural starting point; if you know a few hotel or school managers, the bulk market may be easier. Your first ten customers matter more than your first hundred litres, because repeat buyers are the foundation of this business.

Walk around your area and study the shelves: which brands, sizes and prices do nearby provision stores carry, and what do customers complain about — soap that is too watery, disinfectant that smells harsh, bleach that stains clothes? Every complaint is a gap you can fill. Do not start by fighting big national brands on supermarket shelves. Your advantage is local: direct delivery, lower overheads, and the freedom to adjust fragrance or thickness to what your own customers ask for.

## Step 2: Learn How to Produce — Properly

Production is the heart of this business, and it is a skill you must learn properly before you sell a single bottle. Poorly made products do not just lose customers; they can damage skin, ruin clothes and destroy your reputation before you start.

The most popular products for beginners are liquid soap, disinfectant, bleach and multipurpose cleaner. Liquid soap is usually the first product because it is the most forgiving to learn and the easiest to sell. Disinfectant and bleach follow naturally, since they use some of the same equipment and sell to the same customers. Air freshener and toilet cleaner can come later.

You have two ways to learn: attend a practical training — many run in Lagos, Abuja, Onitsha and other major cities, with some available online — or learn from an experienced producer willing to teach you, practising in small batches until your results are consistent. A good training teaches measurements, mixing order, safety and troubleshooting, not just a list of ingredients. Whichever route you choose, do not rely on a recipe copied from a random social media post: recipes vary, chemical quality varies, and a wrong measurement can produce a batch that separates, smells wrong or irritates the skin.

Learn the basic raw materials by name so you can buy confidently: sulphonic acid, caustic soda, soda ash, texapon, salt, colour and fragrance for liquid soap; pine oil or chloroxylenol-based concentrates for disinfectant; sodium hypochlorite solution for bleach. You do not need to memorise chemistry, but you do need to know what each ingredient does and what happens when you add too much or too little.

Safety is not optional: wear gloves and eye protection when handling caustic soda and bleach, work in a ventilated space, keep chemicals away from children, and label every container clearly. Practise in small batches first, five or ten litres at a time, and test each one yourself and with family and friends: does the soap foam well, clean grease, and leave hands comfortable? Keep notes on every batch — what you used, how much, and how it turned out. That notebook becomes your production manual.

## Step 3: Decide Your Products and Your Starting Capital

Do not try to launch with eight products. Start with two or three that share ingredients and customers. A strong starter combination is liquid soap, disinfectant and bleach. They cover the three most common household cleaning needs, they are made with similar equipment, and a customer who buys one is likely to buy the others.

Your starting capital depends on your scale. A home-based beginner producing 25-litre and 50-litre batches can start with roughly ₦50,000 to ₦150,000, covering raw materials, drums and buckets, a mixing stick or hand mixer, measuring containers, bottles, labels and samples. A bigger setup with a proper mixing drum, a sealing machine and a larger first stock might run from ₦200,000 to ₦500,000. These are realistic ranges, not promises — your exact figure depends on your location, suppliers and sizes.

Spend your capital in the right order: raw materials and packaging first, then only the equipment your batch size actually needs, then branding — many beginners spend too much on fancy labels before they have proven their soap cleans well. Get the product right first, then make it beautiful. Keep a simple record from day one — what you spent, produced, sold and earned — in a notebook or spreadsheet. If you cannot tell whether a batch made a profit, you cannot grow.

## Step 4: Register the Business and Handle the Legal Side

You can start production at home while you sort out registration, but do not skip it. A registered business name builds trust with shops, hotels and corporate customers, and it is required if you want to open a business bank account, supply an organisation or eventually get your products into bigger stores.

Register a business name with the Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC) — choose one that is simple, easy to pronounce and remember, and check availability first. A business name registration is the usual starting point for a small producer; you can upgrade the structure later as the business grows.

If you plan to sell widely through shops and supermarkets, you will eventually need NAFDAC registration. It takes time and money, so many small producers start by selling directly to households, offices and local shops while they build sales and prepare for it — and they never print a NAFDAC number they do not have. That is both illegal and a fast way to lose trust permanently.

Label your products properly from the beginning: product name, brand name, size, directions for use, a caution note where needed, your contact details and a batch date. Even a simple, clean label from a local print shop beats a handwritten one — customers judge a product by its label before they open it, and shops prefer stocking products that look professional. Keep your production area clean, dry and separate from food preparation, and finished products away from sunlight and heat.

## Step 5: Source Raw Materials from Reliable Suppliers

Your product is only as good as your raw materials. Cheap, adulterated or wrongly stored chemicals produce inconsistent soap, weak disinfectant and unhappy customers. Finding a reliable supplier is one of the most important jobs in this business.

The main chemical markets are well known: Ojota and Oyingbo in Lagos, Onitsha Main Market in Anambra, and chemical dealers in Abuja, Kano, Ibadan and Port Harcourt. If you live far from these, find a reputable dealer in your nearest major city or ask experienced producers where they buy. A known dealer with a fixed shop is usually safer than a random roadside seller, because you can return and complain if a chemical turns out fake or diluted.

Buy small quantities first and test them — a supplier who sells you good sulphonic acid this week should sell you the same quality next month, because if the quality changes, your product changes and your customers will notice. Once you find a supplier you trust, build the relationship: pay on time, buy regularly and ask for better prices as volumes grow. Bulk purchases are often cheaper per litre than small bottles.

Store chemicals in a cool, dry place away from sunlight, containers tightly closed and clearly labelled, and keep a simple stock record so you know what is running low. Reorder before you run out, not after — a stock-out in the middle of a large order loses sales and customers. Watch price changes too: chemical prices move with the exchange rate, so review your costs regularly. Customers accept a small, honest price increase far more readily than a product that suddenly becomes watery because you quietly reduced the active ingredients.

## Step 6: Cost Your Products and Set Prices That Make a Profit

This is the step most beginners get wrong. They copy a competitor's price and then wonder why the business never grows. Your price must cover your costs and leave you a profit, or the business is just a hobby that consumes your money.

Costing is simple once you do it properly. For one batch, add up every ingredient, the bottle and cap, the label, and a share of transport, water and electricity, then divide by the number of litres or bottles produced — that is your cost per unit. Then add your margin: many small producers aim for roughly 30 to 50 percent on production cost. A litre of liquid soap that costs ₦400 to produce might retail for ₦600 to ₦800. Cost each product separately and never guess — calculate.

Set two prices: retail for direct customers and wholesale for shops and bulk buyers. The wholesale price must still leave you a profit after the shop adds its margin, so work backwards from the retail price the shop will charge — if a shop sells your one-litre liquid soap for ₦800, your wholesale price might be around ₦550 to ₦650. Check the numbers for your own area rather than copying these examples. Resist the temptation to be the cheapest in the market: the cheapest producer is usually cutting corners on ingredients, and customers who buy only on price leave the moment someone cheaper appears. Compete on quality, consistency and service instead, review your prices every few months, and keep your costing sheet updated — it is one of the most valuable documents in your business.

## Step 7: Package and Brand Your Products

Packaging protects the product, tells the customer what is inside, and is the first thing a shop owner and a buyer see — in a crowded provision store, a clean, professional-looking bottle stands out from a plain, unlabelled one. Choose bottles that suit your market: households like one-litre and 500 ml bottles, while offices and hotels prefer five-litre and ten-litre kegs. Buy from packaging suppliers in your city and check that caps fit tightly — a leaking bottle in a customer's bag is a complaint waiting to happen.

Your label should be simple and readable: brand name, product name, size, short directions, a caution note where relevant, and a phone number or WhatsApp contact. You can design it yourself with free design tools and print it locally, or hire a designer for a modest fee once sales can support it. Pick a brand name that is easy to remember and pronounce, and not already in use by a bigger brand — customers who cannot spell your brand cannot easily recommend it. Use it consistently on every product and label. Consistency matters more than perfection: the same colour, thickness, smell and label, every batch, every time. That reliability is your brand, and it is what turns a small local product into a trusted name in your area.

## Step 8: Sell — Start with the Customers Closest to You

A product sitting in your house is not a business. Selling is a daily activity, not something you do once and hope for the best. Start with the people closest to you: family, friends, neighbours, your church or mosque community, colleagues and your estate. Offer samples, ask for honest feedback, and make it easy to buy — deliver to their door, accept transfers and cash, and follow up after a few days to ask how the product performed.

Then move to shops. Visit provision stores, supermarkets and mini-marts in your area with samples and a clear wholesale price list, and leave a few bottles on consignment if a shop owner is willing to try your product, agreeing how and when you will collect payment. Visit regularly — a shop owner who sees you every week is far more likely to reorder. Ask what is selling and what customers say, then adjust your production to match.

Offices, schools, hotels, restaurants and churches are your bulk market: visit them with a short price list, offer a few litres as a free sample, and agree a clear delivery arrangement. Many buy monthly, so one corporate customer can be worth dozens of household buyers — but one failed delivery can lose an account that took weeks to win. Use WhatsApp as your main sales channel at the start, with a business profile, clear photos and prices, and status updates for new batches. Word of mouth is powerful here because cleaning products are bought on trust, and a neighbour's recommendation carries more weight than any advert.

## Step 9: Handle Money, Records and Growth

Keep business money separate from personal money, even if that just means a separate account or wallet, and pay yourself a modest, regular amount rather than dipping into the business purse whenever you need cash. Record every sale and every expense in a simple book or spreadsheet, and add up your sales and costs at the end of each week. This habit takes a few minutes a day and saves you from the most common small-business failure: working hard, selling a lot, and still having no money because costs were never tracked.

Plan your reorders: when a key chemical falls to about a quarter of what you normally keep, order more — a stock-out means lost sales and customers who may never come back. Give credit carefully; many small producers lose money supplying shops on credit and never getting paid. Prefer cash or transfer on delivery for new customers, and extend credit only to those with a proven record of paying on time.

Grow in steps, following demand. When your liquid soap sells out regularly, increase your batch size before launching new products: bigger drums, a better mixer, a sealing machine, a dedicated production space. Add new products one at a time, based on what existing customers ask for, expand your area gradually, and consider distributors in other parts of your city or state once your brand is established, agreeing prices and territories in writing. Keep learning — attend trainings, join producer groups — because this business rewards patience and consistency far more than a big opening budget.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

Producing without customers is the first mistake — make a small batch, sell it, get feedback, then make a bigger one. The second is pricing too low to make a profit. The third is inconsistent quality: thick, fragrant soap one week and watery, dull soap the next. The fourth is ignoring safety and hygiene. The fifth is poor record-keeping. The sixth is spending on branding before the product is good. And finally, do not borrow heavily to start — begin with what you have, prove the product sells, and grow from your profits.

Follow a simple first-month rhythm: week one, finalise recipes, practise small batches and identify first customers; week two, register your business name, buy stock and print labels; week three, deliver samples, visit shops and take your first orders; week four, add up sales and spending, ask customers for honest feedback, and plan your next, slightly bigger batch. Learn, produce, sell, review, repeat — that is how a small cleaning products business in Nigeria becomes a trusted local brand.
