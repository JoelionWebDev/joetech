Sade sells small chops in Ikeja: meat pie, spring rolls, puff puff and samosa, packed in neat boxes for birthdays and office parties. For three years she has sold the same way, by phone call and bank transfer. A customer calls, Sade writes the order in a notebook, the customer sends money to her Access Bank account, and Sade starts frying.

It worked until the Saturday she catered a birthday in Ogba. The customer had ordered twenty boxes at ₦12,000 each, ₦240,000 in all. Sade bought the flour, the chicken and the oil on Friday, because the woman had promised the transfer that evening. It never came, and the woman's phone went off. Sade spent Saturday morning calling a number that would not ring, with twenty boxes cooling on her table and ₦180,000 of ingredients spent.

That evening, her younger brother said something she had heard before and always brushed aside. "Sade, collect the money with a link before you fry anything." She had always assumed a payment link was for big businesses, people with websites and developers. It is not. It is the simplest thing Paystack sells, built for a seller who has never taken an online payment before.

## Before You Touch the Website, Get These Ready

Before you begin, gather these things and put them on the table next to you.

An email address you actually open. This becomes your Paystack login, and it is where every payment alert goes. Sade used a Gmail address she had owned for years, not the Yahoo address she had forgotten the password to. If you cannot open the inbox, you are stuck at the first step.

A Nigerian bank account in your name. The name on the account must match the name you give Paystack, or the verification will fail. Have the account number written down, not memorised. One wrong digit is the most common reason this step fails.

Your Bank Verification Number. Paystack asks for your BVN to confirm your identity. Sade dialled *565*0# on her phone and her BVN appeared on the screen. Write it down. Do not send it to anyone, and do not type it into any website except Paystack's own.

## Signing Up and Filling in Your Business Profile

Sade opened paystack.com on her phone, on a Tuesday morning after her first batch of puff puff was done. She tapped Sign Up, typed her Gmail address, and chose a password she could remember. Paystack sent a confirmation email. She opened it, tapped the link, and her account was alive.

The next screen asked for her business profile: business name, phone number, a short description of what she sells, and a category. She typed "Sade's Small Chops", her phone number, and "Small chops and finger foods for parties and offices in Lagos", and picked the food category. This shows up on the checkout page her customers will see, so she wrote it plainly and honestly.

Then came the bank account. Inside the dashboard she went to Settings, then Bank Accounts, and typed her Access Bank account number. Paystack checked the number and displayed the name attached to it. It matched her name, so she saved it. If the name had not matched, the fix would have been simple: check the digits again, one by one.

After that came identity verification. She entered her NIN details as requested. This step is called KYC, Know Your Customer, and it is a legal requirement for any company that moves money in Nigeria. Paystack told her the review would take a few hours to a day. Hers came back the next morning. She had spent the whole of Tuesday worrying she had done something wrong. She had not.

## Test Mode and Live Mode: The Switch That Trips Up Every Beginner

One thing about the Paystack dashboard confuses almost every first-timer, and it confused Sade too. Your account has two modes. Test mode is a practice room. You can create links and pretend to pay with test card numbers Paystack provides. No real money moves. Live mode is the real shop. Real money moves, and a customer paying in live mode is paying you for real.

Sade created her first link while her account was still in test mode, because that was the only mode available before verification. She sent it to her brother on WhatsApp and asked him to pay ₦100 through it. He tapped it, typed in a test card number from Paystack's own test page, and the payment "succeeded". Then she tried to withdraw the ₦100 and it was not there, because it had never existed. It was play money in a practice room.

This is the single most common first-time mistake. If you send a customer a link made in test mode, they cannot pay you with a real card. Before you share any link with a real customer, look at the top of your dashboard and check the mode. It should say Live. If it says Test, flip the switch, and only then copy the link.

## Creating the Very First Link, for One Product Only

Here is where beginners go wrong a second time. They open the link creator and try to build their whole business in one sitting. Do not do that. Your first link is for one product, the one you sell most, and you can build the rest later.

Sade's best seller is her party box: twenty pieces of mixed small chops, meat pie, spring rolls, samosa and puff puff, for ₦12,000. She went to Payments, then Payment Links, and tapped Create Payment Link.

The name field came first. She typed "Sade's Party Box — 20 Pieces". Not "Product 1". Not "Small Chops". The name appears on the checkout page, and a customer who has never bought from her should know exactly what she is paying for. She added a description in her own words: "Twenty pieces of mixed small chops, meat pie, spring rolls, samosa and puff puff. Freshly made on the day of your event. Delivery within Ikeja available."

Then the amount. She typed 12000. No comma, no naira sign, just the digits. She checked it twice, then a third time, because this is the mistake that costs real money. Typing 1200 instead of 12000 means you sell a ₦12,000 box for ₦1,200. Typing 120000 means your customer sees a price ten times higher than quoted. Sade read the number out loud before she saved it.

She left the amount fixed, which is right for a product with a set price. Paystack also offers an open amount, where the customer types what she wants to pay, but that is for donations and custom jobs, not a party box. She left the redirect blank too. After payment, Paystack shows its own confirmation page, and that is fine for a first link.

She saved the link and copied the address. It looked like paystack.com/pay/sades-small-chops. She opened it on her own phone before sending it to anyone, and she checked it the way a customer would: the business name, the product name, the price. It was right.

## Sharing It Where Your Customers Already Are

The first person to receive it was Mrs. Adeyemi, who orders for her church's monthly meeting. Sade sent her a short message: "Good afternoon ma, here is the payment link for the party box, ₦12,000. Once payment is confirmed I will prepare your order." She saved it as a quick reply in WhatsApp Business for next time.

She also put the link in her WhatsApp status that evening, next to a photo of a fresh party box. Not "DM to order". The status said "Party box ₦12,000 — tap the link to pay now". "DM to order" starts a conversation that might take an hour. A link starts a payment that takes a minute, even at midnight when Sade is asleep.

On Instagram, she put the link in her bio, because Instagram does not allow clickable links in captions. When she posts a photo of a box, the caption says "Link in bio to order". In her stories she uses the link sticker, so a follower can tap straight through to checkout. WhatsApp first, Instagram second.

## What Goes Wrong the First Time, and How to Fix It

The first was the bank name mismatch. She typed her account number from memory and one digit was wrong, and Paystack showed a name she did not recognise. She went back, typed the number slowly from her bank app, and her own name appeared. If Paystack ever shows you a name that is not yours, do not save it. Go back and check the number.

The second was the wrong amount. She created a link for a smaller box, eight pieces for ₦5,000, and in a hurry she typed 50000. Mrs. Adeyemi opened it, saw ₦50,000 for a small box, and called her, laughing, to ask whether the price of flour had gone up ten times overnight. Sade deleted the link, made it again, typed 5000, read it out loud, and sent it. The lesson never changes: read the amount out loud before you save.

Two more problems you will meet are not your fault at all. The first is a customer's card being declined. Her bank may have blocked the transaction, or the card may not have enough money, or she typed a wrong digit. Do not tell her to keep trying the same card. Tell her to try bank transfer or USSD on the same link. A customer who fails on card will often succeed on transfer in the same minute.

The second is a customer who says she paid but you cannot find the payment. Never argue from a screenshot. Open your Paystack dashboard, go to Transactions, and search for her name or the payment reference on her receipt. If the payment is there, reassure her and prepare the order. If it is not there, the payment did not reach Paystack.

## The First Real Payment

Mrs. Adeyemi paid on a Thursday morning. Sade was frying puff puff when her phone buzzed. The notification said a payment of ₦12,000 had arrived for the party box. She wiped her hands, opened her dashboard, and there it was: the customer's name, the amount, the time, and the reference number. No screenshot to squint at, no phone call to confirm, no waiting for a late bank alert.

She messaged Mrs. Adeyemi on WhatsApp: "Payment received, thank you ma. Your box will be ready by 2 pm." Then she went back to frying, and the order was delivered that afternoon. The money settled into her Access Bank account the next working day, minus Paystack's fee: 1.5 percent plus ₦100, which is ₦280 on a ₦12,000 payment, so she received ₦11,720.

## From One Link to a Habit

Sade still takes bank transfers. Some older customers prefer them, and she is not going to argue with a woman who has ordered from her for three years. But the link is now her default. When a new customer orders, the link goes out first, and the account number is only a backup.

If you have never taken an online payment, your first link is the hardest one you will ever create, and it takes about ten minutes once your account is verified. Get those things ready, sign up, add your bank account carefully, wait for verification, switch to live mode, and make one link for your best-selling product. Then send it to your next customer instead of your account number. Sade's advice: "I wish I had done it the year I started."
