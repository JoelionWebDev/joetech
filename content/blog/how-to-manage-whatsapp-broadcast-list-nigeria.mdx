If you run a small business in Nigeria, WhatsApp is probably your shop, your customer service desk and your marketing department rolled into one. A customer in Surulere asks for your price list on WhatsApp. A buyer in Enugu confirms an order on WhatsApp. A regular in Abuja sends you a bank transfer screenshot on WhatsApp. So it makes sense that when you have a new product, a price change or a weekend offer, your first instinct is to reach everybody at once.

That is exactly what a WhatsApp broadcast list does. One message from you, delivered privately to each person on the list, as if you messaged them one by one. Done well, it is the cheapest marketing channel a Nigerian small business can own. Done badly, it gets your number reported or banned — and a banned business number costs you customers, order history and reputation in one stroke.

This guide walks you through managing a WhatsApp broadcast list the right way: how to build it, how to write messages people actually welcome, how often to send, and how to stay on the right side of WhatsApp's rules.

## What a broadcast list is, and what it is not

A broadcast list is a saved list of contacts. When you send a message to the list, WhatsApp delivers it to each person individually. Each recipient sees the message as a normal one-to-one chat from you. They cannot see who else received it, and their replies come back to you alone.

That privacy is the whole point. A group shows everyone's numbers; a broadcast keeps things personal, so your customer in Lekki feels the message was written just for them.

But a broadcast list has two hard limits you must understand from the start. First, a single broadcast list holds a maximum of 256 contacts. If you have more customers than that, you will need more than one list — for example, one for Lagos customers, one for Abuja customers, or one for wholesale buyers and one for retail buyers. Second, and most important, your message will only be delivered to people who have your number saved in their own phone contacts. If a customer has not saved your number, your broadcast message simply never arrives. There is no error message. It just vanishes. This single rule is why so many Nigerian businesses complain that "broadcast is not working." It is working. The recipients just have not saved your number.

A broadcast list is not a bulk-messaging machine or a shortcut for spamming strangers. WhatsApp designed it for people who already know you — keep that idea at the centre of everything below.

## Start with WhatsApp Business, not the regular app

If you are still running your business on the regular WhatsApp app, your first step is to switch to WhatsApp Business. It is free, it is made by the same company, and it gives you tools the regular app does not have: a business profile with your address, opening hours and website, quick replies, labels for organising customers, automated greeting and away messages, and a catalogue where you can list your products with prices and photos.

Download WhatsApp Business from the Play Store or App Store, register with your business number, and fill in your business profile completely. Add your shop address in Lekki or your market stall location in Onitsha. Add your opening hours. Add your email and your website if you have one. A complete profile makes you look like a real business, and real businesses get reported far less often than blank numbers that only ever send adverts.

One practical warning: do not run the same number on both apps. Pick the business number you want customers to know, move it to WhatsApp Business, and keep it there. Changing numbers confuses customers and breaks the "saved contact" chain that broadcast depends on.

## Build your list only from people who said yes

This is the rule that decides whether your number survives. Every person on your broadcast list should have given you permission to message them, or at least have a real buying relationship with you. That means people who bought from you, people who asked for your price list, people who filled your order form, people who attended your event, or people who joined your list through a link or QR code you shared.

It does not mean contacts you copied from another group, numbers you bought from a vendor in Computer Village, or every number in your phone book. Adding strangers is the fastest route to reports. When someone who does not know you receives a sales message, their easiest response is to tap "Report spam." A handful of reports in a short time can trigger a temporary restriction on your number. Enough of them can get the number banned permanently.

So how do you grow a list the honest way in Nigeria? Start with your existing customers. When someone buys from you, ask: "Can I add you to my broadcast list so you hear about new stock and offers first?" Most will say yes, because they already trust you enough to pay you. Then, after every sale, send a short thank-you message and ask them to save your number so they do not miss updates. Remember the delivery rule: if they do not save your number, your broadcasts will not reach them.

Put your WhatsApp link everywhere your customers already look: on your Instagram bio, on your Facebook page, on your shop banner, on your flyers, on your receipts, on your product packaging. A short link like wa.me/2348012345678 with a clear promise — "Chat with us for new arrivals every Friday" — brings in people who actually want your messages.

Never buy a list of numbers. Those people never asked to hear from you, most will not have your number saved so your messages will not deliver, and many will report you.

## Create the list and organise it with labels

Creating a broadcast list takes less than a minute. On Android, open WhatsApp Business, tap the three dots at the top right, and choose "New broadcast." On iPhone, open Chats, tap "Broadcast Lists" at the top, then "New List." Select the contacts you want, give the list a name only you will see, and save it.

Do not put all your customers in one list. Segment them, because a tailor in Yaba and a wholesale buyer in Kano do not want the same message. Common splits for Nigerian businesses include new customers versus repeat customers, retail versus wholesale, Lagos versus outside Lagos, and buyers of one product line versus another. A food vendor might have one list for weekly soup subscribers and another for party catering enquiries. A fashion seller might separate customers who buy ready-to-wear from those who order custom pieces.

WhatsApp Business labels make this easier. Label contacts "New customer," "Paid," "Pending payment," "Wholesale," "VIP," or "Lagos delivery" — labels are private, and you can build a broadcast list from a label. A few seconds per customer turns a messy contact list into a marketing asset.

Keep your lists fresh. Every month, remove numbers that asked to stop, numbers that no longer engage, and numbers that changed lines. A smaller, engaged list beats a big, cold one — engagement is what keeps WhatsApp's systems happy with you.

## Get people to save your number

Since delivery depends on recipients saving your number, make it easy and rewarding. After a first chat with a new customer, send a message like: "Thank you for your order. Please save this number as [Your Business Name] so you receive your delivery updates and our Friday offers." Give them a reason that benefits them, not you.

You can also save their number first and ask them to save yours in return. In your shop, a small printed card at the counter that reads "Save our number to get delivery updates and weekly deals" works better than a verbal request people forget by the time they reach the bus stop.

Make your messages so useful that people want to stay subscribed. A customer who saved your number because your updates help them will never report you.

## What to send: value first, selling second

The biggest mistake Nigerian businesses make with broadcast is treating it as a free advert board. If every message is "Buy now, 20% off, limited stock," people will mute you, delete you or report you. Your broadcast should feel like a helpful message from a business they like, not a hawker shouting at them through their phone.

A good rule is that at least half of your broadcasts should give value without asking for money: a skincare tip for harmattan, a quick recipe from a food vendor, a warning about fake chargers, a new style from a tailor. Value builds trust, and trust makes the selling messages work.

When you do sell, make the message specific and useful. Instead of "We have new arrivals," try: "New Ankara ready-to-wear just landed — sizes 8 to 18, ₦18,500 each. Only 12 pieces. Reply with your size and I will hold one for you till 6pm today." A price, a quantity, a deadline and a clear next step turn a broadcast into an order.

Keep messages short. Two to four short lines are usually enough. Long paragraphs get skipped, especially by customers reading on a phone in traffic. Write the way you talk to a customer standing in front of you: warm, direct and brief.

Use the customer's name when it fits. WhatsApp Business quick replies can help you send fast, but a broadcast that starts "Good morning, Mrs Ade" feels different from one that starts "Good morning all." You do not need to personalise every broadcast, but the ones that matter most — a restock alert for a product someone asked about — should feel personal.

## How often to send, and when

Frequency is where most businesses lose their audience. Send too often and you become noise. Send too rarely and people forget who you are.

For most Nigerian small businesses, one to three broadcasts a week is the sweet spot. A daily broadcast is too much unless customers specifically asked for daily updates — a food vendor sending a daily menu to subscribers who asked for it is fine; a boutique sending a daily advert is not.

Pick your timing with your customers' day in mind. Early morning, between 7am and 9am, works well for offers people can act on during the day. Lunchtime, around 12pm to 1pm, catches people on break. Evening, between 6pm and 8pm, is when most people are home, on Wi-Fi and relaxed enough to browse. Avoid late-night messages — a sales broadcast at 11pm feels intrusive and earns reports. Avoid very early morning too. And think about payday patterns: messages near the end of the month, when salaries land, often convert better than messages sent in the middle of the month when money is tight.

Test your own audience: send the same offer on a Tuesday morning one week and a Saturday evening the next, and watch which gets more replies.

## Writing messages that do not look like spam

WhatsApp's systems and your customers both judge your messages in seconds. Certain habits make a message look like spam, and you should avoid them all.

Do not send the same message to many lists at the exact same minute, over and over, every day. Space your sends out. Do not forward a message that has already been forwarded many times — WhatsApp labels heavily forwarded messages, and customers distrust them. Write your own message each time, in your own words.

Do not use all capital letters. "BUY NOW!!! LIMITED STOCK!!!" reads like a scam, even when the offer is real. Do not overload a message with emojis, links and phone numbers. One link is enough. Do not attach huge files; compress images before sending so they load fast on a customer's data plan. A 4MB photo that takes a minute to download on a slow connection will be ignored.

Always include a simple way out. A line like "Reply STOP if you would like to stop receiving these updates" costs you nothing and saves you reports. Customers who can leave quietly will leave quietly. Customers who cannot leave will report you instead. Honour every opt-out immediately and remove the person from the list the same day.

## How to know if your broadcasts are working

WhatsApp Business shows you a simple but powerful signal: the blue ticks. When you send a broadcast, a single grey tick means it was sent, two grey ticks mean it was delivered, and two blue ticks mean it was read. If a message shows only one grey tick for most of the list, the problem is usually that recipients have not saved your number. If it shows two grey ticks but no blue ticks, people received it but did not open it — your opening line or your timing needs work.

Track replies, not just reads. A broadcast that gets ten replies and three orders beats a broadcast that gets two hundred reads and silence. Keep a simple notebook or spreadsheet: date, list name, message topic, number of replies, number of orders, amount sold. After a month you will see clearly which topics earn money and which ones waste your effort. A fashion seller in Lekki once told me her "new stock" broadcasts outsold her "discount" broadcasts two to one. She would never have known that without tracking.

## What to do if WhatsApp warns or restricts you

Sometimes, even a careful business gets a warning. It can happen if several people report you in the same week, or if WhatsApp's automated systems flag a sudden burst of activity — for example, sending the same message to four full lists within five minutes after months of silence.

If you see a warning, stop broadcasting immediately for a few days. Do not try to dodge it by using another number to send the same messages; that can get the new number banned too and makes the pattern look worse. Reply to your existing customers one by one only when they message you first. Keep your business profile active and normal.

When you resume, start small. Send to your smallest, most engaged list first — your VIPs and repeat buyers. Send something genuinely useful, not a sales blast. Wait, watch the response, and build back up slowly over a week or two.

If your number is banned outright, you will need to appeal through the app. Open WhatsApp Business, tap "Request a review," and explain briefly that you are a small business messaging your own customers. Be honest about what you did. Appeals sometimes succeed, sometimes do not, and while you wait you should have a backup way for customers to reach you — an Instagram page, a second business line, or a simple website with your contact details — so a ban never shuts your business down completely.

## Mistakes that get Nigerian businesses blocked

Let me name the most common ones plainly, because each of them has cost a real business its number.

Adding people to a list without asking. Scraping numbers from a group and blasting them. Sending the same advert to five lists in five minutes. Messaging at midnight. Using a brand-new number to send two hundred messages on its first day. Copying and pasting a message that was already forwarded a hundred times. Promising "free money" or "double your cash" to get replies. Asking customers to forward your message to ten people. Ignoring people who asked to stop. All of these either trigger reports from real humans or trip WhatsApp's automated systems. None of them is worth it.

## A simple weekly routine that keeps your list healthy

Here is a routine that takes less than an hour a week and keeps a broadcast list working for years.

On Monday, check last week's replies and orders. Midweek, send your main broadcast. On Thursday, reply personally to everyone who responded and update their labels. On Friday, add the week's new customers to the right list, with their permission. Once a month, clean the list of anyone who asked to stop or stopped engaging.

A broadcast list rewards the business that treats it like a relationship: ask permission, give value, respect people's time, and sell only when you have something genuinely worth their attention.

Your customers gave you their numbers because they trust you. A well-managed broadcast list repays that trust every week, with orders, repeat buyers and referrals. A badly managed one spends it in a single afternoon. Manage it like the asset it is, and it will quietly become one of the most profitable tools your business owns.
