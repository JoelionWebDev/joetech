Ask any trader in Balogun, Onitsha Main Market, Bodija or Mile 12 the same question, and you will hear the same story. A customer picks goods worth ₦45,000, reaches for her wallet, and finds she has only ₦10,000 cash. She asks, "Do you take transfer or POS?" If the answer is no, she puts the goods down and walks to the next stall that says yes. That sale is gone, and it happens every day in markets across Nigeria.

Cash is still king in many markets, but it is no longer the only king. Customers now carry less cash because ATMs run dry, because carrying large sums feels unsafe, and because their salaries arrive by bank transfer. Young buyers especially expect to pay by card or transfer. A stall that accepts only cash is a stall that turns away a growing share of its customers.

This guide shows you how to accept POS payments at your market stall, step by step. It covers how to get a terminal, what it costs, how to use it properly, what to do when a transaction fails, and how to protect yourself from the frauds that target market traders. You do not need a registered company or a big shop to start — many POS providers now serve market stalls, food vendors and roadside traders.

## Why a POS Terminal Pays for Itself

The first objection most traders raise is cost. Why pay charges on a sale you could take in cash for free? The answer is that cash is not free either. Cash gets stolen, it gets miscounted, it gets soaked in rain, and it ties you to the bank queue to deposit it. More importantly, cash-only stalls lose sales they never count, because the customer who walks away does not tell you she left.

A POS terminal lets you serve the customer in front of you, whatever is in her wallet. She can pay with her ATM card, and you receive the money in your account, usually the same day or the next business day. You can also accept transfers to the same account, so one setup covers both card and transfer customers. Over a month, the extra sales usually outweigh the charges by a wide margin, especially for stalls selling goods above ₦5,000, where customers rarely carry enough cash.

There is a safety benefit too. Less cash in your drawer means less to lose to theft, and less reason for anyone to target your stall at closing time. Your money sits in your account, where you can track it, instead of in a bag under the counter. For traders who close late or travel with their takings, that alone is worth the charges.

A POS also gives you records. Every transaction produces a receipt and a record in your account. At the end of the week, you can see exactly how much you sold by card, compare it with your cash sales, and know whether your business is growing. Cash sales rely on memory; POS sales leave a trail you can check.

## Step 1: Choose the Right POS Provider for a Market Stall

In Nigeria, you can get a POS terminal from a bank or from a fintech company. Banks offer POS terminals, but they often require a current account, a registered business and a long application process. Fintech providers — such as Moniepoint, Opay, Palmpay, Kuda and others — are usually faster and more practical for market traders, with agents in most major markets who can help you apply in person.

When you compare providers, look at five things:

- **Cost of the terminal.** Some providers give the terminal free if you meet a transaction target; others charge a one-off fee or a refundable deposit. Ask the agent clearly: is this a sale, a lease or a deposit, and what happens if the terminal breaks?
- **Charges per transaction.** POS charges in Nigeria are typically a small percentage of the amount, often capped at a maximum per transaction. Ask for the exact rate in writing, including whether transfers to your account attract a separate charge. Compare two or three providers before you commit — the difference may look small per sale but adds up over a month.
- **Settlement time.** How quickly does the money reach your account? Same day is ideal. Some providers settle the next business day. If you need the money to restock tomorrow morning, settlement time matters.
- **Network and reliability.** A terminal that cannot connect is useless during market hours. Ask other traders in your market which provider's terminal works best in that location. A provider that is strong in Lekki may be weak inside a concrete market building in Onitsha, where network is poor.
- **Support when things go wrong.** If a transaction fails or money is debited without a receipt, you need a human being who can help, quickly. Ask the agent for a support number and a local contact, not just a general helpline. A provider with an agent in your market is worth more than a slightly cheaper rate from a provider nobody can reach.

Do not choose a provider only because an agent approached you first. Walk around your market, ask three traders who already use POS which one they use and whether they are happy, and then decide. Traders share honest opinions about POS providers — a terminal that fails on Saturdays is a reputation that spreads fast.

## Step 2: Apply and Set Up Your Terminal

Applying for a POS terminal from a fintech provider is usually straightforward. You will typically need your Bank Verification Number (BVN), a valid ID such as your National ID, driver's licence, voter's card or international passport, a passport photograph, and your business details — your stall location, what you sell, and your account details for settlement. Some providers ask for a utility bill or a photo of your shop. Have these ready before you visit the agent, so the application is finished in one visit.

The agent will register you, link the terminal to your account, and show you how to use it. Do not rush this training. Ask the agent to show you, slowly, how to do a sale, how to print a receipt, how to check your balance, how to reverse a failed transaction, and how to charge the terminal. Practise each step yourself while the agent watches. A mistake learned at the stall, in front of a customer, is far more expensive than ten extra minutes of training.

When the terminal arrives or is handed to you, check three things before you leave: that it powers on and holds a charge, that it prints clearly, and that a small test transaction works. Keep the receipt from the test. Write down the terminal ID, the support number and the agent's phone number in a notebook you keep at the stall, not only on your phone.

Set a simple daily routine for the terminal. Charge it fully every night, using a power bank as backup during the day if NEPA takes light at the market. Keep it in a safe place at the stall — not on the edge of the counter where it can be knocked off or grabbed. Wipe the keypad and screen at the end of the day. A terminal that is charged, clean and working is ready to take money; a flat or faulty terminal is a lost day of sales.

## Step 3: How to Take a POS Payment Correctly

A correct POS sale takes less than a minute when you follow the same steps every time. Do not improvise, and do not let a customer rush you into skipping a step.

1. **Agree the amount first.** Say the total out loud: "Your total is ₦23,500." Let the customer confirm before you enter anything. Many disputes start because the amount entered was not the amount agreed.
2. **Enter the amount on the terminal and show the customer.** Let her see the screen before she inserts or taps her card. If she is paying by transfer to your POS account, confirm the account name and number from your own records, not from a paper she hands you.
3. **Let the customer enter her PIN herself.** Never ask for a customer's PIN, never watch her type it, and never enter it for her. Shield the keypad with your hand or turn the terminal towards her. If a customer asks you to enter her PIN, refuse politely — it protects both of you.
4. **Wait for the receipt.** Do not hand over the goods until the terminal prints "Approved" and you have the receipt in your hand. A message on the customer's phone saying she was debited is helpful, but the printed receipt from your terminal is your own proof. If the terminal says "Declined" or "Failed", do not release the goods.
5. **Give the customer her copy and keep yours.** Write the customer's name or the item on your copy if you need to match it later. At the end of the day, count your receipts against the money in your account.

For contactless payments, the customer taps her card on the terminal. The same rule applies: wait for approval and the receipt before you release the goods. For transfers, ask the customer to show you the success screen, check that the account name matches yours, and confirm the amount before you hand over the goods. Do not accept a screenshot alone — ask to see the transaction live on her phone if you are unsure.

## Step 4: What to Do When a Transaction Fails

Failed transactions are the part of POS that frightens new traders most, but they are manageable if you stay calm and follow a clear process. The most common failure is a debit without a receipt: the customer's account is debited, but your terminal shows failed and prints nothing. This happens when the network drops in the middle of a transaction.

If this happens, do not panic and do not hand over the goods yet. First, check the terminal for a reversal message and try printing the last transaction. Then ask the customer to check her bank app or SMS alert. If she was debited and you were not credited, tell her honestly what happened, write down her name, phone number, the amount, the time and the terminal receipt number if there is one, and give her your number. Tell her to contact her bank if the money is not reversed automatically, which usually happens within 24 hours, and report the case to your POS provider with the details you wrote down.

Never refund a customer in cash on the spot for a failed POS transaction unless your provider confirms the money reached you and you are returning it. A cash refund for a transaction that later reverses means you lose twice. If the customer is angry, stay polite, explain the process, and offer to follow up with her the next day. Most customers have experienced this before, and a calm, honest explanation keeps the relationship.

If a transaction is declined, it usually means insufficient funds, a wrong PIN, or a bank network issue. Ask the customer to try once more, or to pay by transfer or cash if she has it. Do not keep retrying a declined card many times — it can lock the card and waste both your time and the queue behind her.

## Step 5: Protect Yourself From Fraud

POS fraud targets busy traders, so learn the tricks and guard against them.

- **Fake alerts.** A customer shows you a transfer alert on her phone that was never sent, using a fake app or an edited screenshot. Always check your own account balance or SMS alert before releasing goods, especially for large amounts. Your alert is the truth; her screen is only a claim.
- **Card swapping.** A customer distracts you and swaps your terminal, or swaps her card during the transaction. Keep your terminal in your hand or in sight at all times, check the terminal ID before each sale, and never let a customer take the terminal away from the counter.
- **Overpayment scams.** A customer claims she transferred ₦100,000 instead of ₦10,000 and asks for a cash refund of the difference. Check your account first. If no overpayment arrived, there is nothing to refund.
- **Chargebacks.** Keep your receipts for at least three months. If a customer later disputes a transaction with her bank, your receipt and your records are your defence.

Set a limit for card payments if you are unsure — for example, confirm every transaction above ₦50,000 by checking your balance before the customer leaves. Train any assistant at the stall in the same rules. A thief needs only one careless moment; a trained assistant closes that gap.

## Daily Habits That Keep Your POS Working

- Charge the terminal overnight and carry a power bank at the stall.
- Start the day by printing a test or checking the balance, so you know the terminal is working before the first customer.
- Keep a paper record of card sales alongside the receipts, with the time and amount.
- At closing, count your receipts, check your account balance, and note any failed transactions to follow up the next morning.
- Clean the terminal and store it safely. Report faults to your provider early — a terminal that fails on Saturday morning is a weekend of lost sales.

Tell your customers you accept POS. Put a small sign at your stall: "We Accept POS and Transfers." Mention it on your WhatsApp status and in your market greeting. Many customers will not ask — they will simply walk past a stall that looks cash-only. A sign costs almost nothing and brings in buyers who would otherwise keep walking.

Keep a small notebook at the stall for POS problems. Each time a transaction fails, write the date, the amount, the customer's number and what the provider said. Over a month, that notebook shows you whether failures are rare network glitches or a pattern with one provider, one time of day, or one terminal. That record is what helps you decide whether to stay, complain, or switch.

## Final Word

Accepting POS payments is no longer a luxury for big shops. For a market stall, it is a basic way to serve the customers who are already standing in front of you with a card instead of cash. Choose a provider other traders trust, set the terminal up properly, follow the same payment steps every time, and protect yourself with receipts, checks and calm handling of failures. The charges are real, but the sales you save are real too — and in a busy Nigerian market, the stall that makes payment easy is the stall customers come back to.
