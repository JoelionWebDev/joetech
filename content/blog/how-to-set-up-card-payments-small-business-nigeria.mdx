Walk into any busy shop in Lagos, Abuja or Port Harcourt and watch how customers pay. Many still carry cash, but a growing number reach for a card or ask to transfer. When your answer is "cash only", some of those customers quietly walk away — or pay once and never come back. Accepting card payments is no longer something only big supermarkets and hotels do. For a small business, it is now a basic part of trading.

This guide walks you through the whole process, step by step: choosing between a POS terminal and a payment link, comparing providers, understanding the fees, setting everything up, and handling the problems that come with card payments — failed transactions, chargebacks and fraud. Everything here is written for a Nigerian small business owner working with naira amounts and real local conditions.

## Why Card Payments Matter for a Small Business

The strongest reason to accept cards is simple: customers spend more easily when paying is easy. A customer who has to find an ATM or a POS agent before buying from you is a customer who may change their mind on the way. When the payment happens at your counter in seconds, the sale closes while the customer is still excited about the product.

There are other benefits too. Card payments create a record of every sale, which makes it much easier to know how much you actually made in a day, a week or a month. Cash can be miscounted, forgotten or lost; a card settlement lands in your account with a statement you can check.

## Option 1: A POS Terminal

A POS (point of sale) terminal is the small device you see on shop counters — the customer inserts or taps their card, enters a PIN, and the payment goes through. For a physical shop, market stall, salon, pharmacy or restaurant, a POS terminal is usually the first thing to set up.

### Getting a POS from a Bank

Most Nigerian banks offer POS terminals to their business account holders. The typical process looks like this:

1. Open or hold a business current account with the bank.
2. Visit the branch or apply through the bank's business banking channel and request a POS terminal.
3. Provide your business documents — usually your CAC registration, a valid ID, a utility bill or proof of address, and sometimes a passport photograph.
4. The bank reviews the application and, if approved, deploys the terminal to your shop.
5. A bank representative or agent shows you and your staff how to use it.

Bank POS terminals are generally reliable, and settlement — the money landing in your account — is usually within one business day. The downside is that the process can be slow. Approval and deployment can take days or weeks, and some banks ask for a minimum account balance or a history of transactions before they approve a terminal.

### Getting a POS from a Fintech

Fintech companies such as Opay, PalmPay and Moniepoint have changed the game for small businesses. Their POS terminals are usually cheaper to get, faster to deploy, and designed for exactly the kind of small shop that banks sometimes ignore.

With a fintech, the process is typically:

1. Download the agent or business app and create an account.
2. Complete identity verification with your BVN, a valid ID and a photo.
3. Request a POS terminal through the app, or buy one from an accredited agent in your area.
4. Fund the wallet or account linked to the terminal as required.
5. Start accepting payments, often the same day.

Many of these terminals also work as a mini business account: you can receive transfers, pay bills and check your balance from the same app.

### What a POS Terminal Costs

Costs vary by provider and change over time, so treat any figure as a guide and confirm the current terms before you commit. The main costs are:

- **Terminal purchase or deposit:** Fintech terminals are often sold outright for a one-off price, while banks may give you the device free or against a refundable deposit. Ask clearly whether the money is a purchase price, a deposit you get back, or a rental fee.
- **Transaction charges:** A small percentage of each transaction, often capped at a maximum amount per transaction. This is the cost that adds up, so compare it carefully across providers.
- **Daily or monthly charges:** Some providers charge a small daily fee or a maintenance fee. Others charge nothing beyond the transaction fee. Read the terms.
- **Settlement time:** Some providers settle instantly or within hours; others take until the next business day. If your business depends on quick access to cash, this matters.

Before choosing, ask each provider the same questions and write the answers down side by side: What is the charge per transaction? Is there a cap? How long does settlement take? Is there a daily or monthly fee? What happens if the terminal develops a fault, and who replaces it?

## Option 2: Payment Links and Online Checkout

Not every sale happens at a counter. If you sell on WhatsApp, Instagram, or through a website, you need a way for customers to pay you without being physically present. That is where payment links and online checkout come in.

A payment link is a unique web link you send to a customer. They open it, enter their card details, and pay. The money lands in your account, and you get a notification. Paystack and Flutterwave are the two best-known providers in Nigeria, and both offer payment links alongside full checkout integration for websites.

Setting up a payment link is straightforward:

1. Create a business account with the provider and complete verification. You will usually need your CAC documents, a valid ID, your BVN and a bank account in the business name.
2. Once verified, generate a payment link for a product, a service, or a fixed amount.
3. Share the link with the customer on WhatsApp, in a DM, or by email.
4. The customer pays with their card or bank transfer through the link.
5. You receive a confirmation and the money is settled to your bank account, usually within one business day.

Payment links are excellent for businesses that take orders remotely: caterers in Abuja taking event bookings, fashion vendors in Lagos selling through Instagram, or a Port Harcourt electronics dealer confirming an order before delivery. They also work well for deposits — a customer can pay a deposit to secure a booking without visiting your shop.

## Option 3: Transfers and QR Codes as a Backup

Card payments should be your main electronic option, but transfers are worth keeping as a backup. Some customers prefer to transfer directly to your business account, and on days when a terminal has network problems, a transfer can save a sale.

You can make transfers easier by printing your account details clearly at the counter, or by generating a QR code that customers scan to pay. Always confirm a transfer before handing over goods — check your own banking app, not a screenshot the customer shows you, because screenshots can be faked.

## Choosing the Right Provider for Your Business

There is no single best provider. The right choice depends on how your business actually trades. Work through these questions before you decide:

- **Where do your customers pay?** If most sales happen in your shop, a POS terminal is the priority. If most orders come through WhatsApp or social media, payment links matter more. Many businesses end up needing both.
- **How much do you sell per transaction?** Transaction fees are often a percentage with a cap, so the fee structure that suits a shop selling items of ₦2,000 is different from one selling items of ₦150,000. Run your real numbers through each provider's charges.
- **How fast do you need the money?** If you restock daily from your sales, fast settlement is critical. Ask each provider exactly when the money reaches your account.
- **How good is the network in your area?** Some terminals perform better on weak networks than others. Ask other shop owners in your area which terminal works reliably there.
- **What support do you get?** When a terminal stops working on a Saturday afternoon, you need help fast. Check whether the provider has agents nearby, a responsive support line, or a walk-in office you can reach.

A practical approach for many small businesses is to start with one provider, use it for a month, and track the real costs and problems. If it works well, stay. If the fees are eating your margin or the terminal keeps failing, switch.

## Setting Up Your POS Terminal: A Step-by-Step Checklist

Once you have chosen a provider, the setup itself is simple. Use this checklist:

1. **Complete verification.** Have your CAC certificate, valid ID, BVN, passport photograph and proof of address ready before you start. Delays almost always come from missing documents.
2. **Set up the business account.** Make sure the account receiving settlements is a business account in your registered business name, not a personal account. Mixing business money into a personal account makes record-keeping messy and can cause problems later.
3. **Configure the terminal.** Set your business name so it appears correctly on receipts. Test the terminal with a small transaction of your own before the first real customer.
4. **Train your staff.** Everyone who handles payments should know how to run a sale, print or reprint a receipt, check a transaction status, and handle a failed payment. Write the steps on a card and keep it beside the terminal.
5. **Put up clear signage.** A small sign saying "We accept card payments" or the card logos at your entrance tells customers before they even ask. It costs almost nothing and wins sales.
6. **Keep receipts and records.** Keep a copy of every transaction report, and reconcile daily. This takes five minutes and saves hours of confusion later.

## How to Handle Failed Transactions

Failed transactions are the most stressful part of card payments, and they will happen. A customer pays, the money leaves their account, but your terminal shows "declined" or prints no receipt. Here is how to handle it calmly and correctly:

- **Stay calm and do not argue.** The customer is as frustrated as you are. A calm response protects your reputation.
- **Do not hand over the goods.** If the transaction did not complete on your side, the sale has not gone through. Politely explain that the payment did not go through and offer to try again.
- **Try once more.** Network glitches are common. A second attempt often succeeds. If it fails again, suggest another card or a bank transfer.
- **Check your terminal and app.** Look at the transaction history on the terminal or in the provider's app. Sometimes a transaction that looks failed actually succeeded.
- **Keep a record.** Note the date, time, amount, the last four digits of the card if visible on the receipt, and the reference number. This is essential if the customer later complains to their bank.
- **Tell the customer what happens next.** If money left their account but your terminal declined the payment, explain that the bank usually reverses such debits automatically, typically within a few working days. Advise them to contact their own bank if the money is not returned.

The golden rule: never refund cash on the spot for a failed card transaction. A refund must go through the proper reversal process, otherwise you can end up refunding money you never actually received.

## Understanding Chargebacks

A chargeback is when a customer asks their bank to reverse a card payment, and the bank takes the money back from your account. It is one of the biggest risks of accepting cards, so it is worth understanding.

You cannot prevent chargebacks entirely, but you can reduce them sharply:

- **Always give a receipt.** A printed or digital receipt with your business name, the amount, the date and a description of what was sold is your first line of defence.
- **Keep delivery records.** If you deliver goods, keep a signed delivery note or a message from the customer confirming receipt. For online orders, keep the chat where the customer confirmed the order and the delivery.
- **Describe products accurately.** Most disputes start with a customer feeling they got something different from what they expected. Clear descriptions and photos that match the real product prevent this.
- **Respond quickly.** If a chargeback is raised, your provider will ask for evidence. Respond within the deadline with your receipts and records. Businesses that ignore chargeback notices almost always lose.

## Protecting Your Business from Fraud

Card fraud is real, and small businesses are targets precisely because fraudsters assume they have weaker checks. A few habits will protect you:

- **Never let the card leave the customer's sight.** The terminal should come to the customer, or the customer should come to the terminal. A card taken to a back room is a card that can be copied.
- **Watch for unusual behaviour.** A customer in a hurry, buying high-value items in bulk, using several different cards that keep declining, or refusing a receipt — any of these is worth a pause.
- **Keep your terminal secure.** Do not share your terminal PIN or your provider app password with staff who do not need it. Change passwords regularly, and log out of the provider app on any phone that is lost or given to someone else.

## Reconciling Your Payments Every Day

Reconciliation sounds technical, but it is simply checking that the money you should have received actually arrived. Doing it daily takes a few minutes and catches problems while they are still easy to fix.

At the end of each trading day:

1. Print or open the terminal's daily settlement report.
2. Add up the card payments for the day and compare the total with what was settled into your account.
3. Check for any transactions marked as failed or pending, and follow up on them.
4. Compare the day's card total with your sales record or receipt book. If they do not match, find out why before the next day starts.
5. Keep the report. File it by date, either in a folder or in a simple spreadsheet.

This habit does more than catch errors. It gives you a clear daily picture of your business, and it means that if a customer ever disputes a payment weeks later, you can find the record in minutes.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Charging customers extra for paying by card.** Adding a surcharge for card payments annoys customers and can breach your agreement with the provider. Build the cost of card fees into your prices instead.
- **Using a personal account for business settlements.** It makes your records messy and can cause problems with your bank and your provider. Keep business money in a business account from day one.
- **Ignoring the fees.** A fee that looks small on one transaction adds up across hundreds of sales. Review your total fees every month and compare them with what other providers would have charged.
- **Having no backup.** When your only terminal loses network on a busy day, every card customer becomes a problem. Keep a second option ready — a second terminal, payment links, or at minimum the ability to accept transfers.
- **Skipping staff training.** Your staff handle most payments. If they do not know what to do when a transaction fails, the customer suffers and your business takes the blame.

## Getting Started This Week

You do not need to do everything at once. Here is a simple plan for the next seven days:

- **Day 1–2:** Compare two or three providers. Ask each one for their full fee list, settlement time and terminal cost, and write the answers down.
- **Day 3:** Choose your provider and submit your application with all documents ready.
- **Day 4–5:** While the terminal is being processed, set up a payment link account as well, so you can accept remote payments immediately.
- **Day 6:** When the terminal arrives, test it, train your staff, and put up your card-acceptance signage.
- **Day 7:** Start accepting cards, and reconcile your first day's payments that evening.

Within a week, your business can be taking card payments. The customers who have been asking "do you accept transfer or card?" will finally get the answer they want, and the sales you were losing to cash-only friction will start coming through.
