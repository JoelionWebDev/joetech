If you sell anything in Nigeria, WhatsApp is probably your shop, your showroom, and your cashier all in one. Millions of Nigerians open WhatsApp before they open any other app in the morning. A single broadcast can reach hundreds of customers in seconds, and it costs nothing to send. Yet most broadcasts are ignored. They are opened, glanced at, and forgotten, or worse, the sender is muted or blocked.

The difference between a broadcast that sells and one that is ignored is not luck. It is how the message is written, who receives it, and when it arrives. A short, honest message sent to the right people at the right time can bring in orders the same day. A long, shouty message sent to everyone you know will only train people to skip your name.

This guide shows you how to write WhatsApp broadcasts people actually read in Nigeria, from building a clean list to writing the first line, choosing pictures, and knowing how often to send.

## Why Most Broadcasts Are Ignored

Before you write better broadcasts, you need to understand why most of them fail. The reasons are simple, and you have probably done some of them yourself.

First, the list is wrong. Many sellers add every contact on their phone to a broadcast list, including people who never bought anything and never asked to hear from them. Those people did not choose to receive your messages, so they treat them as spam. A broadcast sent to 500 unwilling people performs worse than a broadcast sent to 100 willing customers.

Second, the message is too long. A broadcast that fills the whole screen before the reader reaches the point is a broadcast that will not be read. People scroll fast. If the first two lines do not tell them why this message matters, they move on. Long paragraphs, long lists of products, and long prayers at the start all push the reader away.

Third, the message is all about the seller. "Good morning my people, we have new stock, come and buy, God bless you" says nothing about what the customer gains. People read messages that solve their problem or offer them something useful. They skip messages that only announce that you have goods.

Fourth, the broadcast comes too often. A seller who broadcasts three times a day quickly becomes background noise. Contacts mute the chat, or they block the number entirely. Once you are blocked, you lose that customer for good.

The good news is that every one of these problems is fixable, and fixing them costs nothing.

## Broadcast Lists Are Not Groups

Many Nigerian sellers confuse broadcast lists with WhatsApp groups, and the confusion hurts their sales.

A broadcast list sends your message to each person privately. Each recipient sees it as a personal message from you, and their replies come to you alone. Other recipients never see who else got the message, and they never see each other's replies. That privacy is powerful. A customer is far more likely to ask about a price or negotiate in a private chat than in front of two hundred strangers.

A group, by contrast, puts everyone in one room. Every reply is visible to all. Many customers stay silent in groups because they do not want others to see them asking questions or buying. Groups also create noise: one person's question becomes fifty notifications for everyone else. For selling, a broadcast list is almost always better than a group.

There is one important rule about broadcast lists that catches many sellers out. A person only receives your broadcast if they have your number saved in their phone. If they have not saved your number, your message will never reach them, no matter how good it is. This is why you should always ask customers to save your number, and give them a reason to do it: "Save my number so you never miss our Friday deals."

WhatsApp Business makes this easier. You can create labels for your contacts, such as "New customer", "Repeat buyer", or "Lagos delivery", and build separate broadcast lists for each group. A message about a new Ankara fabric collection goes to people who buy fabric, not to people who only ever bought shoes. That targeting is what makes broadcasts feel personal instead of noisy.

## Build a Clean List Before You Write a Word

Your broadcast list is your audience, and a good list is worth more than a big one. Start by cleaning up who is on it.

Keep only people who have bought from you, asked about your products, or given you their number at your shop or stall. These are warm contacts. They know who you are, and they have a reason to hear from you. Remove people who have never replied, never bought, and never asked to be added. A smaller list of interested people will always outperform a large list of strangers.

Ask new customers for permission. When someone buys from you, say, "Can I add you to my broadcast list? I send new arrivals and deals twice a week." Most will say yes, because you asked. You can also put a small sign at your shop: "Send 'ADD ME' to 0803 000 0000 for weekly deals." The people who send that message are your best audience, because they chose to join.

Sort your list into groups that make sense for your business. A food vendor might have "Lunch regulars", "Event catering", and "Weekend orders". A fashion seller might have "Ladies wear", "Men's wear", and "Wholesale buyers". Sending the right message to the right group means fewer messages overall, and each one feels more relevant.

Finally, respect people who opt out. If someone asks you to stop sending broadcasts, remove them immediately and say thank you. A person who leaves quietly may come back later. A person you keep messaging after they asked you to stop will block you and tell their friends to do the same.

## The First Two Lines Decide Everything

On WhatsApp, a broadcast appears in the chat list with only the first line or two visible. That preview is your headline. If it is boring, the message is never opened.

Open with the benefit, not a greeting. Compare these two openings. "Good morning my wonderful customers, how are you all doing today? I hope you are all doing well and your families too. I want to use this opportunity to inform you that we have new stock..." By the third line, most readers have left. Now compare: "New Ankara just landed — ₦8,500 per 6 yards, only 12 pieces left." The reader knows what it is, what it costs, and why they should hurry, all in one line.

Put the most important words first. Prices, deadlines, and new arrivals belong at the top. Greetings, prayers, and introductions belong at the bottom, if at all. Your customers already know who you are. They do not need three lines of greeting before the news.

Ask a question when it fits. "Do you still need a Christmas outfit?" or "Who needs data at a good price this weekend?" pulls the reader in because it speaks to them directly. A question also invites a reply, and a reply starts a conversation, which is where sales happen.

Write the way you talk. The best broadcasts sound like a message from a real person, not a radio advert. Short sentences. Simple words. A little warmth. Avoid capital letters for whole sentences, avoid too many emojis, and avoid words like "URGENT" and "LAST CHANCE" in every message. When everything is urgent, nothing is.

## A Simple Structure for Every Broadcast

You do not need to be a writer to send a good broadcast. You need a structure, and you can use the same one every time.

Start with the hook, one line that gives the news or the benefit. "Fresh catfish available today — ₦3,500 per kilo." "Weekend deal: buy 2 get 1 free on all body creams."

Follow with two or three short lines of useful detail. The sizes, the colours, the flavours, the location, the delivery fee. Only the details a buyer needs to decide. "Available in red, blue and black. Sizes M to XXL. Delivery in Lekki ₦1,500, mainland ₦2,500."

Then give the price clearly. Never hide the price in a broadcast. When the price is missing, most readers will not ask; they will simply move on, because they assume it is expensive or that the seller wants to bargain. State the price, and if there is a discount, show the old price next to the new one: "Was ₦25,000, now ₦19,500."

Then tell them exactly what to do. "Reply 'I want one' and I will send you the payment details." "Send a DM with your size." "Tap here to order." One clear instruction beats three vague ones. If you take orders on WhatsApp, say so. If payment is by transfer, say which bank and account name in the same message so the customer does not have to ask.

End with a deadline when there is one. "Offer ends Sunday 9pm." "Only 10 pieces left." A real deadline creates urgency. A fake deadline repeated every week destroys trust, so only use one when it is true.

Keep the whole message under about eight lines. If it is longer, cut something. Every line must earn its place.

## Pictures and Videos That Make People Stop

A broadcast with a good picture gets opened more often than a plain text message. But a bad picture can do more harm than no picture at all.

Use your own photos. Customers in Nigeria have learned to distrust pictures downloaded from the internet, because they have been disappointed before: the item in the picture was not the item that arrived. Take a clear photo of the actual product you are selling, in good light, against a clean background. A simple photo taken by your shop window beats a glossy picture of a product you do not have.

Show the product in use when you can. A dress on a hanger tells a customer less than a dress worn by a real person. A plate of food on a table sells better than a list of ingredients. A short video of you opening a new carton or packaging an order builds trust, because it shows the business is real.

Keep it to one picture or one short video per broadcast. Five pictures in one message look like a catalogue, and most people will not scroll through them. One strong image, with the details in the text, is enough. If you have many products, send them across different days instead of all at once.

## Timing: When Nigerians Actually Read Broadcasts

When you send matters almost as much as what you send. A broadcast sent at the wrong hour is buried under a hundred other chats by the time the customer looks at their phone.

The best times for most Nigerian businesses are early morning, between 7am and 9am, when people are checking their phones before work, and evening, between 6pm and 9pm, when they are home and relaxed. Lunch time, around 12pm to 1pm, also works for food and quick offers. Avoid the middle of the night and very early morning, because a message that wakes someone up is a message that gets you muted.

Match the time to your product. Food vendors do well in the morning, when people are planning lunch, and again in the evening for dinner orders. Fashion sellers do well in the evening and on weekends, when people have time to browse. A seller of school items does well in the weeks before resumption, not during the holidays.

Do not send on the same day and at the same hour every time if your audience is small, because people learn to expect and ignore it. Vary the day a little, but keep the frequency steady.

## How Often Is Too Often

Frequency is where most Nigerian sellers go wrong. The fear is that if you do not message often, customers will forget you. The reality is that if you message too often, customers will block you.

For most businesses, two broadcasts a week is the sweet spot. That is enough to stay in the customer's mind without becoming a nuisance. A food vendor with daily changing menus can send a short daily menu, because the content is genuinely new each day. A fashion seller with new stock twice a month should not pretend to have news every day.

Never send the same message twice to the same list. If nobody replied the first time, sending it again will not help. Change the picture, change the price, or change the offer, and try again another day.

Give people a reason to stay. Mix your messages. Not every broadcast should be a sale. Share a useful tip, a customer review, a behind-the-scenes photo, or a short "thank you" message. A seller who only ever asks for money becomes easy to ignore. A seller who sometimes gives something useful becomes someone worth following.

## Mistakes That Get You Blocked

Some habits will get you blocked faster than anything else. Avoid them all.

Do not add people to a broadcast list without their permission, especially people you have never spoken to. It feels like spam because it is spam, and WhatsApp may restrict your number if many people report you.

Do not forward the same message to many lists at once with no changes. WhatsApp labels forwarded messages, and customers can see that the message was forwarded many times. It looks careless, and it tells the customer they are one of a thousand.

Do not send voice notes as broadcasts. Most people will not listen to a three-minute voice note from a seller, especially in public or at work. Text is faster to read and easier to search later. Save voice notes for personal conversations with customers who already know you.

Do not beg or guilt-trip. Messages like "Please patronise me, things are hard" or "If you ignore this, God will judge you" may get sympathy once, but they damage your business. Customers buy from sellers who look capable and reliable, not from sellers who look desperate.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many people can I add to a WhatsApp broadcast list?

WhatsApp allows up to 256 contacts in one broadcast list. If you have more customers than that, create a second list, or split your list by group, such as Lagos customers and Abuja customers. Remember that a person only receives your broadcast if they have saved your number.

### Should I include my price in a broadcast?

Yes, almost always. Hiding the price forces every interested person to ask, which wastes their time and yours, and most people will not ask at all. A clear price filters out people who cannot afford it and brings you serious buyers. The only exception is a custom service, like catering, where the price genuinely depends on the details.

### What should I do if people keep leaving my broadcast list?

First, check your frequency and your content. Are you sending too often? Are your messages useful? Ask the people who stay what they like. Cut back to once a week for a month, send only your best offers, and watch whether replies recover. Never add back someone who asked to leave.

## What to Do Next

1. Open your broadcast lists today and remove anyone who has never bought from you, never asked about a product, or never replied. A smaller, warmer list will sell more than a big, cold one.
2. Write your next broadcast using the structure in this guide: a one-line hook with the price, two lines of useful detail, and one clear instruction on how to order. Keep it under eight lines.
3. Send it at 7am or 7pm, then write down how many people replied and how many paid. Use that number to make the next broadcast better.
