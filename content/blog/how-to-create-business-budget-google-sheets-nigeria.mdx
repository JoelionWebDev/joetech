Most Nigerian small businesses do not fail because the owner lacks customers. They fail because nobody knows where the money goes. A fashion designer in Lekki sells ten dresses in a week and still cannot pay rent. A food vendor in Surulere makes ₦80,000 on a Saturday and by Tuesday there is nothing left for market runs. A phone accessories seller in Ikeja moves stock every day but cannot say whether the business made a profit last month.

The problem is rarely effort. It is the absence of a budget. A budget is simply a plan that says how much money you expect to come in, how much you plan to spend, and what should remain. When you write it down and check it weekly, you stop guessing. Google Sheets is the best tool for this job for most Nigerian SMEs: it is free, it works on a phone, it saves automatically, and you can share it with your accountant or business partner without sending files back and forth.

This guide shows you, step by step, how to build a simple business budget in Google Sheets that fits a Nigerian reality — cash and transfers mixed together, POS charges, delivery costs, fuel for the generator, data subscriptions, staff wages, and the months when sales slow down. You do not need accounting training. You need one hour, your last three months of bank statements, and the willingness to be honest about your spending.

## Why a Budget Matters More Than a Sales Target

Many business owners set a sales target — "I want to make ₦500,000 this month" — and stop there. Sales alone tell you nothing about survival. If you sell ₦500,000 worth of goods that cost you ₦420,000 to buy, plus ₦60,000 in rent share, ₦25,000 in transport and ₦15,000 in data and airtime, you have lost money while celebrating sales.

A budget protects you in five specific ways. First, it shows your real profit, not your bank balance. A bank balance can look healthy because a customer paid for next month's order in advance. Second, it forces you to separate business money from personal money, which is the single biggest leak in most Nigerian small businesses. Third, it helps you plan for slow months. December is busy for many traders; January is often slow. A budget lets you save in December for January. Fourth, it makes borrowing decisions safer. Before you take a loan or join a cooperative contribution, you can see whether the monthly repayment fits. Fifth, it gives you numbers to show a bank, an investor or a grant programme when they ask for your records.

You do not need a complicated system. A one-page monthly budget, checked every Friday, will change how you run your business within two months.

## What You Need Before You Start

Gather these before you open Google Sheets. The whole exercise takes about an hour if the numbers are in front of you.

1. Your bank statements for the last three months. Download them from your bank app as PDF or check the transaction history. Include your business account and, if you still mix money, your personal account transactions that relate to the business.
2. Your POS and transfer records. Check your Paystack, Flutterwave, Moniepoint or Opay dashboard for the last three months. Note the charges deducted on each transaction, because those charges are a real expense.
3. Your sales records. Your WhatsApp chats, invoice book, POS receipts, Jumia or Konga seller dashboard, or the notebook where you write daily sales. Even rough figures are better than none.
4. Your fixed costs. Rent, staff salaries, shop levy, internet subscription, loan repayments, insurance, and any monthly software subscription. Write the exact amount and the date each one is due.
5. Your variable costs. Stock purchases, transport, fuel, packaging, airtime, data, printing, and market runs. These change every month, so take the average of the last three months.
6. A Google account. Sheets is free with any Gmail account, and it works in the Chrome browser on Android and iPhone, as well as on a laptop.

## Step 1: Create Your Spreadsheet and Set Up the Structure

Open sheets.google.com, click the blank spreadsheet, and rename it. A clear name helps you find it later: "My Business Budget 2026" works. If you run more than one business, add the business name, for example "Adaeze Hair Studio Budget 2026".

Create four tabs at the bottom of the screen. Click the plus sign to add a tab and double-click the tab name to rename it. The four tabs are:

- **Budget** — the monthly plan: expected income, planned expenses, and expected profit.
- **Actual** — what really happened: real income and real expenses for the month.
- **Cash Flow** — money in and money out, week by week, so you can see when cash will be tight.
- **Dashboard** — a simple summary with your total income, total expenses, profit, and your biggest expense categories.

Set the currency format before you type numbers. Select the columns where money will go, click Format, then Number, then Custom currency, and choose Nigerian naira (₦). This avoids confusion later when you are scanning figures quickly on your phone.

Freeze the top row so your headings stay visible when you scroll. Click View, then Freeze, then 1 row. On a phone, tap the row number, then the three dots, then Freeze.

## Step 2: List Every Source of Income

On the Budget tab, start with income. In column A, list every way money enters your business. Be specific. "Sales" is too vague. Break it down so you can see which part of the business is carrying the rest.

A typical Nigerian SME might list:

- Walk-in sales
- WhatsApp and Instagram orders
- Jumia or Konga sales
- Wholesale orders to shops
- Delivery or service fees charged to customers
- Training, consultation or repair fees
- Interest or small side income directly tied to the business

Put the expected amount for the month next to each source in column B. Base the expectation on your average of the last three months, not on your best month. If you sold ₦450,000 in December, ₦280,000 in January and ₦320,000 in February, your planning figure should be around ₦350,000, not ₦450,000. Hope is not a budgeting method.

Add a total row at the bottom of the income list using the SUM formula. In the cell under your last income figure, type `=SUM(B2:B10)` and adjust the range to cover your actual rows. Sheets will add the figures automatically, and the total will update whenever you change a number.

## Step 3: List Every Expense, Fixed and Variable

Expenses are where most budgets fail, because owners forget the small, regular costs. List everything, even the ₦2,000 airtime top-up and the ₦5,000 nylon bags. Small leaks sink a business slowly.

Start with fixed expenses — costs that stay roughly the same every month:

- Rent, divided by twelve if you pay yearly. If your shop rent is ₦600,000 per year, budget ₦50,000 per month, and move that ₦50,000 into a separate savings account each month so the rent is ready when it falls due.
- Staff salaries and wages, including your own salary. Yes, pay yourself a fixed salary, even if it is ₦50,000 or ₦80,000. If you take money whenever you feel like it, you will never know your profit.
- Internet subscription, whether fibre, Starlink, or a monthly MTN or Airtel data plan for the business phone.
- Loan or cooperative repayments.
- Insurance, shop levy, local government fees, and association dues.
- Subscriptions: accounting software, Canva, your website hosting, or your POS terminal monthly charge.

Then list variable expenses — costs that rise and fall with sales:

- Cost of goods or raw materials. This is usually your biggest line. For a trader, it is stock. For a baker, flour, sugar, butter, gas and packaging. For a tailor, fabric, lining, thread, zips and buttons.
- Transport and delivery: fuel, bike dispatch, Bolt or Uber for deliveries, bus fares for market runs.
- Packaging: nylon bags, boxes, branded stickers, thank-you cards.
- POS and transfer charges: Paystack takes a percentage, banks charge for transfers, POS terminals charge per transaction. Add them up from last month's statements and budget a similar figure.
- Airtime and data for the business line.
- Generator fuel and maintenance, or the share of NEPA bills for the shop.
- Marketing: WhatsApp Business ads, Instagram boosted posts, flyers, or a small influencer fee.
- Repairs, cleaning, and miscellaneous. Always include a miscellaneous line of at least ₦10,000 to ₦20,000. Something unexpected always happens — a blender breaks, a customer returns an item, a signboard falls.

Put each expense in column A and the planned amount in column B, just like income. Add a SUM total at the bottom. Then, in a separate cell, subtract total expenses from total income with a formula like `=B15-B30`, adjusted to your rows. That cell is your expected profit. If it is negative, your budget is telling you the truth early: either raise prices, cut a cost, or increase sales before the month starts, not after the money is gone.

## Step 4: Build the Cash Flow Tab

Profit and cash are not the same thing. You can be profitable on paper and still be unable to pay your supplier on Friday because a big customer has not paid you yet. The Cash Flow tab fixes that.

Divide the month into four weeks. For each week, list the money you expect to receive and the money you must pay. Pay attention to timing. Rent may fall due in week one. Salaries fall due at month end. A supplier may need payment before they release stock. A corporate client may pay thirty days after delivery.

A simple weekly cash flow table looks like this: Week 1 opening balance, plus expected receipts, minus expected payments, equals closing balance. That closing balance becomes Week 2's opening balance, and so on. Use formulas so the numbers flow automatically. If any week's closing balance goes negative, you have found a cash problem before it happens. You can then move a payment, chase a debtor, delay a stock purchase, or arrange a short overdraft in advance instead of panicking on the day.

In Nigeria, this matters more than in many other countries because payment timing is uneven. Customers pay in cash, by transfer, on POS, and sometimes in instalments. Suppliers in Balogun, Onitsha Main Market or Computer Village often want cash or transfer before dispatch. A cash flow plan keeps you from promising a delivery you cannot fund.

Update the Cash Flow tab every Friday with real receipts and payments. It takes ten minutes and it is the single most valuable habit in this whole guide.

## Step 5: Record Actual Income and Expenses Daily

A budget only works if the actual figures go in. Set a daily routine that takes five minutes. Every evening, open the Actual tab on your phone and record the day's sales and expenses. Do not wait until month end. By then you will have forgotten half the small expenses, and the budget will look better than reality.

Record sales by source, using the same categories as your Budget tab. Record expenses the same day you spend the money. Keep receipts in a small envelope or snap a photo and save it in a Google Drive folder named after the month. If you use Moniepoint, Opay or your bank app, your transaction history is a backup record, but do not rely on memory alone.

Use data validation to keep entries tidy. Select the category column, click Data, then Data validation, and set a dropdown list of your income and expense categories. This stops spelling variations like "Transport", "transport" and "TP" from splitting one expense into three lines on your summary.

## Step 6: Compare Budget Against Actual Every Month End

At month end, sit down for thirty minutes and compare the two tabs. For each line, calculate the difference between what you planned and what happened. Sheets can do this with a simple subtraction formula, or you can add a Variance column on the Actual tab that pulls the budget figure and subtracts the actual figure.

Ask three questions about every large difference:

1. Was the plan wrong, or did my behaviour change? If you budgeted ₦150,000 for stock but spent ₦230,000 because sales were higher, that is good news. If you spent ₦230,000 because prices rose, you need to review your selling prices.
2. Is this a one-off or a pattern? A generator repair is a one-off. Rising delivery costs for three months in a row is a pattern that needs a new delivery arrangement.
3. What will I change next month? Write the answer in a Notes column. "Reduce market trips from four to two per week" or "Increase delivery fee from ₦1,500 to ₦2,000" are decisions, not wishes.

Then build next month's budget from this month's actual figures, not from the old plan. If transport really costs ₦65,000 a month, budget ₦65,000, not the ₦40,000 you hoped for. Budgets get more accurate every month because reality teaches you. After three months, your budget will be close enough to trust for real decisions.

## Step 7: Add a Simple Dashboard

A dashboard is a one-screen summary you can check in ten seconds. On the Dashboard tab, pull the totals from your other tabs with formulas. Show total income, total expenses, profit, profit margin as a percentage, and your top five expense categories.

To calculate profit margin, divide profit by income and format the result as a percentage. If you made ₦400,000 in sales and ₦80,000 in profit, your margin is 20 percent. That number tells you how much room you have. A business with a 5 percent margin is fragile — one price rise from a supplier can wipe it out. A business with a 30 percent margin can afford to run a promotion or absorb a slow week.

## Common Budgeting Mistakes Nigerian SMEs Make

The first mistake is mixing personal and business money. When school fees, family food and business stock all come from the same account, no budget can work. Open a separate business account — Moniepoint, Opay, Kuda, or a traditional bank business account — and pay yourself a fixed salary into your personal account. Everything else stays in the business.

The second mistake is forgetting irregular costs. Rent paid yearly, a generator serviced twice a year, a business name renewal, or a festive-season stock build-up are all real costs. Divide yearly costs by twelve and budget them monthly so they never arrive as a shock.

The third mistake is under-pricing. Many owners set prices by copying a competitor without knowing their own costs. If your budget shows that a dress costs ₦18,000 to make, including fabric, labour share, packaging and delivery, and you sell it for ₦20,000, your margin is too thin. Your budget gives you the evidence to raise prices with confidence.

The fourth mistake is treating a loan as income. A ₦500,000 loan feels like a windfall on the day it lands. It is a liability with monthly repayments. Record the loan separately, budget the repayment as a fixed expense, and spend the principal only on what it was borrowed for.

The fifth mistake is building the budget and never opening it again. A budget is a living document. Check it every Friday for ten minutes. That single habit separates businesses that survive from businesses that drift.

## How to Keep the Budget Going After Month One

The first month is the hardest because you are building the habit. After that, the routine is short. Every evening, record the day's sales and spending. Every Friday, check cash flow and chase any unpaid invoices. At month end, compare budget against actual, write down three lessons, and build next month's budget in twenty minutes.

A budget will not make customers appear. It will make sure that when they do, the money stays, grows, and funds the next stage of your business. Open Google Sheets today, build the four tabs, fill in last month's real figures, and give yourself the one tool every serious Nigerian SME needs: a clear view of its own money.
