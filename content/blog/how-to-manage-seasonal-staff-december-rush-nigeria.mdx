## Why December Demands Extra Hands

For most Nigerian businesses, December is unlike any other month. Salaries and bonuses land, people travel home with money to spend, weddings and parties fill the calendar, and customers buy gifts, food, clothes and decorations in volumes that dwarf a normal month. A shop in Lekki that serves twenty customers a day in October can serve sixty a day in mid-December. A food business in Abuja can double its orders. An online vendor in Port Harcourt can wake up to more orders than one person can pack before noon.

This is good news, but only if you can handle it. A business that cannot serve its December customers loses sales to competitors, earns bad reviews, and damages relationships that took all year to build. The answer is seasonal staff — temporary workers hired for the rush, usually from late November through early January.

Seasonal hiring is common in Nigerian retail, food, logistics, fashion and e-commerce. The businesses that do it well plan early, hire carefully, train quickly, and manage firmly. The ones that do it badly hire in a panic on the 15th of December, hand a stranger the keys to the shop, and spend January fixing the damage. This guide will help you do it well.

## Start Planning in October, Not December

The single biggest advantage in seasonal hiring is time. Businesses that start planning in October choose from the best available workers. Businesses that start in mid-December take whoever is left.

In October, sit down and answer three questions. First, how much busier will December be? Look at last December's sales if you have them. If you sold twice as much as a normal month, you will need roughly twice the hands. If this is your first December, ask similar businesses what to expect, and plan conservatively — it is better to hire one extra person than to turn customers away.

Second, what exactly do you need people to do? Write down the roles. A retail shop might need a sales assistant and a cashier. A food business might need a cook and a delivery rider. An online business might need someone to pack orders and reply to WhatsApp messages. Each role needs different skills, and you cannot hire well until you know what each role involves.

Third, what can you afford to pay? Seasonal wages in Nigeria vary by city, role and hours, but you should budget for daily or weekly pay that is fair and competitive. A sales assistant in Lagos might earn ₦3,000 to ₦5,000 a day, while a delivery rider might earn more because of transport and risk. Decide your budget before you start talking to candidates, so you do not promise what you cannot pay.

Once you have the answers, set your hiring dates. Aim to have seasonal staff hired and trained by the last week of November, so they are ready when the rush begins in earnest. Staff hired on the 20th of December will still be learning while your busiest days pass them by.

## Where to Find Good Seasonal Workers

The best seasonal workers often come through people you already trust. Ask your current staff if they know someone reliable — a sibling, a friend, a former colleague. Referrals work well because your staff member's reputation is on the line, and they will not recommend someone who will embarrass them. Offer a small bonus to staff whose referrals work out well for the full season.

Your local community is another strong source. Churches, mosques, community groups and neighbourhood associations often know young people looking for December work. A notice on a community WhatsApp group can bring several candidates in a day. Students on holiday from university or polytechnic are a natural fit: they are available, they learn quickly, and many of them want December income.

Social media works too. A clear post on Instagram, Facebook or your WhatsApp status describing the role, the location, the hours and the pay will attract applicants. Be specific in the post. "Sales assistant needed in Lekki, 9am to 6pm, ₦4,000 daily, December only" will attract serious people. A vague post attracts time-wasters.

Job platforms and staffing agencies are an option for larger needs, but for a small business hiring two or three people, personal networks and social media are usually faster and cheaper. Whatever channel you use, always meet the person face to face before hiring them, even if it is just a short chat at your shop.

## How to Interview and Choose Quickly

Seasonal hiring needs speed, but speed is not an excuse for carelessness. A short, focused interview of fifteen minutes is enough to judge most seasonal roles.

Start with availability. Can they work the dates you need, including weekends and the days just before Christmas? Many people want December work but also want to travel home for Christmas. Find out early. If someone must travel on the 22nd of December, they are not the right hire for a business whose peak is the 23rd.

Next, check the basics for the role. A sales assistant should be friendly, presentable and able to communicate clearly. A delivery rider should know the area, have a reliable bike or vehicle, and hold a valid licence. A cook should have experience with the food you sell. Ask for a short demonstration or a practical question rather than relying on claims. Ask a sales candidate how they would handle a customer who says your price is too high. Their answer tells you more than their CV does.

Check references, even for short-term work. A phone call to a former employer or a teacher takes five minutes and can save you weeks of trouble. Ask one question: would you hire this person again? The hesitation in the answer is often as informative as the words.

Finally, be honest about the terms. Tell them the pay, the hours, the start and end dates, and what happens if they do not show up. People who accept clear terms are far less likely to cause problems than people who were promised one thing and given another. Put the agreement in writing, even if it is just a short note on paper or a WhatsApp message they confirm. A written agreement protects both sides.

## Set a Simple Roster and Schedule

Before the season starts, write a roster that shows who works on which days and at what times. Share it with every seasonal worker in advance, on paper or in a WhatsApp group, so nobody is surprised by a shift. A clear roster prevents the most common December argument: two people thinking they are off on the same busy Saturday.

Plan your roster around your busiest days. In most Nigerian businesses, Fridays, Saturdays and the days just before Christmas are the peak. Put your strongest staff on those days, and make sure at least one experienced person is on duty at all times. Avoid rostering all your seasonal staff on the same day and leaving the shop to one person on another.

Build in rest. Nobody performs well working twelve days in a row, and tired staff make mistakes with money and customers. Give each worker at least one day off per week, and rotate the days off fairly so the same person is not always stuck with the Sunday shift. If someone wants a specific day off for a family event or travel, sort it out before the season starts rather than in the middle of the rush.

Keep the roster visible. Pin it on the wall in the shop, or post it in your staff WhatsApp group every Sunday evening for the coming week. When the roster changes, announce the change to everyone affected. A roster that only exists in the owner's head is not a roster — it is a source of confusion.

## Pay Seasonal Staff Fairly and Clearly

Pay is where many seasonal arrangements break down. The worker expects one thing, the owner pays another, and the last week of December ends in an argument. Prevent this by agreeing the pay structure before the first day of work.

Decide whether you pay daily, weekly or at the end of the season, and stick to it. Daily pay is common for very short engagements and for riders. Weekly pay works well for a month-long engagement, because the worker gets regular income and you get a natural checkpoint to review performance. Paying everything at the end of the season is risky — workers lose motivation, and some will leave before the end. If you must pay at the end, consider a small weekly allowance with the balance at completion.

Be clear about what the pay covers. Does it include transport? Does it include feeding? If a rider uses their own bike, is there a fuel allowance? If a staff member works beyond closing time during the rush, is there overtime pay? Answer these questions in advance, not when someone is already tired and angry at 9pm.

Pay on time, every time. Seasonal workers talk to each other, and a business known for late pay will struggle to hire next December. Paying promptly is also practical: a worker who is paid on Friday will show up happily on Saturday. A worker who is owed two weeks of pay will not.

Keep records. A simple sheet with each worker's name, days worked, daily rate, and amount paid is enough. At the end of the season, both you and the worker should be able to look at that record and agree on what was paid. If you run your regular payroll in Google Sheets, add a tab for seasonal staff so the records sit alongside your permanent staff records.

## Train Fast: The One-Day System

Seasonal staff do not need a week of training. They need a clear, focused first day that teaches them exactly what to do and how you expect it done.

Before their first day, prepare a simple list of their duties. For a sales assistant, the list might include greeting customers, showing products, operating the point-of-sale machine, handling cash, and keeping the shop tidy. For a packer, it might include checking orders against a list, packing neatly, labelling correctly, and handing parcels to the rider.

On the first day, walk them through the list yourself, or have your best permanent staff member do it. Show them, then let them do it while you watch. Correct mistakes immediately and kindly. People learn faster from doing than from listening.

Give them the rules that matter most. How do you handle a customer complaint? What do you do if a product is out of stock? When do you call the owner, and when do you decide yourself? What is the dress code? Where do they keep their phone during work hours? Clear rules prevent the small mistakes that irritate customers during a rush.

Most importantly, teach them your standard for customer service. In December, customers are stressed, in a hurry, and spending a lot of money. A calm, polite, helpful staff member can turn a stressful shopping trip into a pleasant one — and that customer will come back in January. A rude or careless staff member can lose a customer permanently. Tell your seasonal staff this plainly on day one.

## Managing the Rush Day to Day

Once the rush starts, your job as the owner changes. You become a manager, not just a seller. That means checking in, solving problems, and keeping energy up.

Start each day with a five-minute briefing. What is expected today? Are there large orders to prepare? Is any product running low? Is a particular customer coming? Five minutes of briefing prevents hours of confusion.

Be present, especially in the first week. Seasonal staff perform better when the owner is around, available and watching. That does not mean hovering over them — it means being in the shop, answering questions, and noticing problems early. If you are absent for the whole season, do not be surprised when standards slip.

Watch for theft and carelessness, but do it with systems, not suspicion. Count stock at the start and end of each day. Reconcile cash against sales records daily. Use a point-of-sale system or a simple sales book so every transaction is recorded. These habits protect honest staff as much as they catch dishonest ones, because they remove doubt.

Look after your seasonal staff during the rush. December work is tiring. Provide water and a meal or a feeding allowance. Allow short breaks. Thank people who work hard. A small gesture — a Christmas gift, a bonus for the busiest week, a kind word — costs little and buys loyalty that shows in how they treat your customers.

## Handling Problems: Absence, Theft and Poor Performance

Problems will happen. Someone will not show up on a busy Saturday. Someone will be slow. Someone may steal. What matters is how you respond.

For absence, have a backup plan. Keep the phone number of one or two extra candidates you interviewed but did not hire, and call them when someone fails to show. If a seasonal worker misses a day without notice, speak to them the next day and make the consequence clear. A second unexplained absence should end the engagement. Your permanent staff and your customers are depending on the team showing up.

For poor performance, correct it the same day. Be specific: "You left three customers waiting while you were on your phone" is useful. "You are lazy" is not. Give the person a chance to improve, but do not carry a poor performer through your busiest month. The cost of keeping them is paid by your customers.

For theft, act on evidence, not suspicion. If stock counts or cash reconciliation show a shortage, investigate calmly. Check the records, talk to the people involved, and if the evidence is clear, end the engagement immediately and involve the police if the amount justifies it. Never accuse someone publicly without proof — a false accusation destroys a person's reputation and yours.

## Ending the Season Well

The season usually ends in the first week of January, when sales return to normal. End it properly.

Pay every seasonal worker in full, on the agreed date. Thank them personally, and tell them what they did well. If someone was excellent, tell them you would like to hire them again next December, or consider them for a permanent role if one opens. Good seasonal workers are worth keeping in touch with — save their numbers in a list for next year.

Review what happened. Which roles were most useful? Did you hire too many people or too few? Were the wages right? Did training work? Write down your answers while they are fresh. That note becomes the starting point for next December's plan, and it will save you hours of thinking.

Finally, take care of yourself. December is exhausting for business owners too. Once the rush is over, rest, review your numbers, and enjoy the profit you worked for.

## Final Thoughts

The December rush is the biggest opportunity of the year for a Nigerian small business, but only for businesses that are ready for it. Seasonal staff are the bridge between the demand of December and the capacity of your normal team. Hire them early, pay them fairly, train them quickly, manage them firmly and treat them well.

Plan in October, hire in November, and manage daily through December. Do that, and the rush becomes a season of growth instead of a season of stress. Your customers get served, your staff get paid, and your business ends the year stronger than it started.
