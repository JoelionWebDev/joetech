If you sell anything online in Nigeria, fraud is not a distant risk. It is a daily part of doing business. A customer sends a screenshot of a transfer that never arrives. A stranger messages your page claiming to be a delivery rider who needs a code. Your WhatsApp Business account gets hacked and the thief starts asking your customers to pay into a different account. These things happen to small sellers in Lagos, Abuja, Port Harcourt and Onitsha every single day, and the losses are often the difference between a business that survives and one that folds.

This guide walks you through the scams Nigerian small businesses face most often, the exact steps that stop each one, and the simple systems you can set up this week with tools you already use, like WhatsApp, your bank app and Paystack or Flutterwave.

## The scams that target Nigerian small businesses

Before you can protect your business, you need to know what you are protecting it from. Here are the scams that appear again and again.

### Fake payment alerts and doctored screenshots

This is the most common scam of all. A buyer orders goods worth ₦45,000, then sends a screenshot showing a successful transfer. The money never arrives in your account. The screenshot was edited, or it was a real screenshot from a different, earlier transaction. Some scammers even send genuine-looking debit alerts generated by fake apps that mimic bank SMS messages.

A hair vendor in Ikeja told us she lost ₦120,000 worth of wigs in one month to fake alerts before she changed her process. The buyers always sounded urgent. "I need it today, my sister's wedding is tomorrow." Urgency is the scammer's favourite tool. It pushes you to dispatch before you check.

### Pay on delivery fraud

Pay on delivery sounds customer friendly, and for honest buyers it is. But it is also a favourite of fraudsters. The common pattern is simple: the buyer orders, the rider arrives, and the buyer refuses the item, claims it is damaged, or simply disappears. You lose the delivery fee both ways and the goods come back, sometimes damaged or swapped for a fake. Worse, some "buyers" use pay on delivery to get goods into their hands and then pay with a fake transfer at the door while the rider is in a hurry.

### Hacked WhatsApp and Instagram accounts

Your WhatsApp Business account is your shop. If someone takes it over, they can message all your customers, take orders, and collect payments into their own account. Hackers usually get in through a trick: they send you a message claiming to be from WhatsApp support, or they pretend to be a customer and ask you to share a six-digit code. That code is the verification code for your own account. The moment you share it, they own your number.

Instagram accounts are stolen the same way, often through fake "copyright violation" messages or phishing links that look like a login page. Once the thief controls your page, they post fake promo offers and collect money from your followers.

### Cloned pages and impersonation

A scammer copies your Instagram page, your logo, your photos and your bio, then runs adverts or messages your followers offering your products at half price. Customers pay the fake page and never receive anything. When they complain, they complain to you, and your reputation takes the damage. This happens to fashion sellers, food vendors and phone dealers more than any other group.

### Fake delivery riders

A buyer says, "My rider is coming, give him the goods." A stranger arrives, collects the package, and disappears. No payment was ever made. Or a rider you found on a random group turns out to be working with the buyer. This is common with high-value items like phones, laptops and human hair.

## Rule one: never dispatch on a screenshot

This single rule would prevent most losses for small Nigerian sellers. A screenshot is not proof of payment. Your bank balance is.

Build this process into every order:

1. The customer sends the screenshot if they like, but you do not act on it.
2. You open your bank app or your Paystack or Flutterwave dashboard and check the balance or the transaction list yourself.
3. Only when you see the money in your own account do you confirm the order and arrange dispatch.
4. If the money has not arrived after a few minutes, tell the customer politely that you will dispatch as soon as the bank confirms it. A genuine buyer understands. A scammer will argue, rush you, or disappear.

Set your bank alerts to notify you for every credit, and keep a dedicated business account separate from your personal account. Mixing the two makes it harder to confirm payments quickly and harder to track your money. Most Nigerian banks now allow you to open a business account with your CAC registration, and a separate account also makes your business look more professional to customers.

For transfers, note that interbank transfers can take a few minutes. Tell customers upfront: "Payment confirms within 10 to 15 minutes; we dispatch after confirmation." Put this in your WhatsApp catalogue, your Instagram bio link, and your order confirmation message. When the rule is public, customers accept it without argument.

## Use payment links instead of account numbers

Sending your account number to every customer creates two problems. First, anyone can send you a fake alert and claim they paid. Second, your account details spread across the internet. Payment links solve both.

Paystack and Flutterwave let you create payment links for free. The customer pays through the link with a card, transfer or USSD, and you get a dashboard record of every payment, including the customer's name, the amount, and the status. A payment either succeeded or it did not. There is no grey area and no doctored screenshot to argue about.

Payment links also protect you from chargeback-style disputes, because you have a clear record: the payment reference, the customer's details, and the delivery evidence you attach to the order. For orders above ₦50,000, insist on a payment link or a transfer you verify yourself. For regular customers who always pay by transfer, the bank-app check still applies.

If you take orders on WhatsApp, you can paste the payment link directly into the chat. If you have a website, add the payment option at checkout. The few minutes it takes to set up a payment link will save you hours of arguments and thousands of naira in losses.

## Protect your WhatsApp Business account

Your WhatsApp account is the front door of your business. Lock it properly.

Turn on two-step verification today. Open WhatsApp, go to Settings, then Account, then Two-step verification, and set a six-digit PIN that only you know. Also add an email address for recovery. This single step stops most account takeovers, because even if a thief gets your verification code, they still need your PIN.

Never share any code that arrives by SMS, no matter who asks. WhatsApp will never ask you for your code. Your bank will never ask you for your code. Anyone who asks for a code is a scammer, even if they claim to be from WhatsApp support, a delivery company, or a customer who "mistakenly" sent a code to your number. That last trick is common: the thief enters your number on their own phone, the code arrives on yours, and they ask you to read it out.

Use a strong phone lock and keep your phone's software updated. If your phone is stolen, the thief should not be able to open your WhatsApp. If you use WhatsApp on a staff member's phone, use WhatsApp Business with a dedicated business number, not a personal number, so the account belongs to the business and can be recovered if the staff member leaves.

If your account is ever hacked, act fast. Message your customers from another number or your Instagram page, warn them not to pay any new account details, and recover the account through WhatsApp's official process. Then change your PIN immediately.

## Secure your Instagram and Facebook pages

Your social media pages are your shop window. Protect them the way you would lock a physical shop at night.

Turn on two-factor authentication on Instagram and Facebook. Use an authenticator app rather than SMS if you can, because SMS codes can be intercepted. Never click links in messages warning you about copyright violations, account suspension, or verification. Instagram and Facebook do not send those warnings through direct messages. If you are worried about a warning, open the app yourself and check your notifications there.

Use a strong, unique password for each platform, and do not share your login with staff. If a staff member needs to post, use Meta Business Suite roles or Instagram's professional dashboard permissions instead of handing over the password. When a staff member leaves, remove their access the same day.

Check your page regularly for cloned copies. Search your business name on Instagram and Facebook once a week. If you find a fake page, report it for impersonation and warn your followers in a story or post. Pin a post on your page with your only real account details and your only real phone number, so customers can check before they pay.

Consider getting your page verified with a Meta Verified subscription if your budget allows. The blue tick helps customers tell the real page from a clone, though it does not replace the other precautions.

## Verify every rider and every delivery

Delivery fraud is easy to prevent with a simple system.

Use riders you know. Build a relationship with one or two dispatch companies or trusted independent riders in your area. In Lagos, for example, many sellers in Lekki work with the same rider for months, and the rider knows the seller's packaging and the regular customers. That trust is worth more than saving ₦500 on a cheaper unknown rider.

When a customer sends their own rider, confirm with the customer first. Message the customer on the same chat where the order was placed and ask: "Your rider's name is Tunde, correct? Please confirm." Do not hand goods to a stranger because he says a name. Get the rider's phone number and, for high-value orders, a photo of the rider and the package before it leaves.

For pay on delivery orders, set a clear policy. Many successful sellers in Lagos now take a non-refundable delivery fee upfront before dispatching a pay on delivery order, and they confirm the customer's address and phone number by call before the rider leaves. If the customer refuses to pay the delivery fee upfront, that is a warning sign. Some sellers have stopped pay on delivery entirely for first-time customers and only offer it to repeat buyers. That is a reasonable business decision, and you should explain it politely to customers.

Always keep delivery proof. Take a photo of the packaged item, the waybill or rider receipt, and the customer's confirmation message. For high-value orders, ask the rider to confirm delivery on WhatsApp with a photo. If a customer later claims they never received the goods, you have evidence.

## Keep records that protect you

Good records are your best defence in any dispute, whether with a customer, a bank, or a delivery company.

For every order, keep a simple record: customer name, phone number, items ordered, amount paid, payment reference, date, and delivery status. A notebook works, but a simple spreadsheet on your phone is better because you can search it. Google Sheets is free and you can update it from anywhere. If you use Paystack or Flutterwave, the dashboard already keeps the payment side of the record.

Keep receipts for everything you buy for the business, and keep your supplier invoices. If a supplier disappears with your money, a bank transfer record and a chat history give you something to report to the bank and, if needed, to the police.

## Train anyone who handles money or messages

Most fraud succeeds because a staff member did not know the rule. If you have a sales assistant, a rider, or a family member who helps with orders, train them.

Write down your payment rules in one page. Tape it near the desk or save it in the staff WhatsApp group. It should say: never dispatch without confirming payment in the bank app or payment dashboard; never share any code; never give goods to a rider without confirming with the customer; never change account details without your approval.

Limit who can change account details. Only you should be able to update the account number on your page, your WhatsApp catalogue, or your website. If a staff member's phone is compromised, the damage is limited.

## What to do if you have been scammed

If fraud happens despite your precautions, act quickly and in the right order.

First, gather your evidence: the chat, the fake screenshot, the payment records, the delivery details, and the phone numbers involved. Save screenshots of everything before the scammer deletes messages.

Second, report the account number to your bank and ask them to flag it. If the money went through Paystack or Flutterwave, contact their support with the transaction reference. Banks can sometimes freeze funds if you report fast enough, especially within the first few hours.

Third, report the fraud to the police and to the Economic and Financial Crimes Commission, the EFCC, through their official channels. Many small business owners do not report because they think the amount is too small, but reports build the record that helps catch repeat offenders.

Fourth, warn your customers. Post on your page that a fake account is impersonating you, or that a particular number is fraudulent. Your customers will thank you, and it protects your reputation.

Finally, review what went wrong and fix the gap. Did you dispatch on a screenshot? Did a staff member share a code? Change the process so it cannot happen the same way again. Every loss should buy you a lesson.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I know if a payment alert is fake?

Check your own bank app or payment dashboard. If the money is not in your account, the payment did not happen, no matter what the screenshot shows. Also check the sender's name and account number against the alert, and watch for alerts that arrive from a regular phone number instead of your bank's official sender name.

### What should I do if a customer insists I dispatch before the payment reflects?

Politely explain your policy and offer to dispatch the moment the payment confirms, which is usually within minutes. A genuine customer will wait. If the customer becomes abusive or threatening, that is itself a warning sign, and you are better off without that sale.

### Can I recover money sent to a fraudster?

Sometimes, if you report to your bank within hours and the receiving bank freezes the funds. Recovery is not guaranteed, which is why prevention matters so much more. Always report anyway, because the report helps the bank flag the account.

### Is pay on delivery safe for my business?

It can be, if you take the delivery fee upfront, confirm the customer by phone, and use a rider you trust. For first-time customers and high-value items, many sellers prefer full payment before dispatch. Choose the policy that fits your risk, and apply it consistently.

## What to Do Next

1. Turn on two-step verification on WhatsApp and two-factor authentication on Instagram and Facebook today.
2. Set up a Paystack or Flutterwave account and create payment links for your best-selling products.
3. Write down your payment rule, "no dispatch until payment confirms in our own account," and share it with your staff and your customers.
4. Open a separate business bank account if you still mix business and personal money.
5. Search your business name on Instagram and Facebook this week and report any cloned pages you find.
6. Create a simple order spreadsheet and start recording every order, payment reference and delivery status.
7. Agree on one or two trusted riders and stop using random riders for valuable orders.
