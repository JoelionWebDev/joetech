Many Nigerian small businesses lose money not because they have no customers, but because they sell on credit and never get paid. A shop owner gives goods to a neighbour, a friend, or a regular customer, with a promise to pay next week. Next week becomes next month. Next month becomes never. The goods are gone, the money is gone, and the business is left struggling to restock.

At the same time, refusing all credit can also lose sales. In Nigeria, many customers buy on credit because they are paid at the end of the month, because their own customers owe them, or because they need goods urgently. A shop that never gives credit may lose customers to a competitor who does. The answer is not to stop selling on credit completely. The answer is to sell on credit with clear rules, careful limits, and a firm follow-up system. This guide shows you how.

## Why Credit Is Risky, and Why Businesses Still Offer It

Credit means you give goods or services now and receive payment later. That creates risk. The customer may forget, may have no money when the payment date comes, may move away, or may simply refuse to pay. For a small business with little cash, even a few unpaid debts can stop you from buying new stock. That is why many traders say that credit has closed more shops than low sales.

Yet credit can also grow a business. A regular customer who buys on credit and pays on time will buy more from you than a customer who must pay cash every time. A shop that gives credit to trusted traders, offices, and organisations can win large orders that cash-only sellers cannot get. A food vendor who supplies a school on credit and is paid at month end can build a steady contract. The difference between credit that grows your business and credit that kills it is control.

The goal of this guide is control. You will learn who deserves credit, how much to give, what terms to set, how to record every debt, and how to collect politely but firmly. You do not need a degree in finance. You need a clear policy and the discipline to follow it, even when the customer is a friend or a family member.

## Step 1: Write a Simple Credit Policy

A credit policy is a set of rules for who can buy on credit, how much they can owe, and when they must pay. Write it down, even if it is just one page in your notebook. A written policy helps you say no without feeling guilty, and it helps your staff follow the same rules when you are not around.

Your policy should answer five questions. Who can buy on credit? How much can each person owe at one time? How long do they have to pay? What happens if they pay late? And what records must be kept for every credit sale? Answer these questions before you give your next credit sale.

Keep your policy simple and visible. For example, you might decide that only customers who have bought from you in cash at least three times can buy on credit. You might set a limit of ₦20,000 per customer for a small shop, or ₦100,000 for a larger shop. You might give seven days for small debts and thirty days for larger business customers. Write the policy in plain language and stick to it.

Share the policy with your customers. Tell a new customer, politely, that your shop gives credit to regular customers who pay on time, with a limit and a payment date. Most honest customers will respect clear rules. Customers who argue against any rule are often the ones who will not pay.

Review your policy every few months. If you notice that most debts are paid on time, you can raise limits slowly for good payers. If debts are growing, tighten the rules. Your policy should protect your cash, not just please customers.

## Step 2: Decide Who Deserves Credit

Not every customer should get credit. Give credit only to people you know and trust, and only after they have shown they pay on time. Start with customers who have bought from you in cash several times, who live or work nearby, and whose phone number and address you know. A customer you have known for months is safer than a stranger who walks in and asks for credit on the first day.

Be careful with friends and family. Many businesses have lost money because the owner could not say no to a relative. If you give credit to family, apply the same rules as for any customer: a clear amount, a clear payment date, and a written record. Tell them, kindly, that the business needs the money to restock. A family member who respects your business will respect the rule.

For business customers, such as a restaurant buying foodstuff, a school buying supplies, or an office buying stationery, ask for basic details before you give credit. Get the business name, the contact person's name and phone number, the office address, and, if possible, a written order or a letter on their letterhead. For larger amounts, ask for a deposit or a part payment upfront. A serious business customer will not mind giving these details.

Do not give credit to a customer who already owes you and has not paid. Do not give more credit to a customer who is late on a previous debt. That is one of the most common mistakes in Nigerian shops. The customer says they need more goods to make money to pay you, but the debt only grows. Stop new credit until the old debt is cleared.

Trust your instincts, but check the facts too. If a customer has a history of owing other traders in the market, ask around quietly before you give credit. Traders in the same market often know who pays and who does not. A short, polite check can save you a large loss.

## Step 3: Set a Credit Limit for Each Customer

A credit limit is the maximum amount one customer can owe you at any time. Without a limit, a small debt can grow into a large debt before you notice. Set a limit based on the customer's buying history, their income, and your own cash position. A small shop should set small limits. A limit of ₦10,000 to ₦50,000 per customer is common for small retail shops. Larger wholesalers may set higher limits for trusted traders.

Start small for new credit customers. Give a small amount first, such as ₦10,000, and see if they pay on time. If they pay well for three months, you can raise the limit gradually. If they pay late, keep the limit low or stop credit completely. This step-by-step approach protects you while you learn who is reliable.

Never let one customer owe you so much that their debt would stop your business if they never paid. As a guide, the total amount owed to you by all customers should not be more than a small part of your working capital. If you have ₦500,000 in stock and cash, you should not have ₦400,000 sitting in unpaid debts. Keep your total credit low enough that you can still buy new stock and pay rent even if some customers pay late.

Keep a separate record for each customer. Write their name, phone number, address, credit limit, current balance, and payment dates. Update it every time they buy on credit or make a payment. A customer who knows you keep accurate records is more likely to pay on time.

## Step 4: Agree Clear Payment Terms Before You Give Goods

Never give goods on credit without agreeing when and how the customer will pay. Say the date clearly: "You will pay ₦20,000 on Friday, 28 November." Do not say "pay me next week" or "pay me soon." Vague dates lead to vague payments. Write the date on the receipt and in your credit book.

Decide how long you will give. For small retail sales, seven days is common. For larger sales to traders or offices, fourteen or thirty days may be normal. Match the term to the customer's pay cycle. A salary earner may pay at month end. A trader may pay after market day. Agree a date that fits their income, but do not agree a date so far away that you forget the debt.

Agree on the payment method. Bank transfer, OPay, PalmPay, or cash are common in Nigeria. Give the customer your account details in writing. If they pay by transfer, ask them to send proof of payment on WhatsApp. That proof helps you match the payment to the right debt.

For larger credit sales, ask for a part payment upfront. For example, if the total is ₦100,000, ask for ₦40,000 now and ₦60,000 in two weeks. A part payment shows commitment and reduces your risk. It also helps your cash flow, because you have some money to restock while you wait for the balance.

Put the terms in writing, even for a small sale. Use a simple credit note or receipt with the customer's name, the goods, the total amount, the amount paid, the balance, and the payment date. Ask the customer to sign it. A signed note is not just a record. It reminds the customer that the debt is serious, and it helps you if you ever need to involve a third party to collect.

## Step 5: Keep Accurate Records of Every Debt

Good records are the backbone of safe credit sales. Without records, you will forget who owes what, customers will dispute amounts, and debts will grow quietly. Keep a credit book, a notebook, or a simple spreadsheet on your phone. For each credit sale, record the date, the customer's name and phone number, the goods sold, the total amount, the amount paid, the balance, and the payment date.

Give the customer a receipt for every credit sale and for every payment they make. When they pay part of the debt, write a new receipt showing the new balance. That way, both you and the customer always know how much is left. Keep your copy of every receipt in a file, in date order.

Check your credit book every week. Add up the total amount owed to you. List the debts that are due this week and the debts that are already late. That weekly check takes less than an hour, but it can save your business. Many shop owners only check when they run out of cash, and by then the debts are too large to collect easily.

Use your phone to help. Set reminders for payment dates in your calendar or in WhatsApp. Save each credit customer's number with their name and a note of their balance. A simple system you use every week is better than a complex system you never use.

## Step 6: Follow Up Early and Politely

Follow-up is where most credit sales are won or lost. Do not wait until a debt is two months late before you ask for payment. Start reminding the customer a day or two before the payment date. A friendly message works well: "Good morning, this is a reminder that your balance of ₦15,000 is due tomorrow. Thank you." Many customers pay on time when they are reminded.

If the payment date passes and the customer has not paid, contact them the next day. Call or send a WhatsApp message. Be polite but clear. State the amount, the original date, and ask when they will pay. Listen to their reason. If they have a genuine problem, such as a salary delay, agree a new date in writing, but do not extend the date again and again without a part payment.

Visit the customer if calls and messages do not work. A face-to-face visit shows you are serious. Visit during business hours, be calm, and bring your records. Show the customer the signed receipt and the balance. Ask for a part payment on the spot, even if they cannot pay the full amount. A part payment is better than a promise.

Keep every follow-up polite and professional. Do not shout, threaten, or embarrass the customer in public. That can damage your reputation and make collection harder. At the same time, do not be so soft that the customer ignores you. Firm, polite, and consistent follow-up is the best approach.

## Step 7: Handle Late Payment and Bad Debts

Even with good rules, some customers will pay late. Have a clear plan for late payment. For a first late payment, send a reminder and agree a new date, with a part payment if possible. For a second late payment, stop all new credit and ask for a written promise to pay by a specific date. For a long-overdue debt, consider involving a trusted third party, such as a market association leader, a community elder, or a lawyer, to help mediate.

Do not keep giving goods to a customer who is already late. That is the fastest way to turn a small bad debt into a large one. Tell the customer, politely, that you cannot give more credit until the old balance is cleared. Most honest customers will understand.

If a debt looks unlikely to be paid, act early. The longer you wait, the harder it is to collect. Keep all your records: receipts, signed credit notes, WhatsApp messages, and proof of delivery. Those records help if you need to involve a mediator or take legal steps for a large amount. For very large debts, get legal advice before you act. For small debts, a mediator or a market association is often faster and cheaper than court.

Learn from every bad debt. Ask yourself what went wrong. Did you give credit to a stranger? Did you give too much? Did you fail to follow up early? Did you have no written record? Use the answer to tighten your policy. A bad debt is painful, but it is also a lesson that can protect your business in future.

## Step 8: Use Cash, Discounts, and Other Tools to Reduce Credit

You can reduce your need for credit by encouraging cash payment. Offer a small discount for cash payment, or a small discount for payment on the day of purchase. Many customers will choose cash if they save money. For example, a 5 percent discount for cash can be cheaper for you than the cost of chasing a late payment.

Use payment links and mobile money to make cash payment easy. A Paystack payment link, a bank transfer, or an OPay wallet lets a customer pay quickly, even if they have no cash in hand. The easier you make payment, the less they will ask for credit.

Consider a layaway or instalment plan for expensive items, instead of giving the goods before payment. The customer pays in parts, and you give them the goods only when they have paid in full, or when they have paid a large part. This works well for furniture, electronics, and other high-value goods. It lets the customer buy what they need without you carrying the full risk.

For regular business customers, consider a monthly statement. At the end of each month, send them a statement showing all their purchases, payments, and the balance. A clear statement helps the customer plan payment and helps you track your income.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

Do not sell on credit because you feel sorry for a customer. Pity is not a business policy. Do not give credit without a written record. Memory fades, but a signed receipt does not. Do not give credit to a customer who already owes you. Do not let your total debts grow so large that you cannot restock. Do not wait months before you follow up. And do not mix credit with friendship. A friend who will not pay is not a good customer, and a business that cannot collect its debts will not survive.

## Final Checklist for Safe Credit Sales

Before you give credit, check that the customer has bought from you before and paid on time, that you know their phone number and address, that you have set a clear limit, that you have agreed a payment date and method, and that you have recorded the sale in your credit book.

Credit can help your business grow, but only if you control it. Start with small limits, follow up early, keep good records, and stop credit for anyone who does not pay. Your goods are your money. Protect them with clear rules.
