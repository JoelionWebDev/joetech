Walk down Allen Avenue in Ikeja on a Saturday morning and look at the shop walls. Dozens of flyers are pasted there, one on top of another, and most of them are invisible. Not because the businesses are bad. A woman selling cakes from her flat in Surulere can bake better than a bakery with a billboard, but her flyer blurs into the wall. Then one flyer catches you. You do not know why. You just stop, read the price, and save the number.

That split second is the whole job. Nobody sits down to study a flyer. Somebody glances at it for two or three seconds — on a wall, on a WhatsApp status, in a group chat — and decides whether your business is worth a second look. The difference between the flyer that gets read and the one that gets ignored is rarely the offer. It is the design thinking underneath it: where the eye lands first, what is biggest, and what quietly disappears.

## The eye lands in one place first

A stranger's eye does not read a flyer top to bottom like a letter. It jumps. It goes to the biggest thing first, then to the brightest colour, then to a face or a plate of food, and only then to the words. On a phone screen, the first landing spot is usually the top third of the image.

That means your flyer has a front door, and you get to choose where it is. If the biggest thing on it is a decorative swirl, or a stock photo of a smiling stranger who has nothing to do with your business, the eye walks in through the wrong door and never finds the offer. If the biggest thing is your headline or your best product photo, the eye lands exactly where you want it.

Shrink your flyer to thumbnail size and hold your phone at arm's length. What do you see first? Funke, who sells small chops in Egbeda, showed me her flyer once. The biggest thing on it was a gold border, while her price — ₦2,500 a pack, the whole reason to order — sat squeezed in a corner in small grey text. We swapped the sizes: the photo went big, the price went second biggest, the border went away. Her next status post got eleven orders in a day. Same food, same price. The only thing that changed was where the eye landed first.

## Give the flyer a size ladder

Hierarchy is a stiff word for a simple idea: some things on the flyer matter more than others, and the sizes should say so. The headline is the top rung — the biggest text on the page, short enough to read in two seconds. The main photo sits beside it or just under it. The price and the offer sit on the next rung, smaller than the headline but bigger than everything else. The contact details sit at the bottom, readable but quiet.

When every element is the same size, the ladder collapses. The business name, the headline, the price, the address, the motto and the Instagram handle all fight at the same size, and the reader's eye has nowhere to rest. It slides off the page. One size for the headline, one size for the price, one size for the details — that is the whole ladder, and you can build it in Canva by picking three text sizes and refusing to add a fourth.

The ladder also decides what gets cut. If a line of text does not fit its rung, do not shrink everything until it fits. Cut words. "Freshly baked celebration cakes, made to order for birthdays, weddings and all your special occasions" is a paragraph pretending to be a headline. "Celebration Cakes from ₦25,000" is a headline.

## Two fonts are enough — here is how to pick them

Fonts are where good flyers quietly fall apart. Canva gives you hundreds of them, and the temptation is to use a different one for every line. Do not. Two fonts, used the same way every time, will beat five fonts every single time.

The pairing rule is simple: one loud font, one quiet font. The loud font does the headline — bold, heavy, readable from across the road, something like Anton, Bebas Neue, or Archivo Black. The quiet font does everything else: prices, details, contact information. Plain, clean, and boring in the best way — Montserrat, Open Sans, or Lato. Loud plus quiet. Heavy plus plain. That contrast is what makes a flyer look designed instead of accidental. Anton with Montserrat suits most sales flyers; Playfair Display with Lato suits a cake business or a salon that wants to feel a little expensive. Pick one pair and use it on every flyer for the next three months.

Two traps to dodge. The first is script fonts on important information. A curly script font can look lovely as a decorative word, but a price or a phone number in script will get misread. Prices and phone numbers go in the quiet font, always. The second trap is all capitals for long text. Capitals are fine for a five-word headline. A whole paragraph in capitals reads like shouting, and it is harder to read, because the eye recognises word shapes and capitals flatten them all into rectangles.

## Colours that survive a Nigerian print shop

Colour is the part of flyer design where the screen lies to you. What looks soft and pretty on your phone can come back from the printer washed out, grey, or a completely different shade. Most print shops in Lagos print on paper that is not pure white, with ink that is not perfectly calibrated, and your flyer goes through a machine that does not care about your pastel palette. Colours that look delicate on screen are the first casualties.

The safe combinations are the strong ones. Deep green with cream and white holds up beautifully. Navy blue with gold and white prints cleanly. Black with yellow and white is loud, cheap to print well, and readable from a distance — a suya spot or a phone repair shop can run on those three colours for years. What these combinations share is contrast: a dark colour, a light colour, and one warm accent between them.

The combinations that wash out are almost always the pale ones. Pale yellow text on a white background disappears on paper — it barely survives on screen. Light pink on white fades to nothing. Pastel blue on glossy paper turns grey. Neon colours look electric on a phone and muddy on paper, because the printer simply cannot reproduce them. And light grey text is the worst offender of all: on a printed flyer it looks like a printing error.

None of this means your flyer has to be dark and heavy. It means the text must sit on a background it can fight against and win. Dark text on a light background is the safest bet there is. White text works too, but only on a genuinely dark background. If you are placing white text over a photo, darken the photo first in Canva, or put a solid colour block behind the text. Before you print five hundred copies, print one: a test copy at a business centre in Ikeja costs about ₦200, and it will show you exactly which colours survived.

## Space is doing half the work

Beginners fill every corner of a flyer because empty space feels wasteful, like leaving food on the plate. In design, empty space is not waste. It is the thing that makes the important parts readable. A headline with breathing room around it looks bigger than the same headline squeezed between two other elements. A phone number with space around it gets noticed. A phone number crammed into a corner gets missed.

The practical rule is a margin. Keep every important element at least a finger's width away from the edge of the flyer — more on a printed flyer, because printers and WhatsApp both crop edges, and a phone number with its last digit cut off is useless. Inside the flyer, give each block its own patch of ground, so the eye reads them as separate pieces of information.

Alignment is the quiet partner of spacing. Canva shows pink guide lines when elements line up — use them. Pick one alignment for the body of the flyer and hold it.

## One flyer, rebuilt from scratch

Chiamaka runs a cake business from her flat in Surulere. She bakes celebration cakes from ₦25,000 and takes orders on WhatsApp. Her first flyer had a pale pink background, a small photo of a cake in one corner, a headline in curly script that read "Welcome to Chiamaka's Cakes, where every celebration is made sweeter with love and the finest ingredients," four more cake photos squeezed along the bottom, the price hidden in a sentence halfway down, and her WhatsApp number in light grey at the very bottom. It was pretty. It also sold nothing.

The rebuild started with the photo. We picked one photograph — a single chocolate cake, shot in daylight on her kitchen table, frosting sharp, background plain — and made it fill the top half of the flyer. One photo, big, doing the job that five small ones could not. The other four went away. A customer does not need to see your whole catalogue on one flyer. They need to see one cake they want.

Then the headline. Out went the sentence about love and finest ingredients. In came six words in Anton, dark brown on cream: "Celebration Cakes from ₦25,000." The price moved into the headline itself, because the price was the information that decided whether someone kept reading. Underneath it, in Montserrat, two short lines: "Chocolate, vanilla, red velvet. Delivered across Lagos."

Then the colours. The pale pink background became cream, the curly script went away, the headline went dark brown, the price went deep red, and a thin dark brown band ran across the bottom carrying the WhatsApp number in white, big enough to read from arm's length. Three colours, two fonts, one photo. She posted it on her status on a Friday morning. By Saturday evening she had nine orders, four of them from people who had seen the old flyer for months and never messaged. Nothing about the cakes had changed. The eye finally had somewhere to land.

## The five-second test

There is a test that settles every argument about a flyer, and it costs nothing. Show the finished flyer to three people who are not involved in your business — a neighbour, a customer, a friend — for five seconds, then take it away and ask three questions. What do I sell? How much does it cost? How do I order? If they can answer all three, the flyer works. If they cannot, the hierarchy is broken, and no amount of decoration will save it.

Run that test before you print and before you post. A flyer that passes it will do its job on a wall in Surulere, on a WhatsApp status at seven in the morning, and in a group chat forwarded three times before breakfast. The principles never change: one front door for the eye, a size ladder that says what matters, two fonts that get along, colours strong enough to survive the printer, and enough space for everything to breathe.
