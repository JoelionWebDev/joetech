A caterer in Ikeja supplies small chops for a bank's training session — two hundred packs, paid on time, and a standing order for every Friday. A printing shop in Surulere lands one contract to produce branded notebooks for a telecom company, and that single order is bigger than three months of walk-in customers. A cleaning company in Lekki signs a six-month contract with a hotel, and suddenly the owner can plan, hire, and buy equipment with confidence.

That is the power of corporate clients. One good corporate customer can change a small business. The orders are bigger, the payments are more predictable, and the work is steadier. But many Nigerian small business owners never try to sell to companies at all. They assume corporate clients are only for big companies, that the process is too complicated, or that they need connections in high places before anyone will listen.

The truth is simpler. Companies in Lagos, Abuja, and Port Harcourt buy from small businesses every day. They buy food, cleaning, printing, branding, transport, training, uniforms, gifts, event services, and much more. They buy from small suppliers because small suppliers are flexible, fast, and often cheaper than the big firms. What they want is not size. They want reliability, professionalism, and proof that you can deliver what you promise.

This guide explains how to sell to corporate clients as a small business in Nigeria, step by step, in plain language: how to find the right companies, how to reach the right person, how to pitch without sounding desperate, how to price and quote properly, how to survive procurement and payment terms, and how to keep the contract once you win it.

## Why Corporate Clients Are Worth the Effort

Selling to individual customers is hard work. You need many customers, every day, and each one buys a little. Selling to companies is different. You need fewer customers, and each one buys a lot.

First, the order sizes are bigger. A company ordering lunch for a training will order fifty or two hundred plates at once. A company ordering branded shirts will order fifty or five hundred at once. One invoice can cover your rent for the month.

Second, the work repeats. Companies hold trainings, meetings, end-of-year parties, and staff events on a regular cycle. If you serve them well once, they will call you again. A single good delivery can become a monthly or quarterly order.

Third, corporate work builds your reputation. When you can say you supplied a known bank, a hotel, an oil company, or a large church, other companies take you more seriously. Your name carries weight.

There are challenges, of course. Companies take longer to decide. They ask for quotations, invoices, and sometimes formal documents. They may pay after thirty days instead of paying cash on delivery. You need patience and proper records. But for most small businesses, the rewards are far bigger than the difficulties.

## The Mindset Shift: You Are a Supplier, Not a Beggar

The biggest mistake small business owners make when approaching companies is acting like they are begging for help. They send messages full of apologies and desperation. They offer to do the work for almost nothing. They accept any condition because they are afraid to lose the chance.

Stop that. You are not begging. You are offering a service the company needs. Companies spend money every day on the things you sell. If they do not buy from you, they will buy from someone else, often at a higher price and with worse service. You are helping them solve a problem.

That does not mean you should be proud or rude. It means you should be confident, clear, and professional. Present your business as a real business. Talk about what you can do, how fast you can deliver, and why your work is good. Let your samples, your photos, and your past work speak for you.

## Step 1: Decide Exactly What You Sell to Companies

Before you approach any company, be clear about what you offer them. "We do everything" is not an offer. Companies buy specific things for specific needs.

Start by listing your products or services, then ask which ones companies actually buy regularly. A fashion designer may sell dresses to individuals, but companies buy uniforms, branded T-shirts, and staff gifts. A baker may sell cakes to families, but companies buy small chops, lunch packs, and event cakes. A graphic designer may design flyers for shops, but companies buy logos, banners, annual reports, and social media designs.

Pick one or two corporate offers and make them sharp. For each offer, write down what is included, how long it takes, and what it costs. For example, a catering offer might read: "Lunch packs for trainings and meetings — rice, chicken, and a drink, from fifty packs, delivered hot to your office in Lekki, Victoria Island, or Ikoyi within three hours of confirmation."

Make your offer easy to understand in one minute. A busy manager will not read a long document to find out what you do. If you cannot explain your offer in two sentences, it is not clear enough yet.

## Step 2: Find the Right Companies to Target

Do not approach every company in Lagos. Approach the companies most likely to buy what you sell, and most likely to pay on time.

Start with companies near you. If you are in Lekki, target the banks, hotels, tech companies, and offices along Admiralty Way, Chevron Drive, and the Lekki-Epe Expressway. If you are in Ikeja, target the companies in the Allen Avenue and Opebi areas. If you are in Abuja, target the ministries, agencies, and corporate offices in the Central Business District and Maitama. Proximity matters because delivery is faster and cheaper, and you can visit in person when needed.

Next, think about which industries buy your product often. Banks and telecom companies hold many trainings and events. Hotels need food, cleaning, laundry, and gifts for guests. Oil and gas companies buy branded items, safety wear, and catering for their offices. Schools buy uniforms, books, and event services. Churches and large ministries buy food, sound, decoration, and printing for their programmes. Real estate companies buy branded gifts for clients and marketing materials.

Make a list of twenty companies to start with. Write their names, their locations, what they might buy from you, and who the decision maker might be. Twenty well-chosen targets are better than two hundred random ones.

## Step 3: Find the Right Person Inside the Company

A company does not buy. A person inside the company buys. Your job is to find that person.

For catering, food, and event services, the right person is often the admin officer, the human resources manager, or the office manager. For printing, branding, and marketing, it is often the marketing manager or the communications officer. For cleaning and facility services, it is often the facility manager or the admin manager. For training, it is often the HR manager or the learning and development officer.

If you cannot find the person online, call the company's general line and ask politely: "Good morning, please, who handles catering for your trainings?" or "Please, who is in charge of procurement for branded items?" Receptionists answer this question every day. Be polite, be brief, and write the name down.

## Step 4: Prepare Your Business to Look Corporate-Ready

Companies judge you before they taste your food or see your work. They judge you by how professional your business looks. You do not need a big office. You need the basics done well.

First, have a clear business name and a way to be reached easily — a WhatsApp Business number, a simple email address, and an Instagram page or a simple website that shows your work with clear photos. If a manager searches for you and finds nothing, they will worry. If they find neat photos, clear prices, and real reviews, they will relax.

Second, have a simple company profile. This is a one- or two-page document that says who you are, what you do, who you have served, and how to contact you. Include photos of your best work. A profile in PDF form, sent on WhatsApp or by email, makes you look organised. Keep it short. Managers do not read long profiles.

Fourth, have your prices written down. Know your prices for common orders, and know them by heart. When a manager asks for a price, you should answer quickly and confidently, not say you will get back to them in three days.

One small thing that helps at first meetings is a neat digital business card you can share on WhatsApp in seconds — and if you want one without paying a designer, a free digital business card tool can produce a clean, shareable card in minutes.

## Step 5: Make First Contact the Right Way

First contact is not a sale. It is an introduction. Your goal is to be remembered and to get permission to send more information, not to force an order on the first message.

If you send a WhatsApp message or an email, keep it short — under one hundred words if you can. Start with the person's name. Say what you do in one line. Say why you are writing. End with a simple request: "May I send our price list and a few photos?" Here is an example: "Good morning, Mr. Okafor. I am Tunde from PrintHub in Surulere. We produce branded notebooks, shirts, and banners for companies, with delivery in three to five working days. May I send you our price list and samples of recent work? Thank you."

Do not send long voice notes to strangers. Do not send your price list before they ask for it. Do not message the same person every day. Send your introduction once, then follow up politely after one week if there is no reply. If there is still no reply after two follow-ups, move on to the next company and come back to this one after a month.

## Step 6: Pitch With a Proposal, Not Just a Price

When a company shows interest, they will usually ask for a quotation or a proposal. This is your real pitch. Do it well.

A good quotation has five parts. First, your business name, address, phone number, and email at the top. Second, the name of the person and company you are quoting for. Third, a clear list of what you will supply — item, quantity, description, and unit price. Fourth, the total amount, including delivery if delivery is included, and the payment terms. Fifth, how long the quotation is valid and how long delivery will take.

Use a clean format. You can prepare a quotation template in a Word document or a Google Doc and reuse it for every client. Number your quotations so you can track them. Always send quotations as PDF files, not as plain WhatsApp messages, because PDF files look professional and are easy for the client to forward to their boss or their finance department.

Never quote a price you cannot deliver on. If your price is too low, you will lose money and cut corners, and the client will notice. If your price is too high, you will lose the job. Price your work honestly: cost of materials, cost of labour, transport, a small margin for your business, and a little room for unexpected costs.

## Step 7: Handle Procurement, Paperwork, and Payment Terms

This is where many small businesses give up, and where you can stand out by simply being prepared.

Procurement is the department or process a company uses to buy things. In a small company, one manager may decide. In a big company, you may need to submit a quotation, fill a vendor form, and wait for approval. Do not be afraid of this. Ask what the process is, and follow it carefully.

Common requirements include a CAC certificate, a Tax Identification Number, a bank account in the business name, and sometimes a pro forma invoice. Some companies will ask you to register as a vendor. Registration is usually a form and copies of your documents. Fill it in fully and return it quickly. Vendors who return forms fast are remembered.

Payment terms are the hardest part for small businesses. Many companies pay after delivery, sometimes after fourteen or thirty days. Some pay fifty percent upfront and fifty percent after delivery. A few pay on delivery. Ask about payment terms before you accept the job, not after you have delivered. If the company pays after thirty days, make sure you can afford the materials and the work without that payment. Do not take a big order that will empty your account while you wait for payment.

## Step 8: Deliver Perfectly, Then Ask for More Work

Your first corporate order is a test. The company is watching everything: did you deliver on time, was the quality good, was the packaging neat, did you communicate well, did you handle problems calmly? If you pass the test, more work will follow.

Deliver early when you can. Communicate before the client asks. If there is a delay, call the client before they call you. If something goes wrong, fix it fast and tell the client what you did. One honest, fast fix can earn more trust than a perfect delivery.

After a successful delivery, do three things. First, thank the client the same day. Second, ask for feedback: "How was the food and the delivery? Is there anything we can improve?" Third, ask for the next order: "When is your next training or event? May I send a quotation ahead of time?"

## A Real Example: A Printer Wins a Bank Contract

Consider a small printing business in Ikeja. For years, the owner survived on walk-in customers — a few flyers here, a few business cards there. Income was unpredictable, and some weeks were very slow.

The owner decided to target corporate clients. He chose five banks with branches near Ikeja and five hotels. He prepared a simple profile with photos of his best work: branded notebooks, diaries, banners, and T-shirts. He visited each bank's branch and asked for the admin or marketing officer. Three people agreed to see him. He left his profile and a sample notebook with each one.

Two weeks later, one bank called. They needed two hundred branded notebooks for a training. He sent a clean quotation the same day, with a clear delivery date. He delivered a day early. The notebooks were neat, the packaging was good, and he sent his invoice immediately.

The bank ordered again the next month, then again. After three months, they added branded shirts and banners. The printer hired an assistant, bought a better machine, and stopped worrying about slow weeks. He did not become big overnight. He became steady, and steadiness is what grows a business.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

First, do not promise what you cannot deliver. Overpromising wins the order and loses the client.

Third, do not ignore paperwork. Keep copies of every quotation, invoice, delivery note, and payment. Good records protect you when there is a dispute.

Fourth, do not depend on one corporate client. One big client is wonderful, but if they stop ordering, your business suffers. Aim for at least three to five corporate clients, so losing one does not break you.

## Your First Month Plan

Here is a simple plan for your first month selling to corporate clients. In week one, choose your corporate offer, prepare your profile and your price list, and list twenty target companies. In week two, find the right person in each company and make first contact with ten of them. In week three, contact the other ten, follow up with the first ten, and send quotations to anyone who asks. In week four, deliver any small orders you win perfectly, ask for feedback, and ask for the next order.

Keep a simple tracker. For each company, note the name of your contact, the date you first reached out, what you sent, and what they said. Review the tracker every Friday. This is how you stay organised, and organisation is what separates a business from a hustle.

Selling to corporate clients is not magic, and it is not only for connected people. It is a skill, and like any skill, it improves with practice. Start with twenty companies, one clear offer, and one professional profile. Make first contact, follow up politely, quote cleanly, and deliver well. The first contract may take a month or two, but once you have it, the second one comes faster, and the third faster still. Your business is ready. Go and meet them.
