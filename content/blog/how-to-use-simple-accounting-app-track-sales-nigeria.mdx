Ask a Nigerian small business owner a simple question — "How much did you make last month?" — and you will often get a pause, a laugh, and a guess. "I think we did well." "Money came in, but money also went out." "Let me check my bank alert." The truth is, most small businesses in Lagos, Abuja, Port Harcourt and Onitsha do not actually know whether they are making a profit. They know their bank balance. They know how busy they were. But they do not know, to the naira, what came in, what went out, and what is left.

That is not a character flaw. It is a systems problem. When sales arrive by transfer, cash, Opay, Moniepoint and Paystack, and expenses go out for stock, transport, data, packaging and food, keeping it all in your head is impossible. A simple accounting app fixes that. It gives you one place where every sale and every expense is recorded, and it does the maths for you.

This guide shows you how to choose a simple accounting app, set it up in an afternoon, record sales and expenses every day, and use the numbers to make better decisions. No accounting degree needed. If you can use WhatsApp, you can use these apps.

## Why Tracking Sales Matters More Than Selling More

Many Nigerian businesses chase more sales when their real problem is invisible money. They sell a lot, but they spend a lot too, and nobody notices until rent is due or a supplier needs to be paid. Tracking sales changes that in four ways.

First, it shows your real profit. Sales are not profit. If you sold ₦500,000 of clothes in a month and spent ₦420,000 on stock, transport, packaging and data, your profit is ₦80,000, not ₦500,000. Many owners discover, once they track properly, that their best-selling product is actually their least profitable one.

Second, it shows what sells and what does not. The app will tell you that wigs bring in 40 percent of your revenue, that lunch packs sell best on Fridays, or that nobody buys the product you keep restocking. That knowledge tells you what to order more of and what to stop buying.

Third, it helps you price correctly. When you know the true cost of each sale — stock, delivery, packaging, airtime, data — you can set prices that leave a real margin. Without records, you price by copying a neighbour, and you may be working for almost nothing.

Fourth, it prepares you for growth. A bank, a lender, a grant programme or an investor will ask for your records. A business with six months of clean sales records looks serious. A business with only bank alerts and memory does not. Even Orange Corners, the Tony Elumelu Foundation and most SME grants ask for evidence that your business earns money.

## Step 1: Choose a Simple App, Not a Complicated One

The best accounting app for a Nigerian small business is the one you will actually open every day. That means it must be simple, cheap, work on your phone, and work offline when network is poor. Do not choose the most powerful software. Choose the simplest one that covers your needs.

Here are the types of apps Nigerian SMEs commonly use. Pick the category first, then the app inside it.

**Simple sales-and-expense apps.** These are built for traders and small shops. You record a sale, you record an expense, and the app shows your profit. Examples that are popular in Nigeria include Kippa, Buka, and Oze. Kippa is widely used by market traders and small shops; it records sales, expenses and debts, and it works offline. Buka is built for food vendors and restaurants. Oze is built for small businesses that want simple records plus business insights. Most have a free plan or a low monthly fee in naira, and they are designed for people who are not accountants.

**Invoicing apps.** If you sell to companies, schools or offices that need an invoice before they pay, you need an app that creates professional invoices. Wave, Zoho Invoice and QuickBooks Online all do this, and they track who has paid and who still owes you. Wave has a free plan. Zoho Invoice is free for small volumes. QuickBooks Online is more expensive and more powerful — it is better suited to a growing business with staff.

**Spreadsheets.** Google Sheets is free, works on any phone, and is a perfectly good accounting tool for a business doing under 30 transactions a day. You can build a simple sheet with columns for date, item, quantity, amount received, amount spent and notes. It is not as automatic as an app, but it is free, it backs up to the cloud, and you control it completely.

Do not choose based on adverts. Download two apps, try each one for three days with real sales, and keep the one that feels easier. The test is simple: can you record a sale in under 30 seconds, without help, while a customer is standing in front of you? If not, try another app.

## Step 2: Set Up Your Business in the App

Set-up takes about an hour, and it is worth doing carefully. Rushing set-up is how people end up with messy records they abandon after two weeks.

Start by entering your business name, your currency (naira), and your financial year. Most apps will ask for a start date — use the first day of the current month. That gives you a clean month to measure.

Next, add your products or services with their prices. Add the items you actually sell, with the prices you actually charge. A food vendor adds jollof rice pack ₦3,500, fried rice pack ₦4,000, and egusi soup 2 litres ₦15,000. A fashion seller adds wig ₦45,000, dress ₦28,000, and shoes ₦35,000. A phone accessories seller adds charger ₦8,000, earpiece ₦12,000, and power bank ₦25,000. Adding products now means recording a sale later is just a tap, not typing.

Then add your common expense categories. Keep the list short: stock, transport, packaging, data and airtime, rent, salaries, and miscellaneous. If you have 25 categories, you will spend more time choosing a category than recording the expense. Seven categories are enough to start.

Finally, add your customers if the app supports it. Start with your regulars and your debtors — the people who buy on credit. When you record a sale on credit, the app will remind you who owes you, which is one of the most valuable features for a Nigerian trader. Unpaid credit kills more small businesses than low sales do.

## Step 3: Record Every Sale the Moment It Happens

This is the habit that makes or breaks the whole system. Record the sale when it happens, not at night, not on Sunday, not "when I have time". A sale recorded at night from memory is a sale recorded wrong. A sale recorded in the moment takes 20 seconds.

When a customer pays, open the app, tap "New Sale", choose the product, enter the quantity, confirm the amount, and choose the payment method: transfer, cash, Opay, Moniepoint, POS, or card. Then save it. That is all. Do it before you pack the goods, before you hand over the receipt, before you reply to the next customer.

For cash sales, keep the cash in one place — a cash box or a separate wallet — and count it at the end of the day. The app total for cash sales should match the cash in the box. If it does not, find the difference that same day, while you still remember the sales.

For transfers, record the sale and keep the bank alert as proof. If a customer pays by transfer and you are busy, record it as soon as you confirm the alert. Do not let "I will record it later" become a pile of 15 unrecorded transfers by Friday.

For credit sales, record the sale and mark it as "unpaid" or "on credit" with the customer's name. This is non-negotiable. A credit sale that is not recorded is a debt you will forget. A customer who owes you ₦50,000 for three weeks is not a loyal customer — they are a cash-flow problem, and your app should be showing you their name every day until they pay.

## Step 4: Record Every Expense, Even the Small Ones

Expenses are where Nigerian businesses leak money silently. The ₦2,000 for bike transport. The ₦5,000 for nylon bags. The ₦3,500 for data. The ₦10,000 for fuel for the generator. None of them feels big on its own. Together, they can eat your entire profit.

Record every expense on the day it happens, just like a sale. Open the app, tap "New Expense", choose the category, enter the amount, and add a short note: "Transport to Balogun market", "Packaging nylon, 100 pieces", "Fuel for generator". The note matters. In two weeks, when you look at "Transport ₦45,000", you will want to know what that was for.

Be honest about personal spending. This is the hardest rule for Nigerian business owners, because business money and personal money often live in the same account. If you take ₦20,000 from the business to buy food for the house, record it as an expense or as "Owner withdrawal". If you do not, your profit will look bigger than it really is, and you will think you can afford things you cannot.

A simple rule helps: pay yourself a fixed amount each week, like a salary, and record it. If your business makes ₦80,000 profit in a month, pay yourself ₦50,000 and leave ₦30,000 in the business. That discipline is what separates a business that grows from one that stays small forever.

## Step 5: Check Your Numbers Every Week

An app you never look at is just a diary. The value comes from checking the numbers regularly and acting on them. Set aside 30 minutes every Sunday evening, or Monday morning before the shop opens, to review the week.

Look at five numbers. First, total sales for the week. Second, total expenses for the week. Third, profit — sales minus expenses. Fourth, who owes you money, and how much. Fifth, your best-selling product and your worst-selling product.

Write those five numbers down in a notebook or a WhatsApp message to yourself. Compare them to last week. Are sales rising or falling? Are expenses rising faster than sales? Is one debtor now owing you more than ₦100,000? Is a product you keep buying barely selling?

Then make one decision based on what you see. Raise the price of a product whose costs have risen. Stop restocking a product that has not sold in two weeks. Call the debtor who has owed you the longest. Buy more stock of the product that sells out every Friday. One small decision a week, based on real numbers, compounds into a much stronger business over a year.

## Step 6: Use the Reports to Price, Plan and Borrow

Most apps generate reports automatically: a profit-and-loss report, a sales-by-product report, and a list of debtors. Learn to read those three reports. You do not need to understand accounting jargon. The profit-and-loss report answers "Did I make money this month?" The sales-by-product report answers "What should I sell more of?" The debtors list answers "Who needs to pay me?"

Use the profit-and-loss report to set prices. If the price of rice, chicken or fabric has risen in the market, your costs have risen too. The report will show your margin shrinking. Raise your prices before the margin disappears, not after. Customers accept a small, explained price rise far better than a sudden big one when you are already losing money.

Use the sales report to plan stock. If you sell 40 wigs a month and 10 dresses, order more wigs. If you sell 100 lunch packs on Fridays but only 20 on Mondays, cook more on Friday and less on Monday. This is not guesswork — it is your own sales history telling you what to do.

Use your records when you need money. If you apply for a loan, a grant or a supplier credit line, clean records are your strongest evidence. A lender who sees six months of steady sales of ₦400,000 a month and a profit of ₦80,000 a month will take you seriously. A lender who sees nothing will not.

## Common Mistakes That Break the System

Even a good app fails if you use it badly. Avoid these mistakes.

- **Recording only big sales.** The ₦2,000 sale matters as much as the ₦50,000 sale. Small sales add up, and skipping them makes your numbers useless.
- **Forgetting cash expenses.** Market runs, transport and food for staff are usually cash. If you only record transfers, you will understate expenses and overstate profit.
- **Mixing personal and business money.** Using the business account to pay school fees, buy fuel for your personal car, or send money to family — without recording it — makes the records meaningless.
- **Choosing a complicated app.** If the app asks for debits, credits, journals and ledgers, it is too much for a small trader. Switch to a simpler one.
- **Recording in batches once a month.** By then you have forgotten half the sales and most of the expenses. Record daily, review weekly.
- **Never backing up.** Set the app to back up to the cloud automatically. If your phone is stolen in traffic or falls into water, your records should survive.

## A Real Example: A Trader in Onitsha

Consider Chidi, who sells phone accessories in Onitsha Main Market. For two years, he kept his records in his head and in bank alerts. He thought he was making about ₦200,000 a month. When he started using Kippa, the truth was different. His monthly sales were ₦850,000, but his expenses — stock, transport from Lagos, shop rent share, data, and money he took for personal use — were ₦790,000. His real profit was ₦60,000 a month, not ₦200,000.

The app also showed him something else. Power banks were his best seller by revenue, but chargers had a better margin: he made ₦2,500 on each charger and only ₦1,500 on each power bank. And three customers owed him ₦180,000 in total, some of it for over two months. He called those customers, stopped giving credit to two of them, ordered more chargers, and raised the price of power banks by ₦1,000. Within three months, his monthly profit rose to ₦110,000, on almost the same sales. Nothing changed except that he could finally see his numbers.

## Your First Month With an Accounting App

Do not try to be perfect. Start this week and build the habit.

Week 1: Download two apps, try both with real sales, and keep the simpler one. Add your products and your seven expense categories. Record every sale and every expense for seven days, no exceptions.

Week 2: Do your first Sunday review. Look at the five numbers — sales, expenses, profit, debtors, best and worst seller — and write them down. Make one decision based on them.

Week 3: Add your regular customers and your debtors to the app. Call the person who has owed you the longest. Check that your cash in the box matches the app's cash total.

Week 4: Look at the full month. How much did you sell? How much did you spend? What is your real profit? Set a target for next month: sell 10 percent more, or cut one expense by 10 percent, and track it.
