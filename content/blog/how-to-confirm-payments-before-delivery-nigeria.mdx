A customer orders a ₦85,000 phone from your shop in Ikeja. They say they have transferred the money and show you a screenshot of a bank alert. You are busy, the shop is crowded, and the screenshot looks real. You hand over the phone. An hour later, you check your account. Nothing arrived. The alert was fake, the customer is gone, and you have lost ₦85,000 worth of stock. This happens to Nigerian sellers every day, and it is almost always preventable.

## Why Fake Payment Alerts Are So Common in Nigeria

Fake bank alerts are one of the most common frauds facing Nigerian small businesses. The tools to create them are easy to find: fake alert apps, doctored screenshots, and cloned SMS messages that look exactly like a real bank alert. A fraudster can generate a message that shows your name, the correct amount, and a transaction reference, all in under a minute.

The fraud works because sellers are under pressure. A busy shop attendant, a rider at a customer's gate, or a vendor chatting with five customers at once does not have time to check carefully. The fraudster relies on that rush. They create urgency — "I am in a hurry", "My driver is waiting", "Just give me the goods, the alert will come" — and the seller hands over the product before confirming.

The losses are real. A fake alert for a ₦15,000 food order hurts. A fake alert for a ₦250,000 laptop or a ₦500,000 bulk order can wipe out a small business's profit for a whole month. Some fraudsters work in pairs: one distracts the attendant while the other shows the fake alert. Others target delivery riders, who are far from the shop and cannot easily verify a payment. The good news is that confirming a payment takes less than a minute once you have a routine.

## The Golden Rule: Check Your Own Account, Not Their Screen

The single most important rule is this: a payment is confirmed only when you see it in your own bank account, your own bank app, or your own payment dashboard. Nothing else counts.

A screenshot on a customer's phone is not proof. Screenshots can be edited in seconds with free apps. A forwarded SMS is not proof. SMS messages can be faked. A customer's word is not proof. Even an honest customer can be mistaken about whether their transfer went through — their bank may have debited them and then reversed it, or the transfer may have failed silently.

What counts as proof? Your bank's SMS alert arriving on your own phone. Your balance increasing in your own bank app. The transaction appearing in your own account statement. A payment showing as successful in your Paystack, Flutterwave, Opay, or Moniepoint dashboard. These are records from your own bank or provider, and a fraudster cannot fake them on your phone. This rule applies to everyone, including regular customers and people who seem trustworthy. It is not about distrust. It is about a consistent process.

## How to Confirm a Transfer Step by Step

Here is a simple process to follow for every bank transfer payment, whether the customer is in your shop or paying for a delivery.

Step one: agree the amount and the account before the customer pays. Tell the customer the exact total, including any delivery fee, and confirm which account they should pay into. If you have more than one account, tell them which one to use. This avoids confusion later when you are checking for the payment.

Step two: ask the customer to pay and wait for your confirmation. Do not hand over the goods while the transfer is still processing. Tell the customer clearly: "Once I see the alert on my phone, I will give you the goods." Most honest customers accept this without argument.

Step three: check your own phone. Look for the bank alert SMS, or open your bank app and check your balance and recent transactions. The payment should show the customer's name or the name on their account, the amount, and the time. Check that the amount matches exactly what you agreed. A payment of ₦49,000 when the price is ₦50,000 is not a full payment, even if the customer insists it is.

Step four: if the alert has not arrived after two or three minutes, check again. Bank alerts can be delayed by network problems, especially on MTN or Airtel during busy hours. Open your bank app and refresh your transaction history. If the money is there, the alert may simply be late. If it is not there after five minutes, ask the customer to check their own bank app to confirm the transfer was successful on their side.

Step five: only release the goods when you have seen the payment in your own account. If the customer is in a hurry, explain that this protects both of you. Once the payment is confirmed, give them the goods and a receipt.

For deliveries, the process is the same, but the rider must follow it at the customer's gate. Train your riders to check with you before handing over the goods. The rider calls or messages you, you confirm the payment on your phone, and then you tell the rider to release the goods. A rider should never accept a screenshot as proof. If you cannot be reached, the rider should not release the goods until you confirm.

## How to Spot a Fake Bank Alert

Fake alerts are getting better, but they still have tells. Here is what to look for when a customer shows you an alert or a screenshot:

Check the sender. Real bank alerts in Nigeria usually come from a short code or a sender name that matches the bank, such as "GTBank", "Access Bank", or a number like 0800 or 3030 depending on the bank. If the message came from a regular phone number, like an 0803 or 0906 number, it is almost certainly fake. Some fraudsters use apps that send messages with a fake sender name, so this check alone is not enough, but it catches many fakes.

Check the details. A real alert shows the amount, your account number or name, the available balance, and the date and time. If the balance looks wrong — for example, it shows a balance that does not match what you know is in your account — the alert is fake. If the message has spelling mistakes, odd spacing, or a different format from your usual alerts, be suspicious.

Check the time. If the alert says the transfer happened at 2:15 p.m. but it is only 2:10 p.m., it is fake. If the customer claims they paid an hour ago but your account shows no payment, something is wrong.

Check your own phone. This is the real test. No matter how convincing the customer's screen looks, open your own bank app and check your balance. If the money is not there, the payment did not happen, whatever the customer's phone says.

Be especially careful with transfers from unfamiliar banks or fintech apps you do not use. Some fraudsters use fake apps that mimic Opay, Palmpay, or Kuda alerts. If you do not recognise the format, take extra time to verify in your own account before you release the goods.

## What to Do When a Payment Is Delayed

Sometimes a customer genuinely pays, but the money does not arrive immediately. Network problems, bank system maintenance, or interbank delays can hold up a transfer for minutes or even hours. Here is how to handle it without losing the sale or the goods.

First, do not panic, and do not accuse the customer of fraud. Ask them to show you the debit alert or the transaction history on their own bank app. A genuine customer will have a debit alert showing the money left their account, with your name as the recipient. If they cannot show any debit, they probably did not pay.

Second, ask for the transaction reference or session ID. Every bank transfer in Nigeria has a reference number, usually a long string of numbers and letters. Ask the customer to read it out or copy it to you on WhatsApp. You can give this reference to your bank if you need to trace the payment.

Third, wait a reasonable time. For a transfer between two accounts in the same bank, the money should arrive in seconds. For a transfer between different banks, it can take a few minutes. If it has been more than 30 minutes and the money is still not in your account, ask the customer to contact their bank. Their bank can confirm whether the transfer was successful or failed.

Fourth, do not release the goods until the money arrives. If the customer needs the goods urgently, you can offer to keep the goods aside for them and deliver as soon as the payment is confirmed. An honest customer will accept this. A fraudster will pressure you to release the goods now, or they will leave and never come back — which tells you everything you need to know.

If the customer's bank debited them but the money never reached you, the money is usually reversed back to the customer automatically within 24 hours. Tell the customer to check with their bank, and keep a record of the transaction reference, the amount, the date, and the customer's phone number, in case you need to follow up.

## Tools and Methods That Make Confirmation Easier

You do not have to rely on SMS alerts alone. Several tools make payment confirmation faster and more reliable.

Use a dedicated business account. Keep your business money in an account that is only for business. This makes it much easier to check whether a payment arrived, because every transaction in that account is a business transaction. If you mix business and personal money in one account, it is harder to tell which alert belongs to which customer.

Turn on instant alerts for your business account. Make sure your bank sends you an SMS or a push notification for every credit. If your alerts are not working, visit your bank or call customer care to fix it. Some banks charge a small monthly fee for SMS alerts, but it is worth it. If SMS alerts are unreliable, use your bank's mobile app with push notifications instead, which are usually free and faster.

Use a payment link or a payment page instead of a direct transfer. When a customer pays through a Paystack Payment Page or a Flutterwave Payment Link, the payment is confirmed in your dashboard automatically, and you get an email receipt. This removes the whole problem of checking alerts, because the dashboard is your proof. For online orders, this is the safest method.

Use Opay or Moniepoint for instant confirmation. Transfers between Opay accounts, or between Moniepoint accounts, are usually instant and free, and the alert arrives in seconds. If your customer also uses Opay or Moniepoint, ask them to pay that way.

Keep a payment log. Write down every payment you confirm: the date, the customer's name, the amount, the product, and the transaction reference. A notebook is fine, or a simple spreadsheet on your phone. This log is your evidence if a customer later claims they paid and you did not deliver, or if a bank asks for details about a disputed transfer.

Send proper invoices with your account details and the exact amount. A clear invoice reduces mistakes and gives the customer something to refer to when they pay. ## How to Train Your Staff and Riders

Your payment routine is only as strong as the weakest person who handles a sale. Train everyone who takes payments — shop attendants, cashiers, and delivery riders — on the same rules.

Rule one: never release goods without confirmation. This is the core rule. Every attendant must understand that a screenshot is not proof, and that they must see the payment on the shop's phone or in the shop's bank app before they hand over anything.

Rule two: know who to call. If an attendant is unsure, they should call you or the manager, not decide on their own. Make sure the shop phone is always charged and that the bank app is logged in on it, so anyone can check a payment quickly.

Rule three: stay polite but firm. Attendants should explain the rule to customers in a friendly way: "Please give me one minute to confirm the payment on our phone, then I will give you your goods." Most customers respect this. If a customer becomes aggressive or tries to rush the attendant, that is a warning sign, and the attendant should call the manager.

Rule four: keep a record. Attendants should write down the customer's name, phone number, amount, and transaction reference for every transfer payment, even after it is confirmed. This takes seconds and it protects the business if there is a dispute later.

For riders, the rules are the same, with one addition: the rider must confirm with you before releasing goods at the customer's gate. The rider should never accept a screenshot, never accept a promise to pay later, and never leave goods with a neighbour or a security guard without your approval. If the customer cannot pay, the rider brings the goods back to the shop.

## What to Do If You Have Already Been Scammed

If you released goods based on a fake alert and the customer is gone, act quickly. There is a chance of recovering your money or your goods, but it drops fast with time.

First, report it to your bank immediately. Call your bank's customer care line or visit a branch and explain what happened. Give them the transaction reference from the fake alert, the customer's name and phone number, and the time of the incident. Your bank can sometimes flag the receiving account, but they can only act quickly if you report fast.

Second, report it to the police. Go to the nearest police station and make a formal report. Take the fake alert screenshot, the customer's phone number, and any CCTV footage from your shop if you have it. A police report is also useful if you need to make an insurance claim.

Third, report the phone number to your network provider and to the bank whose name was used in the fake alert. Banks and networks keep records of fraud reports, and your report may help stop the same fraudster from targeting others.

Fourth, review what went wrong and fix it. Was the attendant rushed? Was the shop phone dead? Was there no clear rule? Change the process so it does not happen again. The cheapest lesson is the one you learn from someone else's mistake. The second cheapest is the one you learn from your own, once.

Finally, tell other sellers in your area. Fraudsters often work the same market or the same street for days, targeting shop after shop. A warning to your neighbours can save them from the same loss.

## Your Checklist: A Payment Routine That Protects Your Business

Day one: open a dedicated business account if you do not have one, and turn on instant alerts. Make sure the bank app is installed on the shop phone and that you can log in.

Day two: write down your payment rule on a card and put it at the counter: "We confirm every transfer on our own phone before releasing goods." Train every attendant on the rule.

Day three: test your confirmation process. Ask a friend to transfer ₦100 to your business account and time how long it takes for the alert to arrive. Practise checking the payment in your bank app.

Day four: set up a payment link or a payment page for your online orders, so customers can pay through Paystack or Flutterwave and you can confirm in a dashboard instead of by screenshots.

Day five: start using the routine with every customer. Check your own phone, confirm the amount, record the transaction reference, and only then release the goods.

A bank transfer is a good way to get paid in Nigeria, but only when you confirm it properly. Check your own account, never trust a screenshot, and make confirmation a habit, not an exception. The minute you spend checking a payment is the cheapest insurance your business will ever buy.
