Every day in Lagos, Abuja, Port Harcourt, and Ibadan, busy workers skip breakfast, office staff order lunch, and families look for someone reliable to cook for a birthday or a small party. Many of them would rather buy from a home cook they trust than eat fast food every day.

That is your opportunity. If you can cook well, you can turn your kitchen into a business without renting a restaurant or buying expensive equipment. You can start small, with the pots and stove you already have, and grow as orders grow.

But a home food business is not just cooking for friends. It is a business, and it needs planning: what to sell, how to price it, how to keep food safe, how to package and deliver it, and how to find customers who order again and again. This guide covers all of it, step by step.

## Step 1: Choose What to Sell

Do not try to sell everything. Pick one or two things you cook really well, and build your name around them.

Good starting options in Nigeria include small chops and finger foods for parties, soups and stews in litres for busy families, lunch packs for offices, cakes and pastries, snacks like meat pie, doughnuts, and chin chin, and local dishes like ofada rice, amala, or jollof rice in bowls. Think about what people in your area already buy, and what you cook better than most people around you.

Test your idea before you spend money. Cook your chosen food for ten people — neighbours, colleagues, church members — and ask them honestly: would you pay for this, and how much? If they say yes without hesitation, you have a product. If they are polite but vague, try another dish.

Also think about how long the food lasts and how easy it is to deliver. Soups in sealed containers travel well. Fried food goes soggy if delivery takes two hours in Lagos traffic. Cakes need careful handling. Choose food that still tastes good when it reaches the customer, not just when it leaves your kitchen.

Finally, check your time. A food that takes six hours to prepare may not work if you also have a job or children to care for. Pick food you can cook in batches, on a schedule you can keep every day.

## Step 2: Know Your Costs and Set Your Price

Many home food businesses fail because the owner never counts the cost. She sells a lot, but at the end of the month there is no profit, because the ingredients, gas, transport, and packaging ate it all.

Write down every cost for one batch. For example, to cook a pot of egusi soup, list the egusi, stockfish, meat, crayfish, palm oil, pepper, vegetables, seasoning, gas, water, and the containers. Add the cost of delivery if you deliver, and the cost of packaging.

Then divide by the number of portions. If a pot costs 25,000 naira to cook and gives you ten portions, each portion costs 2,500 naira before your time and profit. If you sell it for 3,000 naira, you make only 500 naira per portion, which is too little. If you sell it for 4,500 naira, you make 2,000 naira per portion, which is healthier.

A simple rule many Nigerian food sellers use is to price at two to two and a half times the ingredient cost, so there is room for gas, packaging, transport, and profit. Prices also depend on your area: a lunch pack that sells for 3,500 naira in Ikorodu may sell for 6,000 naira in Lekki, because customers there expect to pay more and your rent and transport may cost more too.

Check what similar sellers charge on Instagram and WhatsApp in your area, but do not copy them blindly. Their costs may be different from yours. Price from your own costs, then compare with the market to be sure you are not far off.

Keep a small notebook for costs. Food prices in Nigerian markets change often — a bag of rice or a carton of chicken can jump in price in a week. When prices change, update your costs and adjust your prices. Customers understand a small increase if you explain it and keep the quality the same.

## Step 3: Sort Out the Legal and Safety Basics

You do not need a big office or a company registration to start cooking from home, but you should take the basic steps that protect you and your customers.

First, food safety. This is the foundation of your business. One case of food poisoning can destroy your reputation completely. Always wash your hands before cooking, keep raw meat away from cooked food, cook meat and chicken thoroughly, and store food in clean, covered containers. Use clean water for cooking and washing, and keep your kitchen and cooking area clean every day.

Second, get a food handler's medical certificate if you can. Many local government health offices in Lagos and Abuja issue them after a simple medical test. It is cheap, it shows customers you are serious, and some corporate clients will ask for it before they order.

Third, register your business name with the Corporate Affairs Commission when you can afford it. It is not required on day one, but it makes your business look professional, helps you open a business bank account, and helps when you want to supply offices or schools.

Fourth, keep your cooking and selling separate from your family food where possible. Use separate containers and utensils for business orders, and label them. This avoids mix-ups and keeps your business food fresh.

Finally, be honest about allergens and ingredients. If a customer asks whether a dish contains groundnuts, pepper, or crayfish, answer truthfully. Some people have allergies, and a wrong answer can make someone seriously ill.

## Step 4: Set Up Your Kitchen for Business

You do not need a new kitchen. You need your current kitchen organised for business.

Start with the basics: a reliable gas cooker or stove, enough pots in the right sizes, a freezer or a fridge that works well, clean containers with tight lids, a measuring scale, and packaging materials. A small weighing scale helps you serve the same portion every time, so customers get the same value on every order.

Buy packaging that suits your food. For soups, use strong, leak-proof containers with tight lids. For rice and lunch packs, use food-grade disposable packs or foil containers. For cakes, use proper cake boxes. Test your packaging before you sell: put soup in the container, close it, turn it on its side, and see if it leaks. A leaking container is a refund and a lost customer.

Keep a separate storage area for business ingredients and packaging, even if it is just a shelf or a box. This stops you from using business stock for family cooking and then running short on an order day.

Plan your cooking days. Many home food sellers cook on set days — for example, soups on Mondays and Thursdays, lunch packs on weekdays, and cakes only on order. A clear schedule helps you buy ingredients in bulk, which is cheaper, and helps customers know when to order.

## Step 5: Take Orders the Simple Way

Customers in Nigeria order mainly on WhatsApp, so make ordering easy there. Create a WhatsApp Business account, set your business name, a clear profile photo of your food, your location, and your opening hours.

Write a simple menu with prices. For example: "Jollof rice with chicken — 4,500 naira. Egusi soup, 1 litre — 8,000 naira. Small chops pack (10 pieces) — 5,000 naira." Share the menu as a photo or a short message, and pin it in your WhatsApp Business catalogue so customers can see it any time.

Set clear ordering rules and share them. For example: "Orders close by 9am for same-day lunch. Payment confirms your order. Delivery within Lekki is 1,500 naira, outside Lekki depends on location." Clear rules prevent arguments and wasted food.

Always confirm payment before you cook a large order. For small orders from regular customers, you may cook first and collect on delivery, but for new customers and large orders, ask for payment or at least a deposit first. Many food sellers have lost money cooking for a customer who never came to collect the food.

Keep an order book — a notebook or a simple phone note — with the customer's name, phone number, order, amount paid, delivery address, and delivery time. Check it every morning so no order is forgotten.

## Step 6: Package and Deliver Like a Professional

Good food in bad packaging loses customers. Good packaging makes your food look valuable and keeps it safe.

Label your food. A small sticker with your business name, phone number, and the date cooked makes your product look professional and helps customers find you again. Include a short note: "Thank you for your order. Please refrigerate and eat within two days." That small note shows care.

For delivery, you have three main options. You can deliver yourself if the customer is nearby. You can use a dispatch rider, which is common in Lagos and Abuja — agree the price with the customer before you send the food, and use a rider you trust. Or you can offer pickup, where customers come to your house or a nearby landmark to collect their order. Pickup saves delivery cost and works well in estates and neighbourhoods.

Whatever you choose, be honest about delivery time. Lagos traffic is unpredictable, so give a time range, not an exact minute. "Delivery between 12pm and 1pm" is better than "Delivery at 12pm sharp" if you cannot guarantee it. If you are running late, call or message the customer before they call you. A short message — "Good afternoon, your food is on the way, the rider will reach you in about 20 minutes" — prevents anger.

Keep hot food hot and cold food cold. Use insulated bags or foil to keep food warm, and do not leave cooked food sitting out for hours. If a customer complains that food arrived cold, apologise, fix it, and learn from it.

## Step 7: Find Customers in Your Neighbourhood First

Your first customers are usually the people closest to you: neighbours, colleagues, church members, school parents, and estate residents. Start there before you spend money on adverts.

Tell people what you sell, clearly and often. Post your food photos on your WhatsApp Status every day, with the price and how to order. Share in your estate WhatsApp group, your church group, and your old school group, but do not spam. One good post a day with a beautiful photo is better than ten posts that people ignore.

Offer a first-order incentive. For example, a free sample with the first order, or a small discount for the first ten customers. People are more willing to try a new cook when the risk is low.

Partner with people who already have customers. A salon, a gym, a small office, or a school may let you supply lunch or snacks for their staff. Offer the manager a free tasting first. One office that orders twenty lunch packs every weekday is worth more than a hundred one-off customers.

Use Instagram and TikTok to show your cooking, not just the finished food. Short videos of you cooking, packing, and delivering build trust and make people hungry. Post the price and location in the caption so people know how to order.

Ask happy customers to share your number. A short message — "If you enjoyed the food, please share my number with a friend who may like it" — works well. Word of mouth is still the strongest marketing for food in Nigeria.

Start with customers you already know, and ask them to pay on time. A neighbour who orders every week and pays promptly is worth more than a stranger who orders once and argues about the price. Build your business around reliable, repeat customers first, and let strangers join later through their recommendations.

## Step 8: Keep Customers Coming Back

Getting a first order is hard. Getting the same customer to order again is where the profit is.

Be consistent. The jollof rice a customer loved last week should taste the same this week. Use the same recipe, the same portions, and the same packaging. If you change a supplier and the taste changes, customers will notice.

Follow up after delivery. Send a short message the same day: "Good evening, thank you for your order. How was the food?" Listen to the answer. If they say the pepper was too much, reduce it next time. If they say the portion was small, adjust it. That follow-up is what turns a one-time buyer into a regular.

Offer a simple loyalty reward. For example, after five orders, the sixth small chops pack is free, or regular customers get free delivery on Fridays. You do not need a fancy loyalty card — a note in your order book is enough.

Introduce new dishes slowly. Once a month, add one new item and tell your regular customers first. "We have added ofada rice this week, would you like to try it?" Regulars love to feel like insiders, and a new dish gives them a reason to order again.

Handle complaints fast and kindly. If food arrives late, cold, or wrong, apologise first, then fix it: replace it, refund it, or offer a discount on the next order. A customer whose complaint was handled well often becomes more loyal than a customer who never had a problem.

## A Real Example: A Soup Seller in Abuja

A woman in Kubwa, Abuja, started selling soups from home with 50,000 naira. She chose two soups she cooked well — egusi and ogbono — and sold them in one-litre containers for 7,500 naira each.

She posted her menu in her estate WhatsApp group and on her Status every morning, took orders by 9am, cooked in the afternoon, and delivered in the evening. She used strong containers with her business name on a sticker, and she always sent a thank-you message after delivery.

In her first month, she had fifteen regular customers. By the third month, she had forty, including three families who ordered every week and one small office that ordered lunch packs on Fridays. Her profit after ingredients, gas, and packaging was about 120,000 naira a month, and she was cooking only four days a week.

She did not have a shop, a website, or a big advert budget. She had a good product, clear prices, clean packaging, and customers who trusted her. That is the model to copy.

## Your First Week Plan

Day one and two: choose your food, cost it, and set your prices. Day three: buy packaging, take good photos of your food, and write your menu. Day four: tell your neighbours, your estate group, and your WhatsApp contacts that you are open for orders. Day five: cook your first paid orders, deliver on time, and ask for feedback. Day six: review what sold, what it cost, and what you earned. Day seven: rest, plan the next week, and post your best customer feedback on your Status.

Start small, cook well, deliver on time, and listen to your customers. A home food business grows one satisfied customer at a time, and in Nigeria, a satisfied customer tells her neighbours, her colleagues, and her family. That is how a kitchen becomes a business.
