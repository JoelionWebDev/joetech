Ada runs a small braiding salon off Admiralty Way in Lekki. Her hands are fast, her work is neat, and her Instagram page is full of styles that get saved and shared. Yet every Monday morning she opens her appointment book and half the week is blank. By Thursday she is posting "slots available" on her status, and by Saturday she is turning people away because three walk-ins arrived at once. The problem is not her skill. Her customers have no reason to book ahead, and no reason to come back to her instead of the next salon that posts a fine picture.

Chidi cuts hair in a two-chair shop in Surulere, and he has the same problem from the other side of Lagos. His regulars will tell you "you are my barber" and then disappear for six weeks, try the new shop near the junction, and drift back whenever they remember. He earns well on full days and almost nothing on empty ones. He cannot plan, he cannot save, and he cannot tell which weeks will pay his rent.

Both of them are appointment businesses, and appointment businesses need a different kind of loyalty from a shop that sells dresses or snacks. When a boutique gives a free item, it loses the cost of that item. When a salon chair sits empty for two hours, it loses the whole two hours. That time never comes back. Nobody can buy Tuesday morning again on Wednesday. So loyalty for a service business is not about giving things away. It is about filling the diary before the week starts, and making sure the same faces return on a rhythm you can count on.

## The reward is a slot, not a free item

A customer who buys a bag of rice gets the rice and goes home. A customer who books a hair appointment buys your time, your hands and a chair for two or three hours. That changes what a reward can be. The most valuable thing you can offer a regular is not a free product. It is certainty: her usual chair, her usual day, her usual time, waiting for her, without a queue and without a fight over the phone.

Think about Mrs. Okafor, who gets her hair done every three weeks and hates sitting in a salon all day. What keeps her with Ada is not a free bottle of shampoo. It is a standing slot: every third Saturday, 9am, her chair, her stylist, finished by noon so she can still make her owambe. That slot costs Ada nothing extra to give. It only asks her to plan her diary around the people who already pay her.

## Pre-paid session packages: money in before the work starts

The simplest version of service loyalty is the pre-paid package. Instead of paying per visit, the customer pays for several sessions upfront and gets one extra session, or a better rate, in return. A home cleaning company in Ikeja might sell four Saturday deep cleans for ₦120,000 paid upfront, with the fifth clean at half price. A tutor in Yaba might sell eight Saturday maths lessons for ₦80,000, with the ninth lesson free. A spa in Victoria Island might sell five facials for ₦150,000, with the sixth free.

Run the numbers with a real example. Ada charges ₦25,000 for a full braids session. She offers a package: pay ₦125,000 upfront for five sessions and the sixth session is free. The customer saves ₦25,000 across six visits. Ada receives ₦125,000 in one transfer, on a Monday, before she has lifted a comb. That cash pays for attachment, for the new dryer she has been postponing, for the week when NEPA and the generator both misbehave. More than the money, she now has a customer who has already paid to come back five more times. That customer will not drift to the salon near the junction, because her money is already sitting with Ada.

Chidi does the same at a smaller scale. A haircut is ₦5,000 in his shop. He offers ten haircuts for ₦45,000 paid upfront, and the eleventh is free. Chidi gets ₦45,000 in a week when he needs to pay his shop rent, and his chair has a guaranteed customer every second Saturday.

Packages need rules, and the rules must be said out loud before the money changes hands. Write them on a card and hand it over with the receipt. How long does the package last? Three months for a five-session hair package is fair; six months for a cleaning package is fair. A package that never expires is a debt you carry forever, and one that expires in four weeks makes customers feel cheated. Can it be shared with a sister or a friend? Say no, gently: the price was set for one person's head or one person's flat. If the customer misses a booked slot without telling you, she loses that session or pays a small rebooking fee of ₦2,000. Say it with a smile when she pays, not with a quarrel when she cancels.

The most common fear is that customers will not pay upfront. Some will not, and that is fine. Offer the package to five of your best customers in person, not on a broadcast, and five people saying yes is enough to start.

## Monthly membership slots: rent for your chair

A package is a block of sessions. A membership is a standing arrangement: the customer pays a fixed amount every month and holds a regular slot. A salon can offer ₦60,000 a month for four braids or retouch sessions, one every week, on a fixed day. A barber can offer ₦18,000 a month for four haircuts, one every Saturday morning. A cleaning company can offer ₦100,000 a month for a weekly two-hour clean of a three-bedroom flat in Lekki Phase 1. A tutor can offer ₦50,000 a month for eight lessons, two every week.

Memberships do something packages cannot: they smooth your income across the month. Ada knows that on the first of every month, twelve members will pay ₦60,000 each. That is ₦720,000 she can count on before the month begins, even in a slow week when walk-ins are few. Her members get their fixed slots and a small saving against the walk-in price, and they feel like they belong to the salon, not like they are begging for a chair.

Keep it simple. One price, one slot, one rule for changes. Let a member move her slot once a month with 48 hours' notice, free of charge. After that, a moved slot costs ₦3,000, or she waits for the next open slot. That sounds strict, but it protects the diary. Collect the money on a fixed date, the first of the month, and remind members on the 28th with a short WhatsApp message: "Good evening, your October membership of ₦60,000 is due on the 1st. Your slot is Saturday 9am as usual. Thank you." No long message, no begging, no discount for paying late. Customers respect a business that treats its own rules seriously.

## Rebooking before the customer reaches the door

Packages and memberships lock in the committed customers. Rebooking is what keeps everyone else from drifting. The cheapest loyalty habit in a service business costs nothing at all: before a customer leaves, book her next appointment.

Ada used to say "see you soon" and wave her customers out. Now she says something different while she is still holding the mirror up for them to check the back. "Your hair will need a retouch in three weeks. I have Saturday the 18th at 10am free, or the 25th at 9am. Which one do you want?" Most of them pick one. She writes it in her book, sends a WhatsApp message that evening with the date and time, and the customer leaves with a plan instead of a vague intention.

The reminder matters as much as the booking. Two days before the appointment, send a short message: "Good morning, Mrs. Okafor. Reminder of your appointment this Saturday at 9am. Please let me know if anything changes." Customers forget, traffic happens, children fall sick. A reminder is not pestering. It is the difference between a full chair and an empty one, and it is also a kindness.

No-shows still happen, and you need a policy for them. The first time a customer misses without word, call her, be warm, and rebook her. The second time, ask for a ₦5,000 deposit to hold the next slot, deducted from the session price. The customers who value your time will understand immediately.

## What to reward, and what to stop giving away

Service businesses often reward the wrong things. A free product after ten visits sounds generous, but the customer came for the service, not the product. Reward the behaviour that fills your diary instead: the customer who rebooks before she leaves, the one who arrives on time every time, because her punctuality lets the next customer start on time too, and the one who brings a friend to sit in the next chair. A referred customer tends to stay longer, complain less, and book the same way her friend does.

Keep the rewards in services you already deliver. After five paid sessions, the sixth is free. After ten on-time arrivals, a free deep-conditioning treatment or hot-towel finish worth ₦5,000, which costs you little and feels like luxury. After a referral who books and pays, give the referrer a free add-on on her next visit. The rewards are small, and they never touch your stock.

Stop giving away discounts at the till. A customer who asks "is there a discount?" every visit is not loyal, she is trained. Offer the package instead: "I cannot reduce the single price, but if you take the package of five, you save ₦25,000 across the six visits."

## Keep a book, not a feeling

None of this works on memory. Ada keeps one notebook with four columns: name, phone number, package or membership, sessions used. Every time a customer sits down, Ada ticks a box. When the boxes run out, she says so with a smile and offers the next package. Chidi keeps the same notebook beside his mirror and checks it on Sunday night: who has not been in for five weeks, who is due a free cut, who needs a reminder message on Monday morning.

## Start with five people

You do not need an app, a printed card, or a launch event. This week, pick five regulars. Offer two of them a pre-paid package, offer two of them a monthly membership slot, and ask the fifth to book her next appointment before she leaves. Write the rules on a small card. Send the reminders. Then do it again next week with five more people.

Six months from now, the difference will show up in your diary, not in your follower count. The mornings will be booked before the week begins, and the rent will be covered by membership money that arrived on the first.
