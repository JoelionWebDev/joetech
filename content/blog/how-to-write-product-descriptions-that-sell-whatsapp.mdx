It is 9:14 on a Tuesday night. Blessing, a nurse in Abuja, is scrolling through a WhatsApp catalogue on her phone while her food warms in the microwave. She likes a brown leather handbag. The photo is good. The description under it says: "Bag available. DM for price."

She taps the message button anyway. "Good evening ma, how much is the bag?" Then she waits. The seller, Funke, is asleep. By morning Blessing has seen three other bags on her status feed, and Funke's bag is forgotten. Nobody did anything wrong. The description just made Blessing work for information she should have had in five seconds.

That slow drip of questions is the normal life of a WhatsApp seller in Nigeria. "How much?" "Is it available in black?" "Do you deliver to Abuja?" "How much is delivery?" "Can I pay on delivery?" Each question arrives as its own message, sometimes hours apart. A sale that should take four minutes stretches across a whole day, and half the time the customer drifts away in between.

The fix is not replying faster, though that helps. The fix is writing the description so the chat never has to happen at all — or so short that the only message left is "I want the brown one, here is my address."

## Let Your Last Week of Chats Write Your Descriptions

Open your WhatsApp and scroll back through seven days of customer messages. Ignore the greetings and the "thank you" notes. Circle the questions that keep coming back. For most sellers they are the same four or five, in almost the same order: the price, whether the size or colour they want is still there, the delivery fee to their area, how long delivery takes, and how to pay.

Those repeated questions are your description. Every question a customer has asked twice belongs in the catalogue entry, written out, before the next customer has to ask it a third time.

Funke did this one Sunday afternoon with her handbag catalogue. She counted: in one week, eleven people asked the price, nine asked about delivery to places outside Lagos, and six asked whether the bag came in black. Her descriptions had answered none of those things. She rewrote all fourteen bag entries that evening. The next week, the "how much?" messages dropped to two, and both of those were from people who had not opened the catalogue at all — they had only seen the bag on her status.

Do the same count for your own shop. It takes twenty minutes and it tells you exactly what to write, because your customers have already told you what they need to know.

## Price, Availability and Delivery Belong in the Description Itself

Start with the price, stated plainly, in naira, in the description. Not "DM for price." Not "price in bio." In the description, next to the product. "Price: ₦32,000." A buyer who can see the price can decide. A buyer who cannot see it has to start a conversation with a stranger, wait for a reply, and risk an awkward haggle — and most of them will not bother, because the next seller's catalogue already shows the price.

Availability is the second line, and it should be specific. "In stock in black and brown. Blue sold out." That one sentence does three jobs: it tells the buyer what they can actually buy, it stops the "is blue available?" message, and it quietly creates a reason to order now, because blue is already gone. When a size sells out, change the line the same day. A customer who orders a size 42 shoe, pays, and then hears "sorry, 42 finished yesterday" will not order from you again, and she will tell her sister why.

Delivery is the part sellers hide most, and it causes the most cancelled orders. Write it in the description, area by area, the way you actually charge. "Delivery: Lekki and Ajah ₦1,500. Lagos mainland ₦2,500. Abuja and Port Harcourt ₦3,500, 2 to 3 days." A buyer in Enugu who sees that line knows the full cost before she pays. A buyer who only finds out about a ₦3,500 delivery fee after she has transferred ₦32,000 feels cheated, even when the fee is fair.

Payment goes in too, in one short line. "Pay by transfer before dispatch. Pay on delivery available in Lekki only." That line ends the "can I pay on delivery?" argument before it starts, and it protects you from a dispatch rider standing at a gate in Ibadan with a parcel nobody will pay for.

## Write It the Way You Would Say It

Here is a test that catches almost every bad catalogue description: read it out loud, as if you were sending the buyer a voice note. If you stumble, or if it sounds like a textbook, rewrite it. Your customers already hear your voice in their heads when they read your messages. The description should sound like the same person.

Stiff writing sounds like this: "This premium quality handbag is crafted from superior genuine leather material and features multiple compartments for optimal organisation." Nobody talks like that in a shop in Ikeja. Spoken writing sounds like this: "Soft brown leather bag, handmade here in Lagos. Fits your laptop, your purse and your phone, and it still looks neat on your shoulder. The zip is strong — it will not spoil after two months."

Read that second version again. Short sentences. Words a customer would use. One honest detail about the zip, which is the thing that actually breaks on cheap bags. That is the whole trick: write down what you would say if the woman were standing in front of you, holding the bag.

Try recording yourself. Hold the product, tap the voice note button, and describe it to an imaginary customer in thirty seconds. Then play it back and type out what you said, tidying only the "ehns" and the pauses. The result will read better than anything you write by staring at a blank screen, because your speaking voice already knows what matters about the product and your writing voice is still trying to sound impressive.

## Laying Out the Catalogue So Buyers Find Things

A catalogue is a small shop, and shops have an order to them. The first thing a customer sees should be your best sellers, the products people already ask for by name. New arrivals come next. Everything else follows, grouped by type: all the dresses together, all the shoes together, all the bags together. Never group by the date you added them, which is what WhatsApp does by default if you just keep adding — that leaves a customer scrolling past a Christmas dress in June to find the sandals she actually wants.

The catalogue description field is the signboard over the shop door. Most sellers leave it blank, or write "Welcome to my shop." Use it properly. In that one short field, say where you are, when you reply, where you deliver, and how payment works. "Lekki Phase 1. We reply 8am to 9pm. Delivery across Lagos and nationwide. Transfer before dispatch, pay on delivery in Lekki." A buyer reads that once and never has to ask where you are based.

Hide an item the day it sells out instead of leaving it up with a dead description. WhatsApp lets you hide catalogue items without deleting them, so bringing a restocked product back takes one tap. A catalogue full of things a buyer cannot have feels like a shop with empty shelves — people stop browsing.

And keep one format across every entry. Same order every time: what it is, the one thing that makes it good, the sizes or colours left, the price, delivery, how to order. When every entry follows the same shape, a buyer who has read one entry can read the next in five seconds flat, because her eye already knows where the price sits.

## Before and After: One Real Catalogue Entry

Funke sells handmade tote bags from her shop in Ikeja. This was her catalogue entry for her best-selling bag in October:

> Tote bag available. DM for price. Different colours available.

Fourteen words, and not one of them helps a buyer decide anything. No price, no colours named, no size, no delivery, no order instruction. Every sale from that entry cost her six or seven messages back and forth.

This is the entry she replaced it with:

> Brown leather tote bag, handmade in Lagos. Soft leather, strong stitching, fits a 13-inch laptop and your everyday things.
> Colours left: brown and black. Tan sold out.
> Price: ₦32,000.
> Delivery: Ikeja and mainland ₦2,000. Lekki ₦2,500. Abuja ₦3,500, 2 to 3 days.
> Send a message with your colour and address to order. Transfer confirms your order.

Same bag, same photo, same price. The difference is that the second version answers everything Blessing asked at 9:14 that Tuesday night before she ever opens the chat. The buyer knows the price, the colours left, the delivery fee to her city, and exactly what to type to order. The only message Funke needs to receive now is the order itself.

Notice what the new entry does not do. It does not shout. It does not promise the bag will change the buyer's life. It does not list ten features. It answers five questions, in the order a buyer asks them, in words Funke would actually say across a counter.

## Keeping the Catalogue Honest

A good description goes stale fast in a Nigerian market. Prices move when the dollar moves. A size finishes on a Saturday and three people order it on Sunday. Your catalogue needs a small weekly habit, not a big overhaul.

Pick one evening a week — Sunday works for most sellers, when the shop is quiet — and spend ten minutes on it. Open each entry and check three things: is the price still right, are the sizes and colours listed still the ones you actually have, and is anything sold out still showing? Change what needs changing, hide what is gone, and you are done. Ten minutes a week saves you the angry message, the refund, and the customer who never comes back.

The same habit applies when a price changes. Change it in the catalogue first, before you post the product on your status. A status post with yesterday's price and a catalogue with today's price is an argument waiting to happen, and the customer will always believe the lower one.

Rewrite your five best sellers tonight, using the questions your own customers have already asked you. Read each one out loud like a voice note. If it sounds like you, and it answers the price, the availability, the delivery and the payment before the buyer types a word, it is ready. Your chat will get quieter, and your orders will get faster — because the selling is already done before the conversation starts.
