A woman walks out of a salon in Ikeja with her hair looking exactly the way she wanted it. Her friend sees it at church the next day and asks where she did it. That is a referral. No link was shared, no code was typed, no form was filled. One woman's good work, seen by another woman, in real life.

Service businesses earn referrals differently from shops. A shop sells a product a customer can photograph and forward. A service sells an experience that lives on the customer's body, in her home, or on her plate. The customer is the proof. That makes referrals more powerful for services — and harder to systematise, because the moment of recommendation happens face to face, far away from you.

This guide is for the businesses where the work is done by a person, for a person: hair salons and barbershops, tailors and fashion designers, caterers and bakers, cleaners, makeup artists, photographers, phone and appliance repairers, plumbers, electricians, tutors and event planners. If your customer has to trust you with her hair, her wedding food, or her phone, this is how referrals work for you.

## The work is the advert

Before any programme, be honest about one thing: a referral is a customer putting her reputation on the line for you. When a woman recommends her tailor to her sister, she is saying "I trust this person with your aso-ebi." If the dress comes back late or badly sewn, the sister does not blame the tailor alone. She blames the woman who recommended her.

So the first rule of service referrals is that the service must be recommendable. Deliver what you promised, when you promised it. A caterer who delivers hot food on time at a wedding will be recommended by the bride to every engaged woman she knows. A tailor who delivers a dress a day late will be warned about in the same circles.

Add one small touch that makes the experience memorable. A salon that offers a free scalp massage at the end. A tailor who calls the day before delivery to confirm the fitting. A repairer who cleans the phone and returns it in a small pouch. These touches cost almost nothing, and they are the details people mention when they recommend you. "She even called me the day before" is a sentence that travels.

## Ask at the reveal, not at the payment

Timing is everything, and for services the best moment is the reveal: the moment the customer sees the finished work and smiles. The hair is done and she is looking in the mirror. The dress is fitted and she is turning in front of it. The food has been tasted and the guests are asking who made it. The repaired phone is back in her hand and working. That is when her good feeling is strongest, and that is when to ask.

Say it naturally, in the moment: "I am so glad you love it. If you know anyone who needs the same, please send them to me. Tell them to mention your name, and I will take care of both of you." That is all. Do not hand her a flyer at that moment. Do not launch into the rules. Plant the idea while she is happy, and let her carry it home.

The worst moment to ask is when something went wrong, or when the customer is paying and counting her money. A customer who has just complained about a delay will not recommend you, and asking her to do so will only annoy her. Fix the problem first. Earn the recommendation, then ask for it.

For caterers and event planners, there is a second golden moment: during the event itself. The bride is happy, the guests are eating, and three people have already asked who made the food. Leave a few small cards on the buffet table or with the ushers: your name, your number, and one line — "Referred by a happy guest? Mention their name and get 10 percent off your first order." The guests take the card while the food is still in their mouths. That is the best advertising a caterer will ever get.

## Rewards that fit a service

Cash rewards are a poor fit for most service businesses. If a salon pays ₦2,000 cash for every referral, the money leaves the business and never comes back. Service rewards are better, because they bring the customer back through your door.

A salon can offer a free wash or a free treatment on the next visit. A barber can offer a free shape-up. A tailor can offer free delivery of the finished dress, or a free adjustment. A caterer can offer a free pack of small chops with the next order. A cleaner can offer one free extra room on the next booking. A repairer can offer a free screen protector or a free check-up.

These rewards cost you little — a wash during a quiet hour, a delivery you were making anyway — but they feel generous to the customer, because she receives a service, not a small sum of money. And they have a second benefit: the customer has to come back to use them, which means another visit, another conversation, and another chance to ask for the next referral.

Reward both sides when you can. The existing customer gets the free service, and the new customer gets a small welcome — 10 percent off the first appointment, or a free extra. A new customer who arrives with a discount already in hand is easier to please, and easier to convert into a regular.

## The "bring a friend" appointment

Service businesses have one referral trick that shops do not: the shared appointment. A salon customer who loves her stylist can bring a friend to the same visit — the friend watches, sees the work happen, and books her own appointment on the spot. A tailor can invite a customer's sister to the fitting. A makeup artist can offer a "two faces, one price" deal for a bride and her chief bridesmaid.

Make it easy. "Bring your friend next Saturday and you both get 10 percent off" is a sentence any customer can act on. It works because the friend does not have to trust an advert. She watches the work with her own eyes, in real time, and the decision makes itself.

## Tracking it in a notebook

You do not need software. You need one notebook page per month, with five columns: date, referrer name, new customer name, service done, reward given. Every time a new customer says "Ada sent me", write it down that day. At the end of the week, check the page. Who referred the most people? Who is owed a reward? Pay the reward within 24 hours, and send a message: "Thank you, Ada. Your friend Chiamaka came in today for braids. Your free wash is ready whenever you want it."

That message does two jobs. It proves you keep your promises, and it reminds Ada to send the next person. A reward paid late feels like a broken promise. A reward paid the same day feels like a business that means what it says.

At the end of the month, count. How many new customers came from referrals? How much did they spend? How much did the rewards cost? If you spent ₦15,000 on free washes and those customers spent ₦150,000, the programme is working. If the rewards cost more than the profit the new customers brought, change the reward, not the whole programme.

## When the referred customer is difficult

It will happen. A referred customer arrives with high expectations, complains about the price, or is rude to your staff. The temptation is to blame the referrer. Do not. The referrer did you a favour, and punishing her for it ends the referrals.

Treat the difficult customer professionally, deliver the work well, and if she does not come back, let it go quietly. Then tell the referrer something honest and kind: "Thank you for sending her. We did our best for her." That is all. The referrer will keep sending people, because she saw you handle a hard situation with grace. And the difficult customer's friends may still come — people judge a business by how it handles problems, not only by how it handles easy days.

## What to do this week

On Monday, write the names of your ten happiest customers — the ones who smile, who compliment the work, who come back without being chased. On Tuesday, tell each of them in person or on WhatsApp, at the moment of the reveal: "If you know anyone who needs this, send them to me and mention your name." On Wednesday, put a small sign where customers can see it: "Love your hair? Bring a friend — you both get 10 percent off." On Thursday, start the notebook page. On Friday, check who has already sent someone, and pay the first reward fast.

Service referrals grow slowly and then suddenly. One happy customer tells two friends. One of those friends becomes a regular and tells two more. A salon in Surulere that started with ten names in a notebook now gets half its new customers from referrals, and it has not run a single advert in eight months. The work was always good. The programme just gave customers a reason to talk about it out loud.
