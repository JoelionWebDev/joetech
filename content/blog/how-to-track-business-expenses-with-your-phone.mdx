Ask a small business owner how much they spent last month, and most cannot answer. Not approximately. Not even roughly. They know what came in — the sales, the transfers, the cash in the drawer — but what went out is a blur of transport fares, airtime, small purchases, fuel, food for staff, repairs, and a dozen other payments that felt too small to record. At the end of the month, the money is gone, the business feels broke, and nobody can say where it went.

That is the quiet way small businesses bleed. Not through one big loss, but through hundreds of small, unrecorded expenses. Tracking your expenses with your phone fixes it. Your phone is already with you when you spend the money, so recording the expense takes seconds. This guide shows you how to set up a simple system, which free tools to use, how to organise your spending, and how to turn the records into decisions that keep more money in your business.

## Why Most Small Businesses Lose Track of Money

The first reason is cash. Much of Nigerian small business still runs on cash, and cash leaves no trail. A bank transfer shows up in your statement. A cash payment to a rider, a market trader, or a fuel attendant leaves nothing behind except your memory — and memory is the worst accounting system ever invented.

The second reason is mixing. Business money and personal money live in the same pocket, the same account, the same wallet. You buy stock with business money, then buy lunch with the same money, then pay a supplier from the money a customer just paid you. When everything mixes, you cannot tell whether the business made a profit or not. You only know whether there is money left, and that is not the same thing.

The third reason is the belief that small amounts do not matter. A thousand naira here, two thousand there. But small amounts are exactly what sink a business, because they happen every day. A daily unrecorded expense of a few thousand naira adds up over a month to an amount that would have paid rent, bought stock, or covered a salary. The expenses that feel too small to record are the ones you most need to record.

The fourth reason is that the system feels too complicated. Many owners imagine expense tracking means accounting software, spreadsheets with formulas, and a bookkeeper they cannot afford. It does not. A simple system you actually use beats a perfect system you abandon after three days.

## The First Step: Separate Business Money From Personal Money

Before you track a single expense, do the one thing that makes everything else work: separate your business money from your personal money. Open a separate bank account for the business, or at minimum keep a separate wallet and a separate record for business cash. Business money goes in, business expenses come out, and you pay yourself a fixed amount — a salary, even a small one — instead of dipping into the business whenever you need something.

This separation is the foundation of honest numbers. When the business account only holds business money, every payment out of it is a business expense, and every payment in is business income. Your records become simple because the mixing is gone. Many owners resist this step because it feels restrictive, but it is the opposite: it is the first time you will actually know whether your business is making money.

If you cannot open a second bank account immediately, start with the habit: one physical wallet or envelope for business cash, and a rule that personal spending never comes from it. If you must take money for a personal need, record it as an owner withdrawal — money the business paid you — not as a business expense. That single distinction keeps your records honest.

## Choose Your Tool: Notes, Spreadsheet or an App

You do not need to pay for an expense tracker. Three free options work well on a phone, and the best one is the one you will actually open every day.

The simplest is your phone's notes app. Create one note for each month, and write each expense as a line: date, what it was, amount, category. It is basic, but it works, and it is better than no record at all. The weakness is that adding up categories takes manual effort at the end of the month.

The second option is a spreadsheet on your phone, using a free app like Google Sheets. Create columns for date, description, category and amount, and add a row for each expense. A spreadsheet adds up automatically, and you can sort and filter by category. It takes a few minutes to set up, and once it is set up, recording an expense takes about fifteen seconds. For many small businesses, this is the sweet spot: free, flexible, and always on your phone.

The third option is a dedicated expense tracking app from your phone's app store. These apps are designed for exactly this job: you type the amount, pick a category, and it saves the expense, shows you charts, and totals everything for you. Many have a free version that is enough for a small business. If you choose an app, pick one that lets you export your data, works offline, and does not ask for access to your bank account unless you are comfortable with that. Download only from your official app store, and test it for a week before you commit to it.

Whatever you choose, the tool matters far less than the habit. A notes app used daily beats an expensive app opened once a month.

## Set Up Categories That Match Your Business

Categories are what turn a list of expenses into useful information. Without them, you have a long list of numbers. With them, you can see that transport ate a large share of your money this month, or that data and airtime cost more than you thought, or that stock is your biggest expense by far.

Start with a short list that fits your business. A typical Nigerian small business might use: stock and supplies, transport and fuel, data and airtime, rent, staff salaries, marketing and advertising, equipment and repairs, food and refreshments, bank charges, and miscellaneous. Ten categories are enough. More than that, and recording becomes a chore. Fewer, and the picture is too blurry to act on.

Make the categories yours. A fashion business will have fabric and tailoring costs. A food business will have ingredients and gas. A service business may have almost no stock but heavy transport costs. Look at your last month's spending and create categories for the things you actually pay for, not the things a textbook says you should pay for.

Add one rule: every expense gets a category, and "miscellaneous" is the last resort, not the default. If miscellaneous grows large, it means your categories are wrong — look at what is inside it, and create a proper category for the biggest items.

## The Daily Habit: Record It the Moment You Spend

The system works only if recording is immediate. An expense recorded a week later is an expense remembered wrong, or forgotten entirely. The rule is simple: record it the moment you spend it, while you are still standing at the point of payment.

Build the habit into the payment itself. You pay, you open the app, you type the amount and pick the category, and you put the phone away. Fifteen seconds. Do it for every expense, including the small ones — especially the small ones. The rider fare, the bottle of water for a client, the printing at the business centre, the recharge card. If it came out of business money, it goes in the record.

Keep your receipts. For larger expenses, take a photo of the receipt with your phone and store it in a folder for that month, or attach it to the expense in your tracking app. Receipts matter for two reasons: they prove what you spent if a supplier disputes it, and they protect you if you ever need to show your records to a bank, a partner, or a tax authority. A photo costs nothing and weighs nothing, and a folder of receipt photos is the cheapest filing cabinet you will ever own.

At the end of each day, take one minute to check. Open your tracker and look at today's entries. Is everything there? Did you miss the transport fare this morning? Add it now, while you still remember it. A one-minute daily check catches the gaps that a weekly check never will, because by the end of the week you will have forgotten half of them.

## The Weekly Review: Turn Records Into Decisions

Recording expenses is only half the job. The other half is looking at them and acting on what you see. Set aside thirty minutes once a week — Sunday evening or Monday morning — to review the week.

First, add up the week by category. Which category was biggest? Was it stock, which earns money back, or was it transport, data, or miscellaneous, which do not? Second, look for surprises. Any expense you did not expect? Any category that is larger than last week? Third, look for patterns. Are you paying for the same repair again? Is one supplier costing more than the others? Are you spending on something every week that you could buy monthly for less?

Then make one decision. Just one, each week. Cancel a subscription you do not use. Buy an item in bulk instead of daily. Change your delivery route. Negotiate with a supplier. Renegotiate a service. One decision a week, based on real numbers, compounds into real savings over a year. This is the whole point of tracking: not the record itself, but the decisions the record makes possible.

Once a month, do a bigger review. Add up the month's income and the month's expenses. Did the business make a profit? How much? Where did the money go? Compare with last month. Is the business improving, or is spending growing faster than income? These are the questions that decide whether a business survives, and you can only answer them if you have the numbers.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

The first mistake is tracking income but not expenses. Income feels good to record; expenses feel like a chore. But a business that records only income is flying blind. Record both, every day, or the numbers will lie to you.

The second mistake is recording expenses weekly from memory. By the end of the week, you will have forgotten the small ones and misremembered the large ones. Record the moment you spend, and use the daily check to catch anything you missed.

The third mistake is having too many categories, or none at all. Too many categories make recording slow, so you stop doing it. No categories make the record useless, because you cannot see where the money went. Ten clear categories, reviewed once a month, is the right balance.

The fourth mistake is never looking at the numbers. A tracker you fill but never review is a diary, not a tool. The weekly review is where the value lives. If you skip the review, you are doing the work without getting the benefit.

The fifth mistake is giving up after a bad week. You will miss days. You will forget expenses. You will have a week where the record is incomplete. That is normal, and it is not a reason to stop. Restart the next day. A system used eighty percent of the time still tells you far more than no system at all.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I need a paid app to track business expenses?**

No. A notes app, a free spreadsheet like Google Sheets, or a free expense tracking app are all enough for a small business. Pay for an app only when your business grows to the point where you need features like team access, invoicing, or detailed reports — and even then, test the free version first to see if it fits how you work.

**What counts as a business expense?**

Any money spent to run or grow the business: stock, transport for business trips, data and airtime used for business, rent for your shop or office, salaries, marketing, equipment and repairs, and bank charges on the business account. Personal spending — food for yourself, family expenses, personal transport — is not a business expense, even if you paid for it from business money. Record that separately as money you took from the business.

**How do I track expenses I pay for in cash?**

Exactly the same way as any other expense: record it in your tracker the moment you pay, with the amount and category. Cash is the easiest money to lose track of, so it is the most important to record immediately. Take a photo of the receipt when there is one, and keep business cash in a separate wallet so you always know which money you are spending.

**How much time does this take each day?**

A few minutes at most. Recording each expense takes about fifteen seconds. The end-of-day check takes about a minute. The weekly review takes about thirty minutes. That is less time than most business owners spend scrolling through their phones in a single sitting, and it buys you something no amount of scrolling will: knowing where your money goes.

**When should I hire an accountant or bookkeeper?**

When your records are too many to manage in thirty minutes a week, when you need proper financial statements for a loan or a partnership, or when tax filing becomes complex enough that mistakes could cost you. Until then, a simple phone tracker and a weekly review will carry a small business well. And when you do hire help, your existing records will make their job easier and cheaper, because the data is already there.
