# How to Announce a Price Increase to Your Customers Without Losing Them

Every Nigerian business owner knows this moment. The cost of your materials has gone up again. Fuel is more expensive. The dollar rate moved, and your supplier's new price list is higher than the last one. Your rent went up. Your staff need a raise. You look at your prices and realise you are working harder for less money, or even at a loss. You need to raise your prices. But you are afraid.

You are afraid customers will leave. You are afraid they will complain on WhatsApp, post angry comments, or quietly start buying from the competitor down the road. You are afraid of the awkward conversation. So you delay. Weeks pass. Months pass. Your margins shrink until the business can barely breathe. This fear is understandable, but delaying a needed price increase is more dangerous than announcing one. A business that cannot cover its costs will eventually close, and then every customer loses the business anyway.

The truth is that customers accept price increases all the time — from the fuel station, from the supermarket, from DSTv, from their landlord. What they do not accept is a price increase that feels sudden, unexplained, or unfair. This guide shows you how to raise your prices in a way that keeps your customers with you: how to decide how much, when to announce it, what to say, and how to handle the complaints that will still come from a few.

## First, Be Sure the Increase Is Necessary

Before you announce anything, check your numbers. A price increase should be driven by real costs, not by a guess or by copying a competitor. Sit down with your records and work out your true cost for your main product or service. Include the cost of materials, transport, packaging, delivery, data and airtime, staff time, and a share of your rent and other fixed costs. Then compare that cost with your current price. If your margin has fallen below what the business needs to survive, an increase is justified.

A simple way to think about it: if your costs have risen by 20 percent since you last set your prices, and your prices have not moved, you are now earning 20 percent less on every sale, or you may be selling at a loss without knowing it. Many Nigerian small businesses discover this too late, after months of quietly absorbing increases from suppliers and transporters. Your books will tell you the truth if you keep them.

Also consider whether there are other ways to protect your margin first. Can you negotiate a better price with a supplier by buying in bulk? Can you reduce waste or cut a cost that adds nothing for the customer? Do these things where you can, but do not pretend they will fix a real cost increase. If the price of your main material has doubled, no amount of small savings will save you. You need a price change.

## How Much Should You Raise?

This is the question every owner struggles with. Raise too little and you are back in the same position in three months. Raise too much and you shock your customers. The right amount is usually the amount that restores your margin to where it was before costs rose — no more, no less.

Work it out like this. If your product used to cost you ₦6,000 to provide and you sold it for ₦10,000, your margin was ₦4,000. If your cost is now ₦7,500, you need to sell at ₦11,500 to keep the same ₦4,000 margin. That is a 15 percent increase. If you only raise to ₦11,000, your margin drops to ₦3,500 and you are still sliding backwards. If you raise to ₦13,000, you are taking extra profit, and customers may feel it.

In practice, a price increase of 10 to 20 percent is usually accepted by customers when it is explained well, especially when they can see that costs have risen everywhere. An increase above 25 percent in one jump is harder to accept and may be better split into two steps, a few months apart. For example, instead of jumping from ₦10,000 to ₦13,000 at once, go to ₦11,500 now and ₦13,000 in three months, with clear notice each time.

Round your new prices to clean numbers. Nigerians pay more easily with round figures. ₦11,500 feels better than ₦11,350. ₦25,000 feels better than ₦24,800. Round numbers also make cash handling easier and reduce the need for small change, which is always scarce.

Also think about your price points relative to your market. If your competitors charge ₦15,000 for a similar service and you are at ₦10,000, you have room to move. If you are already the most expensive in your area, you need to be more careful, and your communication needs to be stronger, because customers will compare. Being the most expensive is fine if your quality and service justify it, and your announcement should remind customers of that value.

## When to Announce: Give Notice, Never Surprise

The single biggest mistake is surprising customers with a new price. A customer who arrives to buy something at ₦10,000 and is told at the counter that it is now ₦12,000 feels cheated, even if the increase is fair. They had no time to prepare, no explanation, and no choice. Many will walk away, not because of the price, but because of the surprise.

Give at least two weeks' notice, and a month is better for regular customers and subscription-style services. Announce the new price, the date it takes effect, and the reason, all at the same time. This gives customers time to adjust their budgets, to buy ahead if they want to, and to ask questions while they are still calm.

The timing of the increase matters too. Avoid raising prices in the same week as a major festival or holiday when customers are already spending heavily, unless your costs force your hand. Avoid raising prices immediately after a service failure or a complaint, because it will look like you do not care. The best time is a normal, quiet period, when you can communicate properly and handle questions without pressure.

If you have regular customers on a retainer or a subscription, tell them first, personally, before any public announcement. A phone call or a personal WhatsApp message to your top customers shows respect. They are your most valuable relationships, and they should never learn about a price change from a general broadcast or a poster in the shop.

## What to Say: Honest, Short, and Focused on Value

Your announcement should be short, honest, and focused on the customer, not on your problems. Customers do not need a detailed lecture on your costs. They need to know three things: what is changing, when it changes, and why it is necessary. They also need to feel that you still value them and that the quality they pay for will not drop.

Here is a structure that works. First, thank them. Thank them for their loyalty and for choosing your business. Second, state the change plainly. Say what the new price is and when it starts. Do not hide it in the middle of a long message. Third, give one honest reason. Rising costs of materials, fuel, and transport is a reason every Nigerian understands, because they face the same increases in their own lives. Fourth, reassure them about value. Tell them what stays the same: the same quality, the same service, the same care. Fifth, invite questions. Give them a way to reach you, and mean it.

Here is an example for a WhatsApp broadcast or a shop notice from a skincare business in Lagos:

"Dear valued customer, thank you for trusting us with your skincare. From Monday, 8 December, our glow set will move from ₦15,000 to ₦17,500. The cost of our ingredients and delivery has risen sharply this year, and we have held our price for as long as we could. Our formula, quality and service remain exactly the same. If you have any questions, please chat with us. Thank you for staying with us."

Notice what this message does. It is short. It names the old price and the new price. It gives a date. It gives one reason. It reassures on quality. It invites conversation. It does not apologise excessively, it does not blame anyone, and it does not beg. That tone — confident, respectful, honest — is what keeps customers.

For a service business like a salon, a cleaning company, or a digital agency, the same structure works. A salon in Abuja might write: "Our wash and style service moves from ₦8,000 to ₦9,500 from 1 December, due to the rising cost of quality products and electricity. Our stylists, products and care remain the same. Book ahead at the old price until 30 November." That last line is powerful: it rewards loyal customers with a chance to buy at the old price before the change, and it creates goodwill instead of resentment.

## Use Every Channel You Have

Do not rely on one announcement. Customers miss messages. Use every channel you already use to reach them. Put a notice in the shop or office where customers can see it. Send a WhatsApp broadcast to your customer list. Post it on your Instagram and Facebook pages. If you have a website, update the prices there on the same day the new price takes effect, not before, not after. If you send email, include it in your next message.

Make sure the message is the same everywhere. The price, the date, and the reason should not change from one channel to another. Inconsistency creates confusion, and confusion creates distrust. If you use WhatsApp Business, you can use the catalogue feature to update prices so customers see the new price when they browse.

For businesses that sell on Jumia or Konga, update your listings on the same day. A customer who sees one price on your Instagram and a different price on Jumia will assume one of them is wrong, and they will not buy from either until they are sure.

If you take orders by phone, brief your staff. Everyone who answers the phone or replies to chats must know the new price, the date it starts, and what to say if a customer complains. Nothing damages trust faster than one staff member quoting the old price and another quoting the new one.

## Soften the Increase Without Giving Away Your Margin

You do not have to simply announce a higher price and hope for the best. There are honest ways to make the change easier for customers while still protecting your margin.

One option is to offer the old price for a short window before the new price starts. "Book or buy before 30 November at the old price" rewards loyal customers and brings in a rush of orders. It also turns the announcement into good news for customers who act quickly.

Another option is to introduce the new price with a small bonus. A fashion business might add a free delivery on the first order at the new price. A salon might add a free deep-conditioning treatment for the first month. The bonus costs you far less than the margin you gain, and it gives customers a reason to try the new price without feeling punished.

A third option is to offer a smaller or simpler version at the old price. If your full package goes from ₦25,000 to ₦30,000, consider offering a basic version at ₦25,000. This keeps price-sensitive customers with you, and many of them will upgrade later when they are ready. A restaurant can do this with portion sizes. A service business can do it with a basic and a premium package.

A fourth option is a loyalty reward for your best customers. If someone has bought from you ten times, a small discount on their next order after the increase, or a free add-on, costs you little and says a lot. It tells them you noticed their loyalty and you are not taking it for granted.

What you should not do is apologise so much that you sound unsure, or offer a discount so large that it cancels the increase. A price increase announced with confidence and a small gesture of goodwill works. A price increase announced with panic and a big discount tells customers the increase was not justified.

## Handling Complaints and Lost Customers

Some customers will complain. A few will leave. That is normal, and it does not mean you did it wrong. What matters is how you respond.

When a customer complains, listen first. Do not argue. Do not get defensive. Acknowledge their concern: "I understand, and I appreciate you telling me." Then explain briefly, once, in the same honest terms as your announcement. Do not repeat the explanation five times. If they are a regular customer, you can offer the old price for one more order as a goodwill gesture. This is not weakness. It is smart business, because keeping a regular customer for one order at the old price is worth more than losing them forever.

Watch for patterns in the complaints. If many customers say the same thing — for example, that the increase came too soon after the last one — listen to that feedback. It might tell you that one item should stay at the old price, or that the increase should have been split into two steps.

## What Not to Do

Do not raise prices secretly by reducing quality or quantity. Shrinking the portion, watering down the product, or using cheaper materials without telling customers is the fastest way to destroy trust. Customers notice. They may not say anything, but they stop coming, and they tell their friends why. If you must reduce a portion or change an ingredient, say so openly and adjust the price fairly.

Do not blame your customers or the economy in an angry way. A message that says "everything is expensive now, so we have no choice" sounds like a complaint. A message that says "costs have risen, so we are adjusting our prices to keep serving you at the same quality" sounds like a business that is in control.

Do not raise prices every month. Frequent small increases annoy customers more than one clear increase. If your costs are rising steadily, plan one or two increases a year, announce them properly, and hold the new price steady in between. Customers can accept a price that moves once a year. They cannot accept a price that moves every few weeks.

Do not copy a competitor's price without understanding your own costs. Your competitor might have cheaper suppliers, lower rent, or a different quality level. Your price must cover your costs and your margin, not theirs.

## After the Increase: Check the Results

Two weeks after the new price takes effect, check your numbers. Compare your sales volume, revenue and profit with the same period before the increase. If volume fell slightly but profit rose, the increase worked. If volume collapsed, you may have raised too much or communicated poorly — talk to your customers to find out why. Keep the new price steady for at least three months, and use the extra margin well: pay staff better, improve your product, or build a small reserve for the next time costs rise.

## Raising Prices Is Part of Running a Business

A price increase is not a failure. It is not greed. It is a normal part of running a business in a country where costs move constantly. The businesses that survive in Nigeria are not the ones that never raise prices. They are the ones that raise prices honestly, communicate clearly, and keep delivering value that makes the price feel fair.

Plan the increase, check your numbers, give proper notice, write a short honest message, use every channel, soften it with a small gesture, and handle complaints with grace. Most of your customers will stay. The ones who stay are the ones who matter. And your business will be stronger for it.

Your prices should work for your business, not against it. If your costs have risen and your prices have not, this is the week to act. Write your announcement today, send it tomorrow, and give your customers the notice and respect they deserve.
