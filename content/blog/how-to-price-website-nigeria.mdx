Ask five people in Nigeria what a website costs and you will get five very different answers. One person quotes a small amount for a few pages, another quotes many times more, and both insist they are building you "a website". They are both probably telling the truth, because they are not quoting the same thing.

A website is not a single product with a fixed price. It is a project made of many parts, and the price depends on how many parts you need, how custom they are, and who is doing the work. This guide explains what actually goes into a quote, so that whether you are buying a website or selling one, you can talk about price with confidence and compare quotes fairly.

## The website itself: pages, design, and build

The first part of any quote is the website itself, and the biggest cost driver is scope. A five-page brochure site for a salon is a different job from a twenty-page site with a booking system, a blog, and an online store. Every extra page needs design, writing, images, testing, and future maintenance.

Design is the next factor. A template-based design, adapted to your brand colours and logo, is faster and cheaper than a fully custom design built from scratch for your business alone. Neither is automatically better. A template can be a smart choice for a small business that needs to launch quickly, while a custom design makes sense when your brand or your customer journey is unusual. What matters is that you know which one you are paying for.

Development covers the technical work: making the site load well on phones, making forms actually deliver messages, connecting WhatsApp or maps, and making sure pages work in different browsers. A site that looks good but has broken forms or slow loading is not finished work, no matter how attractive the design is. A fair quote includes time for testing, not just for building.

## Content: the part everyone underestimates

Many clients expect the developer to write all their website text, find all their photos, and somehow know their prices and services. In reality, content is a separate job, and it is often the reason projects stall. A quote may assume the client provides text and photos, or it may include writing and image sourcing. Both are legitimate, but they are different prices.

If you are buying a website, ask directly: does this quote include writing my pages, or do I need to supply the text? Do I need to provide photos, or will you source and edit them? If you are selling websites, state this clearly in your proposal. A client who discovers late that they must write ten pages of content will delay the project, and that delay often gets blamed on the developer.

Good content work includes structuring each page around what the customer needs to know, writing headlines in plain language, and organising services so visitors can find them quickly. It is not glamorous work, but it is what turns a pretty website into one that brings enquiries. ## Features that change the price

Features are where two quotes for "a website" diverge most. A simple site with pages and a contact form is one job. Add any of the following and the work, testing, and support all increase:

- An online store with products, carts, and payment
- A booking or appointment system with availability and reminders
- A customer login area or membership section
- A blog or news section the business can update itself
- Integration with WhatsApp, email marketing tools, or social platforms
- Multiple languages, or a large number of pages

None of these features is a trick to raise the price. Each one needs design, building, testing, and a plan for what happens when something goes wrong. When you compare quotes, compare the feature lists side by side. A cheaper quote that leaves out the booking system you actually need is not cheaper once you pay someone else to add it later.

## Running costs after launch

A website also has running costs, and a good quote separates these from the build cost so there are no surprises. The common ones are:

- Domain name: the yearly cost of your web address
- Hosting: where your website lives on the internet
- Email: professional email addresses on your own domain, if you use them
- Maintenance: updates, backups, security checks, and small fixes
- Renewals: yearly renewals for the domain and hosting

Some developers include the first year of hosting and domain in the build price, and some charge them separately. Some offer a monthly care plan that covers updates and backups, and some hand the site over with no ongoing support. Ask which model applies, and what happens if you do not renew a care plan. You should still own your domain and your content either way, and that should be stated clearly.

## Why quotes differ so much

With all of that in mind, the wide range of prices in Nigeria starts to make sense. A student building a first website with a free template, a freelancer with a portfolio of finished sites, and an agency with a team of designers and developers are offering different levels of experience, reliability, and support. The cheapest option is not always a bad choice, and the most expensive option is not always the best one. The right question is not "who is cheapest?" but "who is offering the scope, quality, and support I actually need?"

Red flags are worth watching for in either direction. A quote with no scope, no timeline, and no terms is risky at any price, because you cannot tell what you will receive. A quote that promises everything, instantly, with no questions about your business, is also risky. A serious professional will ask questions before quoting, because the answers change the work.

## How to compare two quotes fairly

When you have two or more quotes, lay them out and check the same items in each:

- Number of pages and sections included
- Whether design is custom or template-based
- Whether content writing and photos are included
- Features included, such as booking, store, or blog
- Timeline, and what is needed from you at each stage
- Payment stages, and what is due before launch
- What happens after launch: support, updates, and ownership

If one quote is missing an item the others include, ask for a revised quote that includes it. Only then can you compare like with like. And if you are the one quoting, make this comparison easy for your client by presenting your scope in the same clear structure. Clients choose the professional who makes their decision easy.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is a cheap website ever a good idea?**

It can be, if the scope matches your need and the limitations are clear. A simple, well-built site that you can update yourself is far better than an expensive site that never launches. The danger is a cheap quote that quietly leaves out content, testing, or support you assumed were included.

**Should I pay everything upfront?**

It is normal to pay in stages, often a deposit to begin and the balance before launch, with the exact split agreed in writing. Paying everything upfront gives you little protection if work stalls, while paying nothing upfront gives the developer little protection either. A staged payment is fair to both sides.

**Does a higher price mean a better website?**

Not automatically. A higher price should buy you more scope, more custom work, more experience, or more support, and the quote should show which one. If a high quote does not explain itself, ask for that explanation before you decide.

**What should I budget for each year after launch?**

At minimum, budget for domain and hosting renewals. Beyond that, consider a maintenance arrangement that covers updates, backups, and small fixes. The exact amount depends on your site's size and features, so ask your developer to list the yearly items separately from the build price.

## Judge the quote, not just the number

A website quote is a plan written in numbers. When you understand what goes into it — scope, design, content, features, and running costs — you can judge whether a price is fair, whether a cheaper option is really cheaper, and whether an expensive option is worth it. That understanding protects both sides: clients get the website they actually need, and professionals get paid properly for the work they actually do.
