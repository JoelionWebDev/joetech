Every business website reaches a point where it feels tired. The design looks old, the information is out of date, and customers are not enquiring the way they used to. The natural question is: should we redesign it, or rebuild it from scratch?

These two options sound similar, but they are very different jobs, with different costs, timelines, and risks. Choosing the wrong one wastes money: a redesign on a broken foundation will not fix the real problem, and a full rebuild when only the design was tired is money spent for no reason.

## What a redesign actually means

A redesign keeps your website's foundation and changes how it looks and how visitors move through it. Think of it like renovating a house: the structure, wiring, and plumbing stay, but the paint, furniture, and layout change.

In a redesign, your developer typically updates the visual design, improves the homepage and service pages, rewrites or reorganises content, fixes the navigation, and makes the site work better on phones. Your existing pages, web address, and much of the underlying setup stay in place. The content management system, hosting, and core features usually do not change. A redesign is the right scale of work when the site basically works but looks outdated, or when the structure no longer matches how customers actually use it. It is faster and less disruptive than a rebuild, and it preserves the search visibility and links your site has already built up.

## What a rebuild actually means

A rebuild starts again from the foundation. Think of it like demolishing a house and building a new one on the same land: the address stays the same, but everything underneath is new.

In a rebuild, your developer typically creates a new site on a modern platform or codebase, rebuilds the pages, features, and integrations, restructures the content, and migrates what is worth keeping from the old site. The old site is replaced, not patched. A rebuild is a larger project, with more planning, more testing, and a careful launch to protect your search rankings and existing links. It is the right scale of work when the foundation itself is the problem, and no amount of fresh paint will fix it.

## Signs you probably need a redesign

You likely need a redesign, not a rebuild, if most of these are true:

- The site loads reasonably well and rarely breaks, but the design looks old
- Your services, prices, or contact details have changed, but the structure still makes sense
- Visitors can use the site, but struggle to find key information or take the next step
- The site works on a computer but looks poor on a phone
- Your branding has changed, and the site still shows the old colours, logo, or tone
- The content is sound but badly organised, with long pages and unclear headings

In these cases, the bones are fine. What you need is better design, clearer content, and a smoother journey from landing on the site to contacting you. If some of these signs sound familiar, check them against the warning signs in our guide to [the signs your website needs a redesign](/blog/5-signs-your-website-needs-redesign) to confirm the diagnosis before you commit.

## Signs you probably need a rebuild

You likely need a rebuild if several of these are true:

- The site is very slow, breaks often, or cannot be updated safely
- Your developer tells you the platform or code is no longer supported, or updates risk breaking the site
- You cannot add basic features customers expect, such as online booking, payments, or a working contact form, without major workarounds
- Security problems keep appearing, or the site has been compromised before
- The site is not mobile-friendly at its core, and making it so would mean changing almost every page anyway
- Your business has changed fundamentally, for example from a single service to multiple branches or an online store, and the old structure cannot support it

When several of these apply together, a redesign becomes false economy. You would pay to redecorate a site that still breaks, still cannot take payments, and still cannot be updated safely. A rebuild, properly planned, solves the root problem once.

## The questions to ask before you decide

Before you choose, answer these questions honestly, ideally with your developer:

1. **Can the site be updated safely?** If routine updates, such as changing a price or adding a page, are risky or impossible, the foundation is suspect.
2. **Does the site do its core job?** If customers can find you, understand your offer, and contact you, the core works. If they cannot, work out whether design or foundation is blocking them.
3. **What do visitors actually do?** Look at which pages people visit, where they leave, and how many enquire. Good traffic but poor enquiries often points to a design and content problem. Almost no traffic, or a site that crashes, often points deeper.
4. **What must the new site do that the old one cannot?** List the features you truly need. If the list is short and the current platform supports them, a redesign may be enough. If the list is long and the platform fights you, lean towards a rebuild.
5. **What will you lose if you rebuild badly?** A rebuild done without care can lose search rankings, break old links, and confuse returning customers. That risk is manageable with proper redirects and testing, but it must be planned for, not discovered after launch.

## What each option typically involves, and mistakes to avoid

A redesign usually involves a design review, new page layouts, rewritten or reorganised content, improved navigation, and mobile improvements, followed by testing and launch on the existing platform. Because the foundation stays, the project is usually shorter, and your team can often keep updating content during the work.

A rebuild usually involves a discovery phase, where your goals, customers, and required features are mapped out, followed by new design, new development, content migration, redirects from old pages to new ones, testing on phones and computers, and a carefully staged launch. It takes longer and costs more, but it leaves you with a modern, maintainable site your team can build on for years.

In both cases, ask your developer for a clear scope: exactly which pages will change, what will happen to your existing web addresses, how search rankings will be protected, and what you will be able to update yourself afterwards. Watch out for common mistakes: redesigning only the homepage and leaving the rest untouched, rebuilding without redirecting old addresses, copying a competitor's design instead of designing around your own customers, and launching without testing on a real phone, on mobile data, with the contact form actually working.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Will a rebuild hurt my Google rankings?**

It can, if done carelessly, but a well-planned rebuild protects them. Old page addresses should redirect to the right new pages, important content should be carried over, and the new site should be tested before launch. Ask your developer how they will handle redirects and migration before you agree to a rebuild.

**Can I redesign in stages instead of all at once?**

Yes, and for many businesses this is sensible. Start with the homepage and the pages that get the most visits, then work through service pages and the contact journey. Staged work spreads the cost and lets you measure the effect of each change, provided the overall design stays consistent.

**How do I know if my platform is too old?**

Ask your developer whether the platform still receives security updates, whether it can support the features you need, and whether routine changes are safe. If the answer to any of these is no, or if every small change requires unusual workarounds, the platform is a rebuild candidate.

**Is a redesign cheaper than a rebuild?**

Usually, yes, because less work is involved. But a cheap redesign that leaves a broken foundation untouched can end up costing more in the long run, through repeated fixes and lost enquiries. Compare the options on total value over the next few years, not only on the first quote.

## Choose the fix that matches the problem

An outdated website is a symptom, not a diagnosis. Sometimes the cure is a fresh design and clearer content. Sometimes it is a new foundation that can support your business for years. Taking a little time to work out which one you need will save you money and frustration.
