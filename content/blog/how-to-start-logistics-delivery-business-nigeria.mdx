Every day in Nigeria, thousands of packages need to move: food from restaurants, orders from Instagram and WhatsApp vendors, parcels from online stores, documents between offices, groceries, medicines and spare parts. E-commerce keeps growing, small vendors keep selling online, and every one of those sales needs a rider or driver to complete it. That is the opportunity behind a logistics and delivery business — you are the final link between seller and buyer, and sellers will pay for a courier they can trust.

But this is a business of thin margins and hard days: fuel, bike maintenance, traffic, rain, wrong addresses and impatient customers are all part of the job. The delivery companies that survive are not the ones with the most bikes; they are the ones that deliver on time, communicate honestly, handle packages carefully and keep costs under control. This guide walks you through the full setup — niche, legal and insurance, your first bike, pricing, customers, daily operations and growth — so you can start with one rider and build a service people recommend.

## Step 1: Understand the Market and Choose Your Niche

Delivery is a big market, but you cannot serve all of it at once. The first decision is your niche — the type of deliveries you will focus on — because each niche has different customers, prices and demands.

Food delivery serves restaurants, fast-food outlets, bukas and home caterers: urgent orders, short distances, hot food fast. E-commerce and vendor delivery serves online sellers on Instagram, WhatsApp, Jumia, Konga and their own websites — varied parcels, volumes rising around paydays and festive seasons, with sellers wanting proof of delivery and cash-on-delivery handling. Document and office delivery serves law firms, banks, agencies and corporates who need files moved quickly between offices. Grocery, pharmacy and errand delivery serves supermarkets, pharmacies and busy households, often with regular repeat customers.

Pick one niche to start with, ideally one where you already know a few potential customers: restaurants nearby means food delivery, Instagram vendors in your area means vendor delivery, office contacts means steady weekday document work. Your first ten regular customers matter more than a hundred one-off deliveries, because regular customers give you predictable daily income and reduce idle time.

Study your area before you spend anything: which estates, markets and business districts are near you, how long trips take at peak hours, and what other riders charge. Ask vendors and restaurant owners what frustrates them — late arrivals, damaged goods, riders who disappear with cash, no updates. Every frustration is a gap you can fill with reliability and communication.

Be honest about the competition. Big logistics companies and dispatch platforms operate in Lagos, Abuja and Port Harcourt, with apps, fleets and advertising — you will not outspend them. Your advantage is local and personal: you know the streets, you answer the phone, you can serve one estate or market very well, and you can build relationships with sellers tired of being just another number on a big platform.

## Step 2: Decide Your Model and Your Starting Capital

There are three common ways to run a small delivery business. Owner-rider: you ride yourself — the cheapest start, you keep all the earnings and learn the streets, customers and problems first-hand, but income stops when you stop riding and one person can only do so many deliveries a day. Hire-a-rider: you own the bike and employ or contract a rider — this lets you scale, but needs careful management, trust and a clear agreement on pay, fuel and maintenance. Small fleet: several bikes and riders under your brand, with you managing bookings, customers and money. That is the goal for most owners, but not the starting point — it is what you grow into after the first model works.

Your starting capital depends on the model. As an owner-rider with a fairly used motorcycle in good condition, you can start with roughly ₦400,000 to ₦900,000, covering the bike, registration and licence, a delivery box, helmet and rain gear, a phone, initial fuel and a small float for running costs. Hiring a rider adds pay and running costs, so a one-bike, one-rider setup commonly needs roughly ₦700,000 to ₦1,500,000 to start properly, including the bike, documents, insurance and working capital for the first month. A small car or van for bulk and inter-state deliveries costs far more, so most beginners start with a bike and add a vehicle later when corporate and bulk jobs justify it. These are realistic ranges, not promises — prices and fees vary by state and by the bike's condition.

Spend in the right order: a reliable bike first, then documents and insurance, then your delivery box and rider gear, then a phone and a float for fuel and airtime. A cheap bike that breaks down every week is the most expensive bike you can buy — every breakdown is a lost delivery, an angry customer and a repair bill. Buying used? Take a trusted mechanic, check engine, brakes, tyres and lights, and ask for the bike's papers before you pay. Keep a simple record from day one: spending, earnings per delivery, fuel and repairs. If you do not know your cost per trip, you cannot price properly.

## Step 3: Register the Business, Get Licensed and Insure

Registration builds trust and protects you. Register a business name with the Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC) — simple, easy to pronounce and remember, and check availability first. A registered name lets you open a business bank account, issue proper receipts, and supply companies that only deal with registered businesses.

You also need the right documents: a valid rider's licence, plus bike registration, a number plate and the usual vehicle papers for your state. In Lagos, commercial motorcycles used as dispatch bikes fall under state rules that change from time to time, including restrictions in certain areas — check the current rules before you start riding there, since fines for restricted areas can wipe out a week's profit. Other states have their own rules, so confirm what applies where you operate.

Insurance is not a luxury. At a minimum, get third-party motor insurance, which is legally required, and consider comprehensive or goods-in-transit cover if you handle valuable parcels. A single accident without insurance can end a small delivery business overnight. Also agree with customers, in plain language, who is responsible if a parcel is damaged or lost and what your liability limit is, and put it in writing on your receipt or booking message. Clear terms prevent most disputes.

If you hire a rider, have a written agreement covering pay, hours, fuel, maintenance, damage to the bike, and how customer cash is handled and remitted. Keep copies of the rider's licence, identification and a guarantor's details — many small delivery businesses lose money not on the road, but through unclear agreements and unaccounted cash.

## Step 4: Get Your Bike, Gear and Basic Systems Ready

Your bike is your factory, so choose and maintain it well. For city deliveries you want a durable, fuel-efficient motorcycle with a strong engine and good suspension — popular choices among Nigerian dispatch riders are common Japanese and Chinese commuter models with cheap spare parts and mechanics who know them. Whatever you choose, parts must be easy to find in your city: a bike waiting days for a rare part earns nothing.

Fit a proper delivery box: a sturdy, lockable box mounted behind the rider protects parcels from rain, dust and theft, and tells customers you are a serious business. Buy a good helmet, a reflective jacket or branded vest, rain gear, and a phone holder so the rider can follow maps safely. Brand the box and vest with your business name and phone number — every trip is free advertising.

Set up your booking system before your first delivery. At the start, a dedicated WhatsApp Business number, a notebook and a clear process are enough: the customer sends pickup and delivery addresses, the recipient's name and phone number, and a description of the item. You confirm the price, the rider picks up, you update the customer at collection and again at delivery, then you collect payment or confirm the transfer. That loop — confirm, pick up, update, deliver, confirm — is the whole service, and doing it reliably every time is what builds your reputation.

You will also need a simple way to track money: a separate account or wallet for the business, every delivery recorded with its price and cost, and cash collected by the rider reconciled daily. A rider who returns with all cash accounted for is a rider you can trust with bigger jobs. Finally, save the numbers of a good mechanic, a fuel station that sells clean fuel, and a spare rider to call when your main rider is sick or the bike is in the workshop. In delivery, a backup plan is how you keep your promise when something goes wrong.

## Step 5: Price Your Deliveries So Every Trip Makes Money

Pricing is where many small delivery businesses quietly fail: they charge what the customer wants to pay, not what the trip costs, and discover months later they have been working hard for nothing. Your price must cover fuel, rider pay, bike maintenance, airtime and data, and leave a profit — or the business will eat itself.

Price by distance and urgency: a base charge for short trips within a neighbourhood, a higher charge for cross-town trips, and a premium for express or same-hour delivery. As a guide, short intra-area deliveries in Lagos or Abuja commonly go for roughly ₦1,500 to ₦3,000, cross-town trips for ₦3,000 to ₦7,000, and longer or inter-state trips priced per kilometre or by agreement. Your exact prices depend on your city, fuel cost and distances — check what other riders charge, then set your own prices from your costs, not from theirs.

Work out your cost per trip: fuel, a share of maintenance and bike replacement, rider pay and airtime. If a trip costs you ₦1,200 and you charge ₦1,500, one delay or wrong address wipes out your margin; at ₦2,500 you have room to absorb problems and still earn. Offer regular customers a slightly better rate for volume — a vendor giving you ten deliveries a day is worth a small discount, because their work keeps your rider busy. But never discount below cost, and review prices when fuel prices rise. Customers accept a small, explained increase far better than a courier who quietly starts cutting corners.

Agree the price before the rider moves: confirm pickup point, destination, item and price in writing on WhatsApp, and state who pays — sender or receiver. Most disputes come from a price that was never agreed or a payer who was never confirmed. A short written confirmation prevents almost all of them.

## Step 6: Find Customers — Vendors, Restaurants and Offices

Your first customers are the sellers around you. Visit Instagram and WhatsApp vendors in your area and introduce your service: coverage area, prices, hours, and how you handle cash on delivery. Offer a first delivery free or at a discount to a few promising vendors, then deliver it perfectly — on time, with updates, with proof. One good first delivery is worth more than a hundred flyers.

Restaurants and food outlets need riders at lunch and dinner peaks: visit them with a fixed rate per delivery, a rider available at busy hours, and a promise to handle food carefully. Pharmacies, supermarkets and offices need regular, reliable runs — visit them too and leave a price list and your WhatsApp number. Corporate clients pay slower but order steadily, so pursue them once you can issue receipts.

Join local business groups on WhatsApp and Facebook where vendors gather, and be genuinely helpful there — answer questions, share your coverage, and let your reliability speak. Ask every happy customer to recommend you to another seller: a recommendation from one vendor to another is the cheapest and most effective marketing you will ever get, because sellers trust other sellers.

## Step 7: Run Daily Operations Like a Professional

Good operations separate a real delivery company from a man with a bike. Start each day by checking the bike — fuel, tyres, brakes, lights, oil — and confirming the day's bookings. Plan routes so the rider covers nearby deliveries together instead of criss-crossing the city, because every wasted kilometre is wasted fuel and time.

Communication is your most valuable service: tell the sender when the rider is on the way, tell the receiver when the parcel is close, and confirm delivery with a message and, where possible, a photo or signature. When there is a delay — traffic, rain, a wrong address — say so immediately and give a new time. Customers forgive delays; they do not forgive silence. Keep a simple log of every delivery — date, sender, receiver, addresses, price, payment status, problems — as your record and proof when a customer asks what happened to their parcel.

Handle cash carefully: with cash on delivery, the rider must collect the exact amount, record it, and remit it the same day, with the vendor confirming receipt. Use transfers where possible and reconcile every naira at the end of the day — many delivery businesses lose money not on the road but in unrecorded cash. Handle parcels with care too: fragile items packed properly, food upright and covered, documents in a waterproof bag. A damaged parcel costs you the fee, the refund, and the customer.

## Step 8: Hire, Manage and Keep Good Riders

When you are ready to hire, hire slowly and check carefully. A good rider knows the area, rides safely, communicates well, is honest with cash, and treats customers politely. Ask for a valid licence, check references, and do a trial week before any long-term agreement — that week tells you more than any interview about how the rider handles the bike, the parcels and the customers.

Pay fairly and on time: riders who are paid late or poorly will leave, or worse, quietly cut corners. Agree the pay structure clearly — a fixed daily or monthly pay, a commission per delivery, or a mix of both — plus who pays for fuel and what happens with maintenance. Many owners use a mix, a modest base pay plus commission per completed delivery, because it rewards hard work while keeping costs predictable.

Train your rider in the basics: greeting customers, confirming an address before moving, calling the receiver before arriving, handling cash, and what to do when an address is wrong or a customer is unavailable. Give the rider a branded vest and a delivery box, and service the bike on schedule — a rider on a well-maintained bike is faster, safer and cheaper to run. When a rider does well, say so, and consider small bonuses for a month with no complaints and no missing cash. A loyal, trusted rider is the most valuable asset in this business.

## Step 9: Build Trust, Keep Records and Grow

Trust is your brand: every delivery that arrives on time, intact, with clear updates builds it, and every failed delivery damages it. Ask customers for short testimonials and share them on your WhatsApp status and social media.

Keep proper records from day one: daily deliveries, income, fuel, maintenance, rider pay and payments owed. Review them weekly — which routes earn best, which customers order most, where money leaks. Set aside a maintenance fund from every week's earnings for servicing, tyres and repairs, so a breakdown never catches you without money.

Grow in steps: when one bike is busy most days, add a second bike and rider; when vendors in a neighbouring area start asking for you, extend your coverage; when corporate jobs appear, consider a small car or van for bulk deliveries. You can also add services customers already need — scheduled daily pickups, a monthly retainer for an office, or inter-state runs with a trusted partner — each step funded by the profits of the last one.

The mistakes to avoid are familiar: pricing below cost, hiring a rider without checking references, ignoring bike maintenance until it breaks, taking cash without records, promising times you cannot meet, and expanding to a second bike before the first is profitable. Avoid those, keep your records honest, and your delivery business can grow from one bike in one neighbourhood into a name vendors across your city recommend by word of mouth.
