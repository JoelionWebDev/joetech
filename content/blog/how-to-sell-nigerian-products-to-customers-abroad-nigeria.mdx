## Why Selling Abroad Is Worth Your Attention

Every week, Nigerians living in London, Toronto, Houston and Dublin are searching for the same things: proper crayfish, dried stockfish, shea butter that has not been mixed with anything, Ankara fabric in colours you cannot find on a high street, and handmade leather sandals that actually last. They want these products, they are willing to pay more for them than a buyer in Lagos would, and they often buy in bulk because they are stocking up or reselling to their own communities.

That is the opportunity. A bag of crayfish that sells for ₦45,000 in Mile 12 market can sell for the equivalent of ₦90,000 or more to a customer in the UK once you factor in the value of authenticity and convenience. The margin is real. But so are the risks: shipping costs that eat your profit, payments that get stuck, customers who never receive their order, and customs rules you did not know existed.

This guide walks you through the whole process, step by step, in the order a real seller would follow it. No theory, no jargon — just the practical decisions you need to make and the numbers you need to check before you spend a kobo.

## Step 1: Choose Products That Travel Well

Not every product is a good export. The best products for selling abroad share four qualities: they are light for their value, they do not spoil quickly, they are hard to find where your customer lives, and they survive a week or more in transit.

### Products that usually work

- **Dried and packaged foodstuffs:** crayfish, stockfish, dried pepper, egusi, ogbono, dried fish, garri, yam flour, groundnut, dried bitter leaf. Dried goods travel far better than fresh ones and rarely cause customs problems.
- **Beauty and skincare:** raw shea butter, black soap, coconut oil, hair creams and natural oils. These are high value for their weight and have repeat buyers.
- **Fashion and textiles:** Ankara fabric, aso-oke, handmade sandals, beaded bags, agbada and ready-to-wear pieces. Fashion sells on uniqueness — your buyer cannot walk into a shop in Manchester and find the same print.
- **Crafts and home items:** woven baskets, wooden carvings, beaded jewellery, hand-painted items, throw pillows in African prints.
- **Spices and herbs:** dried scent leaf, uziza, uda, Cameroon pepper, suya spice blends.

### Products to avoid at the start

Fresh food, meat, and anything that needs refrigeration are difficult and expensive to ship, and many countries restrict them. Liquids in large quantities are heavy and can leak. Anything with a short shelf life is a gamble when delivery takes seven to fourteen days. Start with the safe list, build your shipping confidence, and only then experiment.

### A quick test before you commit

Before you buy stock for export, answer these questions honestly:

1. Can I buy this product in Lagos, Onitsha, Aba or Kano at a price that leaves me at least 40 percent margin after shipping?
2. Will it still be in good condition after ten days in a box?
3. Can I package it so it looks like a proper product, not something wrapped in a black nylon bag?
4. Is there a real community abroad that already buys this type of product?

If the answer to any of these is no, pick a different product.

## Step 2: Know Your Customer Abroad

The biggest mistake new exporters make is trying to sell to "everybody abroad." You will do far better selling to one specific group first.

### The Nigerian diaspora is your first market

There are large Nigerian communities in the UK, the United States, Canada, Ireland, Germany and South Africa. These customers already know your product, they already want it, and they already understand why it costs what it costs. They buy for themselves, for family, and for small resale in their neighbourhoods.

Start by finding where they gather online: Nigerian community groups on Facebook and WhatsApp, diaspora forums, and Instagram pages that serve Nigerians abroad. Watch what people ask for. When the same product keeps coming up — "where can I get proper stockfish in Toronto?" — that is your market signal.

### Pick one country first

Choose one country to start with — the UK is the easiest for most Nigerian sellers because of the large diaspora, shared language, and relatively straightforward customs rules for dried food and crafts. Learn that one market deeply: what the customers want, what they pay, how long shipping takes, and what the rules are. Once you are selling steadily to one country, add a second.

## Step 3: Set Up a Way to Sell

You need a place where a customer abroad can see your products, trust you, and pay you. You do not need all of these at once — pick one and do it well.

### Option A: Instagram and WhatsApp

This is how most Nigerian exporters start, and it works. Post clear photos and short videos of your products on Instagram, with prices in both naira and the customer's currency. Take orders on WhatsApp, where you can answer questions in real time and build a personal relationship.

The advantage is speed and zero setup cost. The disadvantage is that you have to handle every order manually, and new customers may not trust a seller they only know from Instagram. Fix that by posting consistently, showing your face and your workspace, sharing customer reviews, and always delivering on time.

### Option B: An online marketplace

Marketplaces like Etsy and eBay already have millions of buyers searching every day, and they handle part of the trust problem for you because buyers feel protected. Etsy is particularly good for handmade goods, fashion, crafts and beauty products. The fees are real — listing fees, transaction fees and payment processing — so build them into your price from the start.

The catch is that setting up on some of these platforms from Nigeria requires extra steps, and payouts can take time. Research the current requirements before you invest hours in a shop that may not accept your setup.

### Option C: Your own simple website

A small website with your products, prices and a WhatsApp order button gives you the most control and the most professional look. You do not need a complicated store — a clean page with good photos, clear prices, shipping information and a way to contact you is enough to start. The website becomes your home base, and Instagram and WhatsApp become the roads that lead customers to it.

### What every selling channel needs

Whichever route you choose, every product listing should answer these five questions:

1. What exactly is this product, and how much do I get?
2. What does it cost, in naira and in the buyer's currency?
3. How long will delivery take?
4. How much is shipping to my country?
5. How do I pay, and what happens if something goes wrong?

If a listing cannot answer those five questions, a foreign customer will move on to a seller who can.

## Step 4: Price for Profit, Not Just for Sales

Pricing for export is different from pricing for a customer in Lagos. You are not just selling a product — you are selling the product, plus packaging, plus shipping, plus your time, plus the risk that something goes wrong.

### The export pricing formula

Use this formula for every product:

**Selling price = Product cost + Packaging cost + Shipping cost + Platform or payment fees + Your profit margin**

Let us work a real example. Suppose you want to sell 1kg of dried crayfish to a customer in London:

- Product cost in Mile 12 market: ₦18,000
- Packaging (sealed bag, box, label): ₦1,500
- Shipping to the UK (small parcel): ₦22,000
- Payment processing fees: roughly ₦3,500
- Total cost: ₦45,000

If you sell at ₦65,000, your profit is ₦20,000 per kilo. That is a healthy margin, and the customer still gets a fair price compared to what the same product costs in a London African shop. If you had priced it at ₦50,000 because that "felt" right, you would have made only ₦5,000 — and one lost parcel would wipe out the profit from four good orders.

### Price in the buyer's currency

Always show the price in the currency your customer uses — pounds, dollars, or Canadian dollars — alongside the naira price. A customer in London thinks in pounds. If she sees "£35" she can decide in seconds. If she sees "₦65,000" she has to do maths, and many will not bother.

Use the current exchange rate when you set your price, and review your prices every month. The naira moves, and a price that was profitable in January may not be profitable in June. Many sellers update their foreign prices at the start of each month and tell their regular customers.

## Step 5: Get Paid Without Losing Money

Getting paid is where many new exporters lose money or get stuck. Your customer is abroad, your bank is in Nigeria, and the money has to cross that gap safely.

### Payment options that work for Nigerian sellers

- **International money transfer services:** Services that let a customer abroad send money directly to your Nigerian bank account or for cash pickup are widely used. Your customer pays in their currency, you receive naira. Check the fees and the exchange rate before you recommend one, because the rate offered is often worse than the market rate.
- **Dollar accounts:** A domiciliary account with your Nigerian bank lets you receive dollars or pounds directly. This is worth setting up once your orders become regular, because it protects you from poor conversion rates. Ask your bank what documents they need — usually a valid ID, proof of address, and sometimes evidence of your business.
- **Payment links and invoicing tools:** Some international payment platforms allow Nigerian businesses to send payment links to customers abroad. Availability and rules change, so check the current position before you promise a customer this option.
- **Marketplace payouts:** If you sell through a marketplace, the platform collects the payment and pays you out on its own schedule. This is simpler, but you wait longer for your money and you pay their fees.

### Rules that protect you

1. **Never ship before payment clears.** This is the golden rule of exporting. No matter how friendly the customer sounds, no matter how urgent the order, do not put a parcel in the post until the money is in your account and confirmed. Genuine customers understand this.
2. **Take a deposit for large orders.** For orders above ₦100,000, ask for full payment upfront. If the customer is a regular, a 70 percent deposit with the balance before shipping is a fair compromise.
3. **Keep records of every payment.** Screenshot confirmations, save receipts, and record the date, amount, customer name and order details in a simple notebook or spreadsheet. When a payment is disputed, your records are your defence.
4. **Watch out for scams.** Be careful of customers who overpay "by mistake" and ask you to refund the difference, buyers who want you to ship to a different address than the payment address, and anyone who pressures you to ship before payment. These are classic fraud patterns. Slow down, verify, and walk away if something feels wrong.

## Step 6: Package Like a Professional

Your packaging is the first thing your customer touches, and it decides whether they order again. A product that arrives crushed, leaking or smelling of the market will not get a repeat order, no matter how good the product itself is.

### Packaging basics

- **Seal food properly.** Use heat-sealed bags or airtight containers for dried food. Double-bag anything with a strong smell, like stockfish or crayfish, so the smell does not escape into the whole parcel.
- **Protect fragile items.** Wrap crafts, bottles and ceramics in bubble wrap and pack them tightly so nothing moves inside the box. Fill empty space with crumpled paper.
- **Use a proper box.** A sturdy carton box, not a soft bag, for anything that can break. Tape all edges. Write the address clearly and include your own return address.
- **Add a label.** A simple printed label with your business name, the product name, the weight, and your WhatsApp number makes the product look like a brand instead of a market purchase. It costs very little and it changes how customers see you.
- **Include a small thank-you note.** A short handwritten note — "Thank you for your order, Ada. Enjoy your crayfish!" — costs nothing and turns a transaction into a relationship. Repeat customers are built on touches like this.

### Weigh and measure before you quote shipping

Shipping is priced by weight and size, so always weigh your packed parcel before you tell a customer the shipping cost. A kitchen scale that costs ₦8,000 to ₦15,000 will save you from undercharging on shipping, which is one of the fastest ways to lose money on exports. Measure the box too — some couriers charge by the size of the box if it is large and light.

## Step 7: Ship It the Right Way

Shipping is the part that frightens most new exporters, but it becomes routine once you have done it a few times. The key is to compare your options before every shipment, not after.

### Shipping options from Nigeria

- **International courier companies:** DHL, FedEx and UPS operate in Nigeria and deliver to most countries in three to seven working days. They are the most reliable and the most expensive. Use them for high-value orders, urgent orders, and your first few shipments while you learn the process.
- **The national postal service:** NIPOST offers international parcel services at lower prices, but delivery takes longer — sometimes two to four weeks — and tracking is less detailed. It can work well for low-value, non-urgent orders once you understand the process.
- **Freight and cargo agents:** For larger shipments — say, 20kg or more of dried food to one customer or a small shop abroad — a cargo agent can be much cheaper per kilo than a courier. Ask around in markets like Mile 12, Balogun and Onitsha; experienced traders know the reliable agents.
- **Travellers and informal channels:** Some sellers send goods with trusted travellers flying to the destination. This can be cheap and fast, but it carries risks and you have little protection if something goes wrong. Use it only with people you trust completely.

### How to choose

For each order, compare at least two options on three things: total cost, delivery time, and tracking. A courier that costs ₦25,000 and arrives in five days with full tracking is often better value than a ₦12,000 option that takes three weeks with no tracking, because your customer is happier, you get fewer complaints, and you get paid faster for the next order.

## Step 8: Handle Problems Like a Professional

Things will go wrong. A parcel will be delayed. A product will arrive damaged. A customer will say the crayfish does not smell right. What separates sellers who last from sellers who quit is how they handle these moments.

### The common problems and what to do

- **Delayed delivery:** Tell the customer before they ask. "Your parcel left Lagos on Monday and is currently in London. It should arrive by Thursday." A customer who is informed will wait. A customer who is ignored will demand a refund.
- **Damaged product:** Apologise, ask for a photo, and offer a replacement or a partial refund. One replacement costs you far less than a bad review that turns away ten future customers.
- **Wrong item sent:** Own it immediately, send the right item, and let the customer keep the wrong one if returning it costs more than it is worth.
## Checklist: Before You Ship Your First International Order

- [ ] Product chosen and tested for travel
- [ ] Price calculated with product, packaging, shipping, fees and profit
- [ ] Price shown in naira and the buyer's currency
- [ ] Payment method confirmed and tested
- [ ] Full payment received and cleared
- [ ] Product sealed, labelled and packed in a sturdy box
- [ ] Parcel weighed and measured
- [ ] Customs declaration filled in honestly
- [ ] Tracking number sent to the customer
- [ ] Customer's name, address and order saved in your records

Selling Nigerian products to customers abroad is not a get-rich-quick scheme. It is a real business that rewards patience, honesty and attention to detail. Start small, deliver on every promise, and let your customers abroad become your best salespeople. The demand is already there. Your job is to meet it well enough that they come back.
