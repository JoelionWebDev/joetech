Nigerians love to look good. From church on Sunday to weddings on Saturday, from the office on Monday to owambe parties, people buy clothes for every occasion. Ready-to-wear fashion, often called RTW, means clothes made in standard sizes and sold ready to wear, without the customer waiting for a tailor. That simple idea has built many small fashion businesses across Nigeria, from Instagram shops in Lagos to market stalls in Onitsha and boutiques in Abuja.

You do not need to be a tailor to start an RTW business. Many successful sellers do not sew at all. They design, source fabrics, work with tailors, and focus on selling. Others sew their own pieces and sell them directly. Either route can work if you understand your customers, control your costs, and sell consistently. This guide explains how to start, step by step, with realistic naira figures and practical advice for the Nigerian market.

## Why Ready-to-Wear Works in Nigeria

The demand for clothes never stops. People outgrow clothes, wear them out, and buy new ones for events, work, and everyday life. In Nigeria, fashion is also social. A new style seen at a wedding or on Instagram can become the style everyone wants the following week. RTW sellers who move fast can meet that demand.

RTW also solves a real problem. Many Nigerians are tired of tailors who disappoint them, deliver late, or sew a style that looks nothing like the picture. When a customer can see the exact dress, shirt, or two-piece set, try it or check the size, and take it home the same day, that is a strong reason to buy. You are selling convenience as much as fashion.

The business is flexible. You can start online with a small collection, sell from home, or open a small shop later. You can focus on women, men, children, or a mix. You can sell casual wear, corporate wear, native wear, or party outfits. Starting narrow helps you learn what sells before you spend money on a wide range.

## Step 1: Choose Your Niche and Know Your Customer

Do not try to sell everything to everybody at the start. Pick a clear niche. A niche is the type of customer and the type of clothes you will focus on. Examples include affordable dresses for young working women, native wear for men, children's party outfits, corporate shirts and trousers, modest fashion, plus-size fashion, or Ankara ready-to-wear sets.

Think about who will buy from you. A student in Yaba has a different budget from a banker in Lekki. A mother buying for her children has different needs from a young man buying for a wedding. Write down your ideal customer: their age, where they live, what they do, how much they can spend on one outfit, and where they already shop.

Visit markets and check Instagram shops that sell what you want to sell. Note their prices, their styles, and how customers respond in the comments. What do people ask for most? What sizes sell out first? What complaints do you see? This research costs nothing and saves you from producing clothes nobody wants.

Choose styles that suit your customer and your skill level. Simple, well-finished designs sell better than complicated styles that are hard to sew well. A clean A-line dress, a well-cut shirt, or a smart two-piece set can sell again and again if the fit and finishing are good. Test a small batch first. Make or order ten pieces in your best styles, sell them, and learn from the response before you order fifty.

## Step 2: Plan Your Money and Startup Costs

Write a simple plan before you spend money. List what you will sell, who will buy it, how much each piece will cost to make, what price you will sell it for, and how many pieces you need to sell each month to make a profit. This plan does not need to be long. One page is enough if it is honest.

Startup costs depend on whether you sew yourself or work with tailors. If you work with tailors, your main costs are fabrics, sewing charges, and selling costs. A small starter collection of 20 to 30 pieces can cost between ₦300,000 and ₦700,000, depending on the fabric and the style. That includes fabric, sewing, labels, packaging, and a small budget for photos and online adverts.

If you sew yourself, add the cost of a sewing machine, an overlock machine, pressing iron, scissors, and other tools. A good industrial sewing machine can cost from ₦250,000 upwards, while a reliable fairly used machine costs less. You will also need a workspace with a table, good light, and storage for fabrics.

Keep money aside for transport, market runs, and phone data. Many new sellers forget these small costs, yet they add up quickly. Keep a separate wallet or bank account for the business. Do not mix business money with personal spending. At the end of each week, record what you spent and what you earned. That habit will tell you quickly whether a style is making money or losing money.

Start small and grow from profit. It is better to sell out of 20 pieces and reorder than to be stuck with 100 pieces nobody wants. Fashion trends change fast, and unsold stock ties up your cash.

## Step 3: Source Fabrics and Find Good Tailors

Your fabric and your tailor decide the quality of your clothes. Buy fabrics from trusted markets. In Lagos, Balogun Market, Yaba Market, and Tejuosho Market are popular for Ankara, lace, chiffon, and other fabrics. In Onitsha, the main market has a wide range at competitive prices. In Kano, Kantin Kwari Market is known for fabrics. In Abuja, Wuse Market and Garki Market are useful. Compare prices in at least three shops before you buy in bulk.

Learn the names and prices of common fabrics: Ankara, Adire, lace, chiffon, crepe, cotton, linen, velvet, and senator material for men. Ask the seller how the fabric behaves after washing. Some fabrics shrink or fade. A fabric that looks beautiful in the market but fades after one wash will bring complaints and refunds.

If you do not sew, find two reliable tailors, not just one. A single tailor can fall sick, travel, or become too busy, and your business will stop. Test a tailor with a small order first. Give clear instructions, a sample picture, and the exact measurements. Check the finishing: straight seams, neat hems, strong zips, and clean lining where needed. Pay fairly and on time. A tailor who is paid well and treated with respect will prioritise your work.

Agree on sewing charges per style before work starts. Write down the price for a dress, a shirt, a skirt, a trouser, and a two-piece set. Agree on delivery dates and what happens if a piece is sewn badly. A simple written agreement, even on paper, prevents arguments later.

Consider working with a small production unit if your orders grow. Some fashion houses in Lagos and Aba take bulk sewing jobs. Aba, in Abia State, is famous for garment production and can be a good source for larger orders, but always order a sample first and check the quality before you pay for a full batch.

## Step 4: Get Your Sizes, Fit, and Quality Right

Sizes are the biggest source of returns in RTW. Nigerian customers buy by size, but sizes differ from seller to seller. Use a clear size chart and measure every piece before you list it. Your chart should show bust, waist, hips, length, and shoulder measurements in inches or centimetres, and the Nigerian and UK size it matches.

Make your clothes in the sizes your customers actually buy. For women, sizes 10 to 16 often sell fastest, but do not ignore smaller and larger sizes. For men, stock a range of shirt and trouser sizes. If you can, offer a few pieces in plus sizes, because many customers struggle to find stylish clothes in their size.

Check every piece before you sell it. Look for loose threads, weak zips, uneven hems, stains, and missing buttons. Iron each piece before you photograph it or hand it to a customer. A well-pressed outfit looks more expensive and sells faster. Pack each piece neatly in a bag with your label. Good packaging costs little and makes your brand look serious.

Ask for feedback after a sale. Did the dress fit well? Was the fabric comfortable? Would the customer buy again? Use the answers to improve your next batch. If many customers say a style is too tight at the waist, adjust the pattern. If a fabric itches, stop using it. Small improvements build a loyal customer base.

## Step 5: Price Your Clothes to Make a Profit

Pricing is not guesswork. Add up the full cost of one piece: fabric, sewing, lining, zip, buttons, label, packaging, transport, and a share of your photo and advert costs. That is your cost price. Then add your profit. Most RTW sellers aim for a profit margin that covers their time and leaves room for discounts and slow days.

Study the prices of similar sellers. If a similar Ankara dress sells for ₦25,000 in your area, you need a good reason to charge ₦40,000, such as better fabric, better finishing, or a stronger brand. You can also charge less to attract first customers, but do not price so low that you make no profit. Cheap prices bring customers once, but no profit means no business.

Be clear about your prices. List the price on WhatsApp, Instagram, and in your shop. Avoid the habit of saying "DM for price" for every item. Many customers will not message you if they cannot see the price. When you do run a sale, set a clear start and end date, and do not sell at a loss just to look busy.

Offer payment options that suit Nigerians. Bank transfer, OPay, PalmPay, and Paystack payment links all help customers pay quickly. If you sell online, collect payment before delivery, or use pay on delivery only with trusted customers and a clear policy. Keep records of every payment, including the customer's name, the item, the amount, and the delivery address.

## Step 6: Sell Online and Offline

Most RTW businesses in Nigeria sell mainly online, especially on Instagram and WhatsApp. Create an Instagram page with a clear name, a good profile picture, and your WhatsApp link in the bio. Post clear photos of each outfit on a real person or a mannequin, not just flat on a bed. Show the front, the back, and the fabric close up. Add the price, the available sizes, and how to order.

Use WhatsApp Business well. Set up your catalogue with photos, prices, and sizes. Use broadcast lists and status updates to announce new arrivals, but do not spam your contacts. Reply quickly. In fashion, a customer who waits three hours for a reply may buy from another seller. Save each customer's size and preferences so you can tell them when a style they like arrives.

Sell offline too. A small shop, a market stall, or a stand at a trade fair lets customers try clothes and buy on the spot. Pop-up sales at offices, churches, and events can work well. If you have a shop, keep it tidy, play soft music, and make it easy for customers to browse. Let customers try clothes where possible, and keep a mirror and a changing area.

Delivery matters. Partner with a reliable dispatch rider in Lagos or Abuja, or use a trusted logistics company for interstate delivery. Pack clothes neatly so they arrive clean and pressed. Tell the customer the delivery fee before they pay, and give them a tracking update. A late or dirty delivery can turn a happy customer into a complaint.

## Step 7: Build Your Brand with Good Photos

In fashion, your photos are your shop window. You do not need an expensive camera to start. A good phone camera, natural daylight, and a clean background are enough. Photograph in the morning or late afternoon when the light is soft. Avoid harsh midday sun and dark rooms.

Use real models where you can. A friend, a family member, or a paid model who fits your target size will show how the clothes look on a real body. Show the outfit styled with shoes and a bag, so the customer can imagine wearing it. Take several shots and pick the best ones. Edit lightly for brightness, but do not change the colour of the fabric. A customer who receives a dress in a different colour from the photo will be disappointed.

Keep your brand consistent. Use the same style of photos, the same colours, and the same logo or label on every piece. Choose a brand name that is easy to remember and easy to spell. Register your business name with the Corporate Affairs Commission when you have steady sales, and open a business bank account so customers can pay into an account that carries your brand name.

Collect reviews and repost them with permission. A photo of a happy customer wearing your outfit is stronger than any advert. Ask customers to send a photo and a short comment. Offer a small discount on their next order as a thank you, but never force a review.

## Step 8: Manage Stock, Records, and Growth

Fashion stock moves fast, so you must track it. Keep a simple record of each style: how many pieces you made, in which sizes, how many sold, and how many are left. Use a notebook or a spreadsheet on your phone. When a size sells out, note it. That tells you what to reorder and what to stop making.

Track your best sellers every month. Which styles sold fastest? Which colours sold out? Which sizes were left over? Use those answers to plan your next batch. Do not reorder a style that sold slowly just because you like it. Follow what your customers buy.

Plan for seasons and events. Demand rises before Christmas, Easter, weddings season, and school resumption. Order and produce ahead of those periods, because tailors get busy and fabric prices can rise. Keep some cash aside for those peak periods so you are not caught without stock when demand is highest.

Avoid common mistakes. Do not copy another brand's designs exactly and claim them as yours. Do not take full payment for a custom order and then fail to deliver. Do not spend your profit on personal items before you reorder stock. Do not ignore customer complaints. A complaint handled well can keep a customer for years, while a complaint ignored can cost you many customers.

As you grow, you can add new product lines: matching sets, children's wear, or accessories such as scarves and bags. You can hire a shop assistant, work with more tailors, or open a small shop in a busy area. You can also sell on marketplaces and through other shops on a wholesale basis. Sell a batch to a boutique owner at a wholesale price and let them sell at retail. That can move stock faster, even if the profit per piece is lower.

## Final Checklist Before You Launch

Before you launch, check that you have a clear niche and a named customer, a small tested collection in the right sizes, reliable fabric suppliers and tailors, a price list that covers your costs and leaves a profit, clear photos with prices and sizes, a WhatsApp Business catalogue, a delivery plan, and a simple record book for sales and stock.

Launch with a small collection, not a huge one. Announce your launch on WhatsApp and Instagram, invite your first customers, and ask for honest feedback. Sell out, learn, reorder, and improve. Ready-to-wear fashion rewards sellers who listen to customers, keep their quality steady, and show up every day. Start with what you have, focus on fit and finishing, and let your work speak for your brand.
