A business owner in Lagos paid for a website two years ago. It looks good. It loads well. Her friends compliment it. But when asked how many customers it has brought her, she has no answer. She does not know if the website is making money, losing money, or simply sitting there.

Measuring your website is not complicated, and you do not need to be technical. You need to track a few simple numbers, compare them with what the website costs you, and make small improvements based on what you learn. This guide shows you how.

## Start with what the website costs you

You cannot know if a website is making money until you know what it costs. Write down every cost, once a year and once a month, in one simple list:

- **Building the website.** This is usually a one-time cost. Write down what you paid, and divide it by the number of years you expect the site to last. A site that cost a lump sum and lasts three years costs one third of that sum per year.
- **Domain name and hosting.** These are yearly costs. Your domain name renews every year, and your hosting is usually paid monthly or yearly.
- **Maintenance and updates.** If you pay someone to update the site, fix problems, or add new pages, include that cost.
- **Content and adverts.** If you pay for articles, photos, or adverts that send people to the site, include those costs too.

Add the numbers up for one year. That total is your website's yearly cost, and without it every other measurement is guesswork. For a simple way to keep track, read our guide on the [five numbers to track every month](/blog/marketing-analytics-101-five-numbers-monthly).

## Track where your enquiries come from

The most important question is simple: how many customers found you through the website? Most businesses never ask, so they never know. Fix that with one habit: ask every new customer how they found you, and write the answer down.

A simple tally works. Keep a notebook or a phone note with four columns: Google search, social media, WhatsApp, and referral or other. Every time a new customer contacts you, ask "How did you find us?" and add a mark in the right column. After a month, you will see the pattern clearly.

You can also make tracking easier on the website itself. Use a different WhatsApp number or a different contact form for the website than for your social media pages, so you can tell website enquiries apart. Or add a simple question to your contact form: "How did you hear about us?" with a few options to choose from. The answers go straight into your inbox with every enquiry.

## Count what each enquiry is worth

Knowing you received twenty enquiries from the website is useful. Knowing what those enquiries turned into is what matters. For each website enquiry, track three things:

1. **Did it become a sale?** Count how many website enquiries turned into paying customers.
2. **How much did each sale bring in?** Add up the money from website customers over a month.
3. **How many enquiries did you need for one sale?** If ten enquiries produce two customers, your website converts one in five visitors who contact you. That is your conversion rate, and it is the number you want to improve over time.

Now compare. If your website costs a certain amount per month and brings in more than that in sales, it is making money. If it brings in less, it is losing money, and you need to find out why: too few visitors, visitors who never contact you, or enquiries that never become sales.

## Use simple analytics, not complicated dashboards

Free analytics tools can tell you how many people visit your website, which pages they look at, and how they found you. You do not need to study every report. Check four simple numbers once a week:

- **Visitors.** How many people visited your site this week? Is the number going up, down, or staying flat?
- **Most visited pages.** Which pages do people actually look at? If your services page gets almost no visits, customers cannot find it, or nothing sends them there.
- **Where visitors come from.** Are they arriving from Google, from Instagram, from WhatsApp, or from typing your address directly? This tells you which of your efforts is working.
- **How long they stay.** If visitors leave after a few seconds, your page may be loading slowly, or the headline may not match what they expected.

## Five signs your website is earning its keep

A healthy website usually shows most of these signs:

- **Regular enquiries.** At least a few new enquiries every week that you can trace back to the website.
- **Google visibility.** Your business appears when people search for what you offer in your area.
- **Visitors who stay.** People spend time on your pages instead of leaving in seconds.
- **Sales you can trace.** You can name customers who found you through the website and what they bought.
- **A cost that makes sense.** The money the website brings in each month is more than the money it costs to keep it running.

## Small improvements that raise the return

Once you are measuring, small changes often produce the clearest results:

- **Add a WhatsApp button to every page.** Many Nigerian visitors prefer WhatsApp to a contact form. Make it easy for them.
- **Show your prices or a price range.** Websites that hide all prices lose visitors who assume the worst. Even a starting price helps.
- **Add customer questions to your pages.** If visitors keep asking the same thing in chats, answer it on the page itself.
- **Fix slow pages.** A page that takes too long to load loses visitors before they read a word. Test your site on a phone with mobile data.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How long should I wait before judging my website?**

Give a new website at least three months of regular promotion before judging it. Check the numbers weekly, but judge the trend over a quarter, not over a single week. A website with no promotion and no measurement for a year is not a failed website. It is an unused one.

**What if my website brings enquiries but no sales?**

Then the problem is usually not the website. It may be slow replies, unclear prices, or a sales process that loses people after they contact you. Track where enquiries drop off: do they go quiet after the first reply, after the price, or after the payment details? Fix that step first.

**Do I need paid analytics tools?**

No. Free analytics tools are enough for most small businesses. What matters is checking a few simple numbers regularly and acting on them, not owning an expensive dashboard nobody opens.

**What is a good conversion rate for a small business website?**

There is no single number that fits every business, because a free consultation and a high-value service convert very differently. What matters is your own trend: if one in ten enquiries becomes a customer this quarter and one in seven does next quarter, your website is improving. Compare yourself with your own past, not with a stranger's claim.

## A website is an investment, so treat it like one

You would never run a shop for a year without checking whether it makes money. A website deserves the same attention. It costs money to build and keep, and it should return more than it costs.

Write down the costs. Ask every customer how they found you. Track which enquiries become sales. Check four simple numbers every week. Then improve one thing at a time and watch the trend. That is the whole system, and it takes less than an hour a week.

A website that is measured gets better. A website that is ignored quietly wastes money. Choose measurement, and your website will start telling you exactly what it needs to earn its keep.
