Pricing is the hardest part of running a catering business in Nigeria, and it is also the part that decides whether you make money or just stay busy. Many small caterers cook excellent food, serve hundreds of guests, and still end the month with empty pockets — because the price they charged never covered the real cost of the job.

This guide shows you how to price your catering services properly: how to cost a plate of food down to the last naira, how to build per-head rates for different types of events, how to write a quote that protects you, and how to raise your prices when the market moves. Everything here is built for the Nigerian reality — rising food prices, fuel and gas costs, transport across Lagos traffic, and clients who will always ask for a discount.

## Why Most Small Caterers Underprice

The most common pricing mistake in Nigerian catering is guessing. A client asks, "How much for 200 guests?" and the caterer names a figure that sounds reasonable — ₦500,000, maybe ₦800,000 — without working out what the food, the staff, and the transport will actually cost. If the real cost turns out to be ₦750,000, a ₦800,000 quote leaves almost nothing after the stress of the event.

The second mistake is copying a competitor's price. A big catering company in Lekki can charge ₦4,000 per head because it buys rice by the trailer load and owns its own chafing dishes, vans, and staff. A small caterer in Ikorodu buying from Mile 12 market in small quantities has different costs. Copying the big company's price without its cost structure is a fast way to lose money.

The third mistake is forgetting the hidden costs. The price of the rice is obvious. The cost of the gas to cook it, the transport to the venue, the wages of the servers, and the breakage of plates are easy to forget — but they are real, and they come out of your profit whether you count them or not.

## Step 1: Cost One Plate of Food

Every catering price starts with the plate. Before you can quote for 100 guests, you must know what one guest's food costs you. This is called your food cost per head, and it is the foundation of everything else.

Pick a standard menu and cost it honestly. Take a common Lagos party menu: jollof rice, fried rice, chicken, salad, and a drink. Work out the quantity of each item for one person. A typical serving is about 250 g of cooked rice per person, one piece of chicken, and a small portion of salad.

Now price the ingredients. If a 50 kg bag of rice costs ₦75,000 and yields about 200 servings of cooked rice, the rice per plate costs about ₦375. If a carton of frozen chicken costs ₦45,000 and gives 60 pieces, each piece costs ₦750. Add cooking oil, tomatoes, pepper, seasoning, and vegetables — say ₦250 per plate. Add the drink at ₦300. Your food cost per plate is roughly ₦1,675.

Those figures will change with the market, and that is exactly why you must redo this costing regularly. When the price of a bag of rice moves, your per-plate cost moves with it. Keep a costing sheet on your phone or in a notebook, with the date, the item, the quantity, and the price. Update it every time you buy in bulk.

## Step 2: Add All the Costs Around the Food

Food is usually only 40 to 50 percent of what a catering job costs you. The rest is everything around it, and this is where small caterers lose money.

**Labour.** How many people do you need? For 100 guests, you may need one cook, two servers, and one supervisor — yourself included. If you pay a server ₦8,000 to ₦15,000 per event in Lagos, three staff cost ₦24,000 to ₦45,000. Pay your staff fairly and on time; a server who feels cheated will not serve your clients well.

**Transport.** Fuel, the cost of hiring a van, and the rider who delivers the food. Moving food, chafing dishes, and serving equipment across Lagos can cost ₦30,000 to ₦80,000 depending on the distance and the size of the job. If the venue is in Epe and you are based in Surulere, the transport cost is a real line in your quote.

**Equipment.** Chafing dishes, serving spoons, plates, coolers, and a canopy if you provide one. If you own them, charge a small hire or wear-and-tear fee per event — say ₦10,000 to ₦20,000. If you rent them, add the actual rental cost. Breakage happens at almost every event; budget for one or two broken plates per job.

**Gas and cooking costs.** A 12.5 kg gas cylinder that costs ₦15,000 may cover one medium event. Add it to the costing sheet.

**Packaging and extras.** Takeaway packs for leftover food, serviettes, water for your staff, and the plastic containers for the client's food. These small items can add ₦10,000 to ₦20,000 to a job.

Add all of these to the food cost. If the food for 100 guests costs ₦167,500 and the other costs add up to ₦120,000, your total cost is ₦287,500 — or ₦2,875 per head. That is your break-even point. Charge less than that and you pay the client to eat your food.

## Step 3: Set Your Profit Margin

Once you know your total cost per head, add your profit. For catering in Nigeria, a healthy target is 30 to 40 percent profit on top of your total cost. This is not greed; it is what keeps the business alive when a client cancels, a pot of soup spills, or the price of chicken jumps the week of an event.

Using the example above: total cost ₦2,875 per head, plus 35 percent profit, gives a price of about ₦3,880 per head. Round it to ₦4,000 per head for a clean, professional figure. For 100 guests, the quote is ₦400,000.

If that price feels high compared with what your competitors charge, check your costing before you cut your price. Maybe your food cost is high because you buy in small quantities — in that case, the fix is to buy smarter, not to charge less. Or maybe the competitor is genuinely cheaper because they use lower-quality ingredients. You cannot win a race to the bottom against someone willing to serve bad food.

There are times to accept a thinner margin. A first job for a big company that could become a regular client may be worth pricing at 20 percent profit to win the relationship. A job in your slow season may be worth taking at a lower margin rather than earning nothing. But make these decisions deliberately, knowing exactly how much profit you are giving up — never by accident.

## Step 4: Build Clear Per-Head Packages

Clients compare caterers by the per-head price, so make yours easy to understand. Build three packages with clear names and clear contents, and quote from them.

**Standard package — ₦3,500 to ₦4,500 per head.** One rice dish, one protein, salad, and a drink. Suitable for office end-of-year parties, church programmes, and small family events.

**Premium package — ₦5,500 to ₦7,500 per head.** Two rice dishes or rice and swallow, two proteins, small chops, salad, and drinks. Suitable for weddings and birthdays where the host wants guests to feel well fed.

**Luxury package — ₦9,000 to ₦15,000 per head.** A full spread: multiple dishes, grilled fish or asun, small chops, desserts, and a drinks station. Suitable for high-end weddings and corporate events in Lekki, Victoria Island, and Abuja.

Adjust these ranges to your area. Prices in Abuja and Lekki run higher than in Ibadan or Enugu, because food, transport, and labour all cost more there. Your packages should also state what is included and what is not: number of servers, serving equipment, setup time, and whether drinks are included or supplied by the client.

Keep your packages on a single page you can send on WhatsApp as a PDF or image. When a client asks for a price, you send the packages, they choose, and the conversation moves to the date and the guest count instead of a long negotiation about what is included.

## Step 5: Quote for Events, Not Just Plates

A catering quote is more than a per-head price multiplied by the guest count. Write it as a proper document, even if it is a WhatsApp message or a one-page PDF. A clear quote prevents arguments later.

Every quote should include: the client's name and event date, the venue, the menu with quantities, the guest count, the per-head price, the total, the deposit required, the balance due date, and what happens if the guest count changes.

Guest count changes are the most common source of loss. A client books for 200 guests, pays for 200, and 280 people show up. Or the client books for 200 and only 120 attend, and then demands a refund for the 80 who did not come. Your quote should state your rule plainly: the price is based on the confirmed guest count given seven days before the event; extra guests are charged at the per-head rate; and food prepared for the confirmed count is not refundable if fewer people attend.

Always include a validity period — for example, "This quote is valid for 7 days." Food prices move fast in Nigeria. A quote written in November may be wrong by December if the price of rice jumps. A validity period protects you, and clients understand it when you explain it.

For large events, visit the venue before quoting. A venue with no kitchen, no water, or a long walk from the parking area to the hall will cost you more in labour and time. Seeing the venue lets you price the real job, not the job you imagined.

## Step 6: Deposits, Payment Terms, and Protecting Yourself

Never start cooking for an event without a deposit. The standard in Nigerian catering is 50 to 70 percent upfront, with the balance paid before or on the day of the event — ideally before you serve the first plate.

The deposit covers your market shopping. You will spend most of it on rice, chicken, oil, and gas before the event day. Without it, you are funding the client's party from your own pocket, and if the client cancels, you are left with perishable food and no money.

State your cancellation terms in the quote. A common and fair approach: if the client cancels more than 14 days before the event, you refund the deposit minus the cost of items already bought. If they cancel within 7 days, the deposit is non-refundable, because you have already bought and possibly started cooking. Put this in writing and get the client to confirm it on WhatsApp or by signing the quote.

Accept payment by bank transfer and confirm receipt immediately. Many caterers lose jobs because a client sent money and waited hours for confirmation. Check your account, reply with a receipt message, and keep a record of every payment against each event.

For corporate clients, expect slower payment. A company may pay 30 days after the event, and you must decide whether you can afford to fund the job for a month. If you take corporate jobs on credit, price them higher to cover the wait, and get a written purchase order or agreement before you cook.

## Step 7: Handle Discounts and Negotiations Without Losing Money

Every Nigerian client will ask for a discount. It is part of the culture, and it does not mean they disrespect your work. The skill is in giving a discount that costs you little and keeps the profit intact.

Never cut your price by reducing your profit to zero. Instead, trade something. If a client wants a lower price, offer a simpler menu: one protein instead of two, or water instead of soft drinks. The client gets a lower total, and your margin stays healthy.

Offer discounts that bring you more business. A 5 percent discount for a client who books three events at once, or a free small-chops platter for a client who refers two new customers, costs you less than a straight price cut and builds your pipeline.

When a client says "my last caterer charged less," ask what was included. Often the cheaper quote did not include drinks, servers, or equipment. Show your client your full quote, line by line, so they can compare properly.

## Step 8: Review and Raise Your Prices Regularly

Your prices are not permanent. Food prices in Nigeria move constantly, and a price that was profitable in January may lose money by June. Review your costing every month, and adjust your packages every quarter.

Track the prices of your five most important ingredients — rice, chicken, cooking oil, tomatoes, and gas — in a notebook or on your phone. When two or more of them rise by more than 10 percent, recalculate your per-plate cost and update your packages. Do not wait until you have lost money on three events before acting.

Raising prices feels uncomfortable, especially with regular clients. The honest way to do it is to tell them early and plainly: "Because the price of rice and chicken has gone up, our standard package moves from ₦4,000 to ₦4,500 per head from next month." Most clients accept a small, explained increase.

Keep an eye on what other caterers in your area charge, but do not let them set your price. Your costs, your quality, and your service are what determine your price. A caterer who charges more and delivers excellently will always have clients; a caterer who charges the least and cuts corners will always be replacing lost customers.

## Step 9: Keep Records That Show the Truth

A catering business that does not keep records is guessing about its own success. After every event, write down four numbers: what you charged, what you spent, what you earned, and how many guests were served. Over a few months, these numbers tell you which jobs make money and which ones only look profitable.

You will discover patterns. You may find that weddings make the best profit, while office lunches barely break even after transport. You may find that your premium package sells better than your standard one, because clients planning a wedding want quality. Or you may find that one particular menu item — say, small chops — costs more to make than you thought and eats your margin.

Keep a separate record of clients: their name, phone number, event type, and what they ordered. After the event, send a short thank-you message on WhatsApp and ask for a review or a photo you can share. A happy client who posts about your food is worth more than any advert you can pay for, and a list of past clients is the cheapest source of future bookings.

At the end of each month, add up your total income and total spending. If the profit is less than 20 percent of your income, something is wrong — either your prices are too low, your costs are too high, or you are taking jobs you should refuse. Fix it before the next month's bookings.

## A Simple Pricing Plan for Your Next 30 Days

Week one: cost your three main menus properly, plate by plate, including labour, transport, and gas. Build your three packages — standard, premium, and luxury — with clear per-head prices.

Week two: write a standard quote template with your terms: deposit, balance date, guest-count rule, cancellation policy, and validity period. Save it as a document you can send on WhatsApp in seconds.

Week three: apply the new prices to your next two quotes. Track the prices of your five key ingredients. Tell your regular clients about any price change early and honestly.

Week four: after your next event, record the four numbers — charged, spent, earned, guests served. Compare the real profit with what you expected. Adjust your costing sheet and your packages based on what you learned.

Good pricing is not about charging the highest price in the market. It is about knowing your costs, charging enough to make a real profit, and being able to explain your price with confidence. When you can show a client exactly what goes into their event — the food, the staff, the transport, the equipment — the price stops being a negotiation and becomes a fact. That is the caterer clients trust, recommend, and rebook.
