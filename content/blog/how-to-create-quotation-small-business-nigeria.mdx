A client asks you for a quotation. You know your work is good. You know your price is fair. But the document you send is a short WhatsApp message with a single figure and no details. The client goes quiet. Another business sends a neat, detailed quotation on letterhead, with a breakdown of costs, a timeline, and clear terms. That business wins the job, even if their price is higher.

That scenario plays out every day in Nigeria. A quotation is not just a price. It is a sales document, a trust signal, and a record of what you agreed to do. A careless quotation tells them the opposite, even when your work is excellent.

This guide shows you how to create a professional quotation for your small business in Nigeria, step by step. You will learn what to include, how to price your work, how to present it beautifully, and how to follow up so more quotations turn into paid jobs.

## What a Quotation Is and Why It Matters

A quotation, sometimes called a quote or an estimate, is a formal document that tells a client how much a job will cost, what is included, and the terms under which you will do the work. It is different from an invoice. A quotation comes before the job, to help the client decide. An invoice comes after the job, or after an agreed milestone, to request payment.

A good quotation does three things. First, it helps the client make a decision. When costs are broken down clearly, the client can see what they are paying for and compare your offer fairly with others. Second, it protects you. When the scope, price, and terms are written down, there is less room for misunderstanding later about what was included or how much extra work should cost. Third, it builds your reputation. A neat quotation with your business name, logo, and contact details makes a small business look established and reliable.

In Nigeria, quotations are used across many industries. A caterer quotes for a wedding. A fashion designer quotes for aso-ebi. A web designer quotes for a business website. A printer quotes for branded souvenirs. A cleaning company quotes for office cleaning. A builder quotes for a renovation. The format is similar in each case, even though the details change. Learning to write one well is a skill that pays you back on every job.

## Step 1: Understand the Client's Needs Before You Quote

Never rush to send a price before you understand the job. A quotation based on guesswork is likely to be wrong. You will either price too low and lose money, or price too high and lose the client.

Start by asking the client clear questions. What exactly do they want? How many units, how many guests, how many pages, how many rooms, or how many days? When do they need it? Where will the work happen? What is the quality or standard they expect? Are there any special requirements, such as branding, delivery to a specific location, or work on a weekend?

Write down the answers. If the client explains the job on a phone call, send a short WhatsApp message afterwards summarising what you understood and ask them to confirm. That message protects both of you. For example: "Thank you for the call. Just to confirm, you need catering for 150 guests in Lekki on Saturday 12 December, with small chops, jollof rice, and drinks. Please confirm so I can prepare your quotation." A client who confirms in writing is less likely to change the scope later without discussion.

If the job is large or complex, visit the site or ask for photos. A cleaning company quoting for an office should see the space. A decorator quoting for a hall should know its size. A web designer should know what pages and features the client wants. A site visit shows professionalism and helps you price accurately.

Also find out who will approve the quotation and how quickly they need it. A quotation for a company may need to go through a manager, a procurement officer, or a finance team. A quotation for a wedding may need to be approved by a couple and their family. Knowing the decision process helps you follow up at the right time and with the right person.

## Step 2: Calculate Your Price Carefully

Pricing a quotation is where many small businesses make costly mistakes. They copy a competitor's price, guess a figure that sounds reasonable, or set a price based only on materials and forget their time, transport, and profit.

Calculate your price in parts. Start with direct costs: the materials, stock, or items you must buy for this specific job. For a caterer, that is food ingredients, gas, and packaging. For a printer, that is paper, ink, and finishing. For a web designer, that may be domain registration, hosting, and any paid tools. Write each direct cost down with its amount.

Next, add labour. How many hours will the job take, and how many people will work on it? Even if you work alone, your time has value. If a job takes two days and you pay an assistant ₦5,000 per day, that ₦10,000 is part of the cost. If you do the work yourself, assign a fair daily rate for your time and include it. Many business owners forget to pay themselves and then wonder why they are always broke despite being busy.

Next, add other job costs: transport to buy materials, transport to the venue, delivery, airtime for coordination, and any hired equipment. A decorator who must transport chairs and canopies to a venue in Ajah from a shop in Ikeja must include that transport cost, not absorb it silently.

Next, add a share of your fixed business costs, such as rent, electricity, and internet. You do not need a complex formula. A simple approach is to add a small percentage, such as five to ten percent of the direct costs, to cover overheads. That keeps your pricing realistic without making the quotation confusing.

Finally, add your profit margin. Profit is not greed. It is what allows you to grow, replace equipment, handle unexpected costs, and pay yourself properly. A margin of fifteen to thirty percent is common for many small business jobs in Nigeria, depending on the industry and the competition. For a job with direct and overhead costs of ₦100,000, a twenty percent margin adds ₦20,000, making the total ₦120,000. If that price is far above what competitors charge, review your costs and see if you can source materials more cheaply, rather than removing your profit entirely.

Consider taxes where relevant. If you are VAT-registered or if the client expects VAT to be shown, state clearly whether your price includes VAT or not. For most small businesses that are not VAT-registered, a simple note that the price is the total payable is enough. Do not add VAT if you are not registered to charge it.

## Step 3: Structure Your Quotation Like a Professional

A professional quotation has a clear structure that any client can follow in under a minute. Use the same structure every time, so your quotations become faster to prepare and easier for clients to trust.

Start with your business header. Include your business name, logo, address, phone number, WhatsApp number, and email address. If you have a website, include it. The header should be at the top of the first page and look neat. If you do not have a printed letterhead yet, you can create a simple header in Word, Google Docs, or Canva with your business name in a bold font and your contact details below it.

Next, add the quotation title and reference number. Write "QUOTATION" clearly, with a reference number such as "QT-2026-011" and the date. A reference number helps you and the client track the document, especially when a company requests several quotations in a year. Add the client's name, company name if applicable, address, and phone number under a heading like "Prepared for."

Next, add a short introduction. One or two sentences are enough. For example: "Thank you for considering us for your event catering. Below is our quotation for catering services for 150 guests as discussed." That sentence shows the client you listened and personalises the document.

Next, add the itemised breakdown. This is the heart of the quotation. Use a table with columns for item number, description, quantity, unit price, and total price. List each item separately. Instead of writing "Catering — ₦450,000," break it down: "Jollof rice and chicken for 150 guests — 150 plates — ₦1,800 per plate — ₦270,000," "Small chops — 150 packs — ₦1,000 per pack — ₦150,000," "Drinks and water — 150 servings — ₦200 per serving — ₦30,000." A breakdown builds trust because the client can see what they are paying for. It also makes it easier to adjust the quotation if the client wants to remove or add an item.

After the items, show the subtotal, any discount if you offer one, and the grand total in bold. Write the total in words as well as figures for large jobs, for example "Total: ₦450,000 (Four hundred and fifty thousand naira only)." That practice is common in Nigerian business documents and reduces disputes about figures.

Next, add the validity period. A quotation should not be open forever. Prices of materials change, and your availability changes. Write a line such as "This quotation is valid for 14 days from the date above." That creates a gentle urgency and protects you if the client returns two months later expecting the same price.

Next, add payment terms. State how much is required upfront, when the balance is due, and how payment can be made. A common structure in Nigeria is fifty percent upfront to confirm the booking and fifty percent before delivery or on the day of the event. For smaller jobs, you might ask for full payment upfront or payment on delivery. State your bank details clearly, including the bank name, account name, and account number. If you accept transfers, POS, or cash, say so.

Next, add delivery or completion details. State when the work will be done, where it will be delivered, and what happens if the client changes the date or scope. For example: "Delivery to the venue in Lekki on Saturday 12 December by 10 a.m. Any change in guest number must be confirmed at least three days before the event."

Finally, add your terms and conditions in short, clear points. Cover cancellations, changes to the scope, and what is not included. Keep the language simple. Avoid long legal paragraphs that clients will not read. Five to seven short points are enough for most small jobs.

End with a closing line and a space for acceptance. Write something like "We look forward to working with you. Please sign below to confirm acceptance of this quotation." Include lines for the client's name, signature, and date. A signed quotation becomes a simple agreement that protects both sides.

## Step 4: Design and Format Your Quotation for Trust

Presentation matters. A quotation that is hard to read or looks careless will make a client doubt your work, even if your price is fair.

Use a clean, readable font such as Arial, Calibri, or Times New Roman, at size 11 or 12 for body text. Use bold for headings and totals. Use your brand colours sparingly: one colour for headings and one for accents is enough. Avoid bright backgrounds that make text hard to read.

Align your table properly. Numbers should line up. Use the naira symbol ₦ before amounts, and use commas in large figures, such as ₦150,000, not ₦150000. Check your arithmetic twice. A quotation with a wrong total is embarrassing and can cost you the job. If you use a spreadsheet or a Word table with formulas, the totals will calculate automatically and reduce errors.

Save the quotation as a PDF before you send it. A PDF looks the same on every phone and computer, and it cannot be edited accidentally. A Word document can shift in layout when opened on a different device. Name the file clearly, such as "Quotation-Catering-150-Guests-Ada-Events-Nov-2026.pdf," so the client can find it later.

Check spelling and grammar carefully. Read the quotation aloud before you send it. Ask a friend or colleague to check the client's name, the figures, and the date. A misspelt client name or a wrong amount is a common and avoidable mistake.

If you send the quotation on WhatsApp, send the PDF, not a screenshot of the document. A PDF is easier to print, forward to a manager, and store. Add a short message with it, such as "Good afternoon Mrs. Ade. Please find attached our quotation for your event as discussed. The total is ₦450,000, valid for 14 days. Please let me know if you have any questions." That message is professional and gives the client the key facts without opening the file.

## Common Quotation Mistakes Nigerian Small Businesses Should Avoid

The first mistake is sending a price with no breakdown. A single figure with no explanation forces the client to guess what is included. Always itemise. A client who understands the breakdown is more likely to accept the price.

The second mistake is forgetting to include your time and transport. A job that looks profitable on materials alone can become a loss once you add fuel, delivery, and your own labour. Include every cost, even if it is small.

The third mistake is using vague descriptions. "Branding" or "miscellaneous" as a line item tells the client nothing. Write specific descriptions: "Logo design — 3 concepts, 2 revisions," "Delivery to Lekki Phase 1," or "Installation and testing." Specificity builds trust.

The fourth mistake is not setting a validity period. Without one, a client can return months later and expect the same price, even though your costs have risen. Always add a validity period of seven, fourteen, or thirty days.

The fifth mistake is sending a quotation with errors. A wrong phone number, a wrong total, or a misspelt name can cost you a job. Check every detail before you send, and check it again after you convert it to PDF.

The sixth mistake is not keeping a copy. Save every quotation you send, with the date, the client name, and the outcome: accepted, declined, or no reply. Over time, those records will show you which prices win jobs, which clients pay on time, and which types of work are most profitable for you.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Business Quotations

**What is the difference between a quotation and an invoice?**

A quotation is sent before the job to show the price and terms so the client can decide. An invoice is sent after the job, or after an agreed milestone, to request payment for work done. A quotation can become the basis for an invoice once the client accepts it.

**How long should a quotation be valid?**

Seven to fourteen days is common for most small business jobs in Nigeria, because material prices and availability can change quickly. For larger projects, thirty days may be appropriate. State the validity period clearly on the quotation.

**What should I do if a client asks me to reduce my price?**

Ask what they would like to change. Offer a smaller scope, a simpler option, or a phased approach. Avoid reducing your price for the same work, as that reduces your profit and sets a difficult precedent. If the client's budget is genuinely too low for the job, it is better to decline politely than to accept a loss-making job.

**Can I send a quotation on WhatsApp?**

Yes. Many Nigerian clients expect quotations on WhatsApp. Send it as a PDF with a short, professional message. A PDF is easier to read, print, and forward than a long chat message or a screenshot.
