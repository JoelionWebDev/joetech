Every freelancer has a story about a difficult client. The one who approved the design, then changed their mind three times. The one who owes you money and stops replying to your messages. The one who calls at 11pm on a Sunday to ask for "one small change" that takes four hours.

Difficult clients are part of freelancing. You cannot avoid them entirely. But you can handle them in a way that protects the project, protects your income, and protects your reputation.

## First: Understand What "Difficult" Really Means

Not every difficult client is a bad client. Most of the time, difficult behaviour comes from something understandable:

- **They are anxious.** They are spending money they worked hard for, and they are afraid of getting it wrong. Anxiety shows up as constant questions and last-minute changes.
- **They do not know what they want.** They have a vague idea — "make it fine" — and they keep changing their mind because they have never seen the finished result before.
- **They feel unheard.** They asked for something, it did not happen, and now they are frustrated. Sometimes the frustration is justified.
- **They are under pressure themselves.** Their boss wants results, their own client is waiting, and the stress lands on you.

When you understand the cause, you can respond to the cause instead of reacting to the behaviour. That is the difference between a freelancer who loses clients and one who keeps them.

## Stay Calm — Always

This is the hardest rule and the most important one. When a client is rude, unfair, or unreasonable, your first instinct is to defend yourself. Do not.

- **Do not reply immediately.** Read the message, put the phone down, and give yourself time. A reply written in anger almost always makes things worse.
- **Do not match their tone.** If they shout in caps, you reply in calm, short sentences. If they accuse you, you answer with facts.
- **Do not take it personally.** Most of the time, the client's anger is about their own stress, not about you. Separate your feelings from the work and deal with the facts.

## Listen Before You Respond

When a client complains, the most useful thing you can do is listen properly. Let them finish. Then repeat back what you heard, in your own words:

"So if I understand correctly, the problem is that the homepage does not show your new products clearly, and you want the prices visible without clicking. Is that right?"

This does three things. It shows the client you are listening. It makes sure you actually understand the problem before you try to fix it. And it often calms the client down, because being understood is half of what they wanted.

## Set Boundaries Early — In Writing

Most difficult-client problems are boundary problems. The client asks for extra work because nobody told them where the work ends. The client calls at midnight because nobody told them your working hours. The client delays payment because nobody agreed on a payment date.

Prevent this before the project starts:

- **Put the scope in writing.** What is included, what is not, and how many rounds of revision are covered. A simple agreement or a clear quotation does this.
- **State your working hours.** "I work Monday to Saturday, 9am to 6pm, and I reply to messages within one working day." Reasonable clients respect this. Unreasonable ones reveal themselves early.
- **Agree payment terms upfront.** How much, when, and what happens if payment is late. A client who knows the terms is far less likely to delay.
- **Charge for extra work.** When a client asks for something outside the agreed scope, say so kindly: "That is outside what we agreed, but I can do it for an additional fee." This is not rude — it is professional. Free extra work teaches the client that your time has no value.

## How to Handle the Common Difficult Situations

### The client who keeps changing their mind

Limit revisions in your agreement — for example, two rounds of changes included, further changes at an extra cost. When a change request comes in, confirm it in writing before you act: "Just to confirm, you would like the colour changed to green and the headline rewritten. I will do that and send the new version tomorrow." Written confirmation stops the client from changing the same thing three times and then blaming you for the confusion.

### The client who will not pay

Stay polite, stay consistent, and keep everything in writing. Send a friendly reminder before the due date, a firm one on the due date, and a phone call if the payment is a week late. If the client has a genuine problem, offer a short payment plan. If the client is simply avoiding you, pause the work and tell them so: "I will be happy to continue once the outstanding payment is settled." Never start new work for a client who has not paid for the old work.

### The client who is never satisfied

Some clients will never be happy, no matter what you deliver. If you have met the agreed scope and the client still finds fault, ask a direct question: "Can you tell me exactly what needs to change for this to be complete?" Get the answer in writing. If the requests keep moving beyond the agreement, point back to the scope kindly and offer the extra work at an additional cost. If the client still cannot be satisfied, it may be time to end the project professionally and move on.

### The client who is rude or disrespectful

You do not have to accept abuse to keep a client. Be polite, be firm, and be clear: "I want to do good work for you, and I work best when we communicate respectfully." If the behaviour continues, you are allowed to end the working relationship. One rude client costs you more — in stress, in time, in lost work for good clients — than they are worth.

## When to Walk Away

Not every project is worth saving. Walk away — politely and professionally — when:

- The client refuses to pay for work already delivered.
- The client demands work far outside the agreement and will not pay for it.
- The client is abusive, and you have asked them to stop.
- The project is losing you money and damaging your health or your other work.

Walking away is not failure. It is a business decision. End it in writing, hand over what has been paid for, and move on.

## Protect Yourself for Next Time

Every difficult client teaches you something. After each project, ask yourself what you would do differently: a clearer agreement, a deposit upfront, a shorter revision window, a firmer payment term. Then build that lesson into your next project.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I take a deposit before starting work?

Yes, always. A deposit of 50 percent upfront is standard for freelance work in Nigeria, with the balance due on delivery or in agreed stages. A deposit protects you if the client disappears, and it shows the client is serious.

### What if the client threatens to damage my reputation?

Stay calm and keep everything in writing. Deliver the work you were paid for, keep your messages polite and factual, and do not argue publicly. Most threats are empty — the client is trying to get free work. If the situation is serious, keep all records and consider getting advice from a lawyer before responding further.

### How do I say no to extra work without losing the client?

Be warm and clear: "I would be glad to do that. It is outside the work we agreed, so it would cost an additional amount. Shall I send you a quick quote for it?" This says yes to the client and yes to your own business at the same time. Clients respect freelancers who value their own work.

### Is it okay to fire a client?

Yes. Freelancing is a business, and you choose who you work with. If a client is abusive, will not pay, or costs you more than they bring in, end the relationship politely, in writing, and move on. Your time is better spent finding clients who respect your work.

## The Client Is Not Always Right — But the Relationship Still Matters

You do not have to agree with every client. You do have to handle every client professionally. Stay calm, listen first, set clear boundaries, keep everything in writing, and know when to walk away. That is how you keep the good projects, survive the difficult ones, and build a freelance career that lasts.
