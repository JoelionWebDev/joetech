Nigeria is building. In Lagos, Abuja, Port Harcourt, Enugu, and every growing town in between, new houses, shops, schools, and estates go up every month — and almost all of them are built with concrete blocks. A block making business, also called a block industry, turns sand, cement, and water into the single most-used building material in the country. It is a business with steady demand, visible daily output, and a clear path from a small manual operation to a large mechanised yard.

This guide explains how to start a block making business in Nigeria, step by step: choosing a site, buying equipment, getting the mix right, pricing your blocks, and winning the builders and developers who will buy from you week after week.

## 1. Understand the Business First

A block maker produces concrete blocks — mainly 9-inch and 6-inch hollow blocks, and sometimes solid blocks — and sells them to builders, contractors, and individual homeowners. The customer usually buys in large quantities: a small house can require 2,000 to 5,000 blocks, and a bigger project can require tens of thousands.

The business is simple in structure but demanding in execution. Your profit per block is small, so you make money through volume, low waste, and efficient use of cement — your most expensive input. A block that is too weak will break, crack, or be rejected by builders, and a block that uses too much cement will eat your profit. The whole skill of the business is finding the balance: a strong block at a cost that leaves you a margin.

## 2. Research Your Area Before You Spend

Before you buy a machine or rent land, study the construction activity in your area. Drive around and count the building sites: new estates, houses under construction, shops, churches, schools. Talk to the builders and site engineers. Ask them where they buy their blocks, what they pay per block, what sizes they use most, and what they complain about — late delivery, weak blocks, inconsistent sizes, or suppliers who run out of stock mid-project.

Check the competition. How many block industries already serve the area? What are their prices? How far do they deliver? An area with heavy construction and only one or two suppliers is a good place to start. An area with five block industries on the same street is a price war waiting to happen.

Also check the availability of your raw materials. Sand, cement, and water are your lifeline. Is there a reliable sand supplier nearby? How far is the nearest cement depot, and what is the current price of a bag of cement? Is there water on the site, or will you need to buy water from tankers? A site far from sand and cement will bleed money on transport.

## 3. Register Your Business

Register a business name with the Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC). It is affordable, and builders, estate developers, and suppliers will take you more seriously with a registered name and a certificate. Open a business bank account and get a Tax Identification Number (TIN) from the Federal Inland Revenue Service.

You will also need to check local requirements for your site. Some local governments require a trade permit or a small levy for industrial operations. Ask at the local government office before you set up, not after. Keep your documents in a folder — builders who give you large orders sometimes ask for your CAC certificate before they pay a deposit.

## 4. Choose the Right Site

Location is one of the most important decisions in this business. The right site has four qualities: space, access, water, and proximity to customers and raw materials.

**Space.** You need enough land for the moulding area, a curing area where fresh blocks sit and harden, a stockpile of finished blocks, sand heaps, cement storage, and space for trucks to load and turn. A plot of about half a plot to one full plot of land is a reasonable starting size, though larger is better if you can afford it. Blocks take up a lot of space — a thousand 9-inch blocks stacked neatly still cover a large area.

**Access.** Trucks must be able to enter and leave easily, even in the rainy season. A site on a narrow street or at the end of a bad road will cost you deliveries. A site on or near a main road, close to where building is happening, is ideal.

**Water.** Block making uses a lot of water — for mixing, for curing, and for cleaning. A site with a borehole or a reliable well is a big advantage. If you have to buy water from tankers, factor that cost into your pricing from the start.

**Proximity.** The closer you are to your customers and your sand supplier, the lower your transport costs. Sand is heavy and cheap per tonne, so transporting it over a long distance can cost more than the sand itself.

## 5. Buy the Right Equipment

Your equipment decides your daily output and your block quality. Here is what you need:

**Block moulding machine.** This is your main investment. A manual mould is cheap — roughly ₦50,000 to ₦150,000 — but slow. A vibrating block moulding machine, which produces stronger and more uniform blocks, costs roughly ₦800,000 to ₦3,500,000 depending on capacity and whether it is locally fabricated or imported. A full hydraulic machine costs more, from about ₦4,000,000 upward, but produces blocks faster and with less labour. Buy the machine that matches your expected daily output, not the biggest one you can afford.

**Concrete mixer.** A mixer ensures the sand, cement, and water are blended evenly, which is essential for strong blocks. A fairly used mixer costs roughly ₦400,000 to ₦1,200,000. Mixing by hand is possible for a manual operation but slow and inconsistent.

**Moulds.** You will need moulds for 9-inch hollow blocks, 6-inch hollow blocks, and possibly solid blocks. Moulds wear out over time, so budget for replacements. A set of moulds costs roughly ₦100,000 to ₦400,000.

**Other essentials.** Wheelbarrows, shovels, head pans, a water tank or drums, pallets or boards for carrying fresh blocks, and a weighing scale or measuring boxes for the mix. Budget roughly ₦150,000 to ₦400,000 for these.

**Transport.** A truck for delivering blocks is a major advantage, because many customers expect delivery. A fairly used tipper or flatbed truck costs roughly ₦3,000,000 to ₦8,000,000. If that is too much at the start, hire a truck per delivery and add a delivery charge to the customer's bill.

## 6. Get the Mix Right — This Is the Heart of the Business

The quality of your blocks depends on the mix: the ratio of cement to sand, the amount of water, and how well the mix is compacted in the mould. Getting this right is the difference between a block industry that builders trust and one they avoid.

A common mix for 9-inch hollow blocks is one bag of cement to about 10 to 12 head pans of sharp sand, with just enough water to make the mix damp and workable — not wet and sloppy. The exact ratio depends on the quality of your sand, so test it. Make a batch, let the blocks cure properly, and then test their strength: a good block should not crumble when dropped from waist height, and it should not break easily when struck. Builders also check the edges — a good block has sharp, clean edges and a uniform size.

Sand quality matters enormously. Use sharp sand, also called river sand, not soft or salty sand. Sand with too much clay or silt weakens blocks. Buy from a reputable supplier and check each delivery: rub the sand between your fingers — if it feels smooth and leaves a lot of dust on your hands, it may have too much silt.

Curing is the step most beginners rush — and it is the step that makes the difference between a strong block and a weak one. Fresh blocks must be kept moist for at least 7 days, and ideally up to 14 days, before they are sold. Water them two or three times a day and keep them in the shade. Blocks sold after only two or three days of curing are weak, and builders who discover this will not come back. It is tempting to sell early when a customer is waiting, but a reputation for weak blocks is far more expensive than a few days of patience.

## 7. Work Out Your Costs and Price Your Blocks

Your price per block must cover cement, sand, water, labour, power, transport, and a profit. Cement is usually the biggest single cost — often more than half of the cost of a block — so the price of cement in your area sets the floor for your prices.

A realistic way to price is to calculate your cost per block, add your margin, and then check the result against what other block industries in your area charge. In many Nigerian cities, a 9-inch hollow block sells for roughly ₦500 to ₦800, and a 6-inch block for roughly ₦400 to ₦650, but these prices move with the price of cement and vary widely by location. Always confirm the current market price in your own area before you set yours.

Decide your payment terms. Most block sales are paid upfront or with a deposit before production, especially for large orders. For a big order, take a deposit of 50 to 70 percent before you start moulding, and collect the balance on delivery. Never mould thousands of blocks for a customer who has not paid a deposit — if they disappear, you are left with stock you may not be able to sell quickly.

Keep a simple cost record: bags of cement used per day, blocks produced, blocks sold, and cash received. If you know your cost per block, you will know immediately when a cement price increase means you must raise your selling price.

## 8. Find Customers and Win Their Trust

Your customers are builders, site engineers, contractors, estate developers, and individual homeowners building their own houses. Here is how to reach them:

**Visit building sites.** This is the most effective method. Go to active sites in your area, introduce yourself to the site engineer or the person in charge, and leave your contact details and a sample block. Site engineers buy blocks in large quantities, and once they trust your quality, they will order repeatedly.

**Build relationships with developers.** Estate developers buy tens of thousands of blocks over the life of a project. Offer them a standing arrangement: a fixed price for the duration of the project, guaranteed supply, and scheduled delivery. A developer who knows you will deliver 500 blocks every Tuesday does not need to shop around.

**Serve homeowners directly.** Many Nigerians build their houses gradually, buying blocks in batches as money comes in. Be friendly and flexible with these customers: allow them to buy 100 or 200 blocks at a time, keep their orders organised, and deliver promptly. They will recommend you to their neighbours.

Never compromise on the count. A customer who orders 500 blocks must receive 500 blocks. Count carefully at loading, and let the customer or their representative count at delivery if they want to. Short-counting is the fastest way to lose a customer in this business.

## 9. Run the Daily Operation

A block industry runs on a daily routine that starts early. Moulding usually begins in the morning when it is cool, because fresh blocks cure better when they are not moulded in the heat of the day.

**Morning:** Check the orders for the day. Measure out sand and cement, mix, and begin moulding. Fresh blocks are carried on boards or pallets to the curing area and arranged neatly.

**Midday:** Water the blocks moulded in previous days. Load deliveries for customers. Receive sand and cement deliveries and check them.

**Afternoon:** Continue moulding if there are orders. Stack cured blocks in the sales area. Take new orders and confirm deliveries for the next day.

**Evening:** Count the day's production, count the stock of cured blocks, reconcile the cash, and record the cement used. This daily record keeps your costs honest and tells you whether you are making money.

Protect your cement. Cement is your most valuable input and a target for theft. Store it in a locked room, count the bags daily, and reconcile bags used against blocks produced. If the numbers do not match, find out why immediately.

## 10. Hire and Manage Your Team

A small block industry needs a machine operator, two to four moulders or labourers, and possibly a driver. The machine operator is your most important worker — their skill affects the quality of every block. Pay them well and treat them well, because a good operator is hard to replace.

Pay your workers fairly and on time. Daily or weekly pay is common in this trade. Agree on the rates clearly at the start: some workers are paid a daily wage, others are paid per block produced. Per-block pay encourages speed, but watch the quality — workers paid per block may rush and produce weak blocks. A combination of a base wage plus a small bonus for meeting quality and output targets works well.

Train your team on the mix, the moulding technique, and the curing routine. The owner should be able to do every job in the yard — if your operator is absent, you should still be able to produce. Your presence on site, especially in the early months, is what keeps quality consistent.

## 11. Common Problems and How to Handle Them

**Cement price increases.** Cement prices in Nigeria change frequently, and each increase squeezes your margin. When the price rises, raise your block price promptly and inform your customers. Most builders understand, because they face the same increases on other materials. Never absorb a big increase silently and hope it goes away.

**Weak blocks.** The usual causes are a poor mix, bad sand, too little water during curing, or selling blocks before they have cured properly. Fix the cause, not the symptom. Test your mix, check your sand, and enforce the curing period.

**Machine breakdowns.** A broken moulding machine on a busy Monday morning is a disaster. Service your machines regularly, keep spare parts for the parts that wear out most, and know a reliable mechanic. Budget for maintenance every month.

**Bad debts.** Never deliver a large order on credit to a customer you do not know well. Take deposits, and for regular customers who buy on credit, set a strict limit and a clear repayment date. A developer who owes you for two months is not a customer — they are a risk.

## 12. Growing the Business

Once your yard is running profitably, growth can take several forms. Add a second machine to double your daily output. Add a truck so you can deliver directly and charge for delivery. Open a second yard in a fast-growing area, managed by a supervisor you trust. Or move into related products: interlocking paving stones, kerbs, and concrete rings use the same raw materials and the same skills, and they open up new customers.

As you grow, a simple professional presence helps you win bigger clients. A basic website or a WhatsApp Business catalogue with your prices, your location, and photos of your yard makes developers take you more seriously. ## Conclusion

Block making is a business built on a simple truth: as long as Nigerians keep building, somebody has to make the blocks. It is hard physical work, and it demands discipline about quality, curing, and costs. But it is also one of the most straightforward businesses you can start: the raw materials are available, the market is visible on every street with a building site, and the path from a manual operation to a mechanised yard is clear.

Start with research, choose a good site, buy equipment that matches your ambition, and — above all — get the mix and the curing right. Deliver strong blocks, count honestly, and collect your money on time. Do that every day, and your block industry will grow one building site at a time.
