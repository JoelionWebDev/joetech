A 40-page report lands in your inbox. A client sends a two-hour webinar recording. A long article keeps showing up in your reading list, and you never get past the first paragraph. The information is there, but the time to read or watch all of it is not.

This is exactly the kind of work AI handles well. Modern AI assistants can read a long document, watch a video transcript, and pull out the main points in minutes. You still need to think — the summary is a starting point, not a replacement for your judgement.

## How AI Summarising Actually Works

An AI summariser does not read the way you do. It scans the full text, works out which ideas repeat and which sentences carry the most meaning, and then writes a shorter version in plain language. For videos, it usually works from the transcript — the written version of everything said — rather than the pictures.

That is why the quality of the summary depends on the quality of the source. A clear, well-written report produces a clean summary. A rambling meeting recording with three people talking over each other produces a messier one.

## Summarising a PDF

PDFs are the most common thing people need summarised: reports, proposals, contracts, training manuals, and e-books. Here is a practical way to do it.

### 1. Get the text out of the PDF

Most AI assistants accept a PDF file directly — you upload it and ask for a summary. If the tool you are using does not accept files, open the PDF, select the text, copy it, and paste it into the chat. If the PDF is a scanned image with no selectable text, the AI cannot read it until you convert it, so use a PDF reader that can extract the text first.

### 2. Tell the AI what kind of summary you want

A vague request — "summarise this" — gives you a generic result. Be specific about what you need:

- "Summarise this in 10 bullet points, keeping all figures and dates."
- "Give me a one-paragraph summary a busy manager can read in 30 seconds."
- "List the main arguments for and against, with one sentence each."
- "What are the action points in this report, and who is responsible for each one?"

The clearer your instruction, the more useful the result. Think about what you will do with the summary before you ask for it.

### 3. Ask follow-up questions

A summary is not a one-shot job. Once you have the overview, dig into the parts that matter to you:

- "Explain the section about pricing in more detail."
- "What does the report say about the risks?"

This turns a single summary into a conversation with the document, and it is often faster than reading the whole thing yourself.

### 4. Check the important details

AI can get small things wrong — a figure, a name, a date. If a number or a claim matters for a decision, find it in the original document and confirm it. A summary is a map, not the territory. Use it to know where to look, then look.

## Summarising an Article

Long articles are simpler than PDFs, because the text is already on a web page. Copy the article text and paste it into your AI assistant, or paste the link if the tool can read web pages.

Ask for the format that fits your purpose. If you are researching a topic, ask for the key arguments and the evidence behind them. If you are deciding whether the article is worth reading in full, ask for a short summary and a verdict on what is new.

## Summarising a Video

Videos take the longest to consume, so they benefit the most from summarising. A two-hour training video can become a one-page summary.

### Use the transcript

Most videos on YouTube and similar platforms have a transcript — a written record of everything said. On YouTube, open the description and look for the transcript option, or use a free transcript tool. Copy the transcript text and paste it into your AI assistant, then ask for a summary with timestamps if you want to jump back to specific parts later.

### Ask for structure, not just a paragraph

A video summary works best when it is organised. Ask for:

- The main points, in the order they appear in the video.
- Any step-by-step process explained in the video, written out as numbered steps.
- The tools, links, or resources mentioned.
- Anything the speaker said to avoid or warned against.

With timestamps in the summary, you can go back and watch only the three minutes that matter, instead of sitting through the whole recording.

## Getting Better Summaries: Five Rules

### 1. Say who the summary is for

"Summarise this for a small business owner in Lagos" gives a different result from "summarise this for a university student." The AI will pick different details and use different language. A sentence about your audience makes the summary far more useful.

### 2. Set the length

Tell the AI how long you want the result: "in five bullet points", "in under 200 words", "in one page". Without a length, summaries tend to be either too short to be useful or almost as long as the original.

### 3. Ask for what matters to you

If you only care about the costs, say so. If you only care about the deadlines, say so. A focused question — "What are the costs mentioned in this document?" — beats a general one every time.

### 4. Keep the original nearby

Do not delete the source after summarising it. Keep the PDF, the link, or the video saved, so you can go back and verify anything important. A summary is a shortcut, not a substitute for the source when a decision depends on it.

### 5. Never trust a summary blindly on important matters

AI summaries are very good, but they can misread a sentence, mix up two numbers, or state something confidently that is not in the document at all. For everyday reading and research, a quick check is enough. For anything that affects money, a contract, a legal matter, or someone's health, verify every key fact in the original.

## Free Ways to Start

You do not need a paid subscription to start summarising with AI. The free versions of the well-known AI assistants — ChatGPT, Google Gemini, and Claude — can all summarise pasted text and, in most cases, uploaded PDFs. Start with one of them, paste a document you already need to read, and compare the summary with the original.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can AI summarise a scanned PDF?

Only if the text can be extracted from it. A scanned PDF is an image, so the AI cannot read the words until the image is converted to text. Use a PDF reader or a free OCR tool to extract the text first, then paste it into the AI assistant. If the PDF has selectable text, you can upload or paste it directly.

### How long a document can AI summarise?

Most free AI assistants handle documents of several thousand words without trouble, and many accept files of dozens of pages. Very long documents may need to be split into parts — summarise each section separately, then ask for a summary of the section summaries.

### Is it safe to upload private documents to an AI tool?

Be careful with sensitive material. Avoid uploading documents that contain private client information, passwords, bank details, or confidential business data to a free AI service. For public reports, articles, and training material, there is little risk. For anything confidential, summarise it yourself, or use a tool your organisation has approved for private data.

### Will the summary ever be wrong?

Yes, sometimes. AI can misread a figure, confuse two similar names, or add a detail that was not in the source. That is why you should always check the numbers, names, and dates that matter before you act on a summary. For everyday reading the risk is small; for decisions involving money or commitments, verify against the original.

## Start With the Longest Thing on Your List

Pick the longest PDF, article, or video you have been avoiding. Upload it or paste the text, ask for a summary in the format you need, and read the result. Then check one or two key points against the original. You will know within ten minutes whether this saves you real time — and for most people, it does.
