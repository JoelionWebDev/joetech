## What Cash Flow Really Means for a Small Business

Cash flow is the movement of money in and out of your business. Money comes in when customers pay you, and money goes out when you buy stock, pay rent, pay staff, buy fuel for the generator, or settle a supplier. When more money comes in than goes out, your business can breathe. When more goes out than comes in, you feel the pressure immediately, even if the business looks busy on paper.

Many Nigerian small businesses fail not because they have no customers, but because the money is not managed well. A shop in Ikeja can sell goods worth ₦800,000 in a month and still struggle to pay rent, because most of that money went back into restocking, personal spending, and unpaid customer debts.

## Step 1: Separate Your Business Money from Your Personal Money

This is the single most important step in managing cash flow, and it is also the one most small business owners in Nigeria skip. When your business money and your personal money sit in the same account or the same wallet, you cannot tell how your business is really doing. You may think you made a profit, when in fact you spent the profit on school fees, food, or a family event.

### Open a Separate Business Account

Open a bank account that is used only for the business. It does not need to be a corporate account with heavy documentation at first; a separate personal savings or current account dedicated to the business works as a starting point, and you can upgrade later as the business grows. The rule is simple: business money goes into the business account, and business expenses are paid from it.

All customer payments should go into this account. If a customer pays by bank transfer, give them the business account number. If they pay by POS, link the POS to the business account. If they pay cash, deposit it into the business account at the end of the day.

### Pay Yourself a Fixed Salary

Once the money is separated, pay yourself a fixed amount each month, just like an employee. Decide on a realistic figure based on what the business can afford, for example ₦100,000 or ₦150,000 a month, and transfer that amount to your personal account on a fixed date. That is your salary. Everything else stays in the business.

This habit changes everything. It stops the daily dipping into the till for transport fare, lunch, or small personal needs. It also forces you to see the truth: if the business cannot pay you your fixed salary and still cover its costs, then the business has a cash flow problem that needs fixing, not hiding.## Step 2: Track Every Naira In and Out

You cannot manage what you do not measure. If you do not know exactly how much came in last week and how much went out, you are running your business blind. Tracking does not have to be complicated, but it has to be consistent.

### Use a Simple Daily Record

Every day, record two things: money in and money out. Money in includes cash sales, bank transfers, and POS payments. Money out includes stock purchases, transport, airtime, data, fuel, staff wages, rent contributions, and any other expense, no matter how small.

You can use a notebook, a spreadsheet on your phone, or a simple bookkeeping app. The tool matters less than the habit.

### Record Sales the Same Day

Do not wait until the end of the week to record sales. Memory fades, receipts get lost, and cash gets mixed up. Make it a closing routine: before you lock up for the day, spend ten minutes writing down the day's sales and expenses. If you have staff, make one person responsible for this, and check the record yourself at least twice a week.

For businesses that sell on credit, record the sale and the amount owed separately. Write the customer's name, phone number, amount owed, and the date they promised to pay. This small record is the difference between chasing money you forgot about and collecting money you are owed.

### Sort Your Expenses into Categories

At the end of each week, group your expenses into a few simple categories: stock and supplies, rent and utilities, transport and logistics, staff costs, marketing, and personal withdrawals. When you see the categories, patterns appear. You may discover that transport is eating more money than you thought. That knowledge lets you cut where it matters.

### Reconcile Weekly

Once a week, compare your records with your actual bank balance and cash on hand. If your records say you should have ₦250,000 but the balance is ₦210,000, find the ₦40,000 gap. It could be an unrecorded expense or a transfer you forgot. Finding gaps early keeps small problems from becoming big ones.

## Step 3: Build a Simple Cash Flow Forecast

A cash flow forecast is a simple prediction of the money you expect to come in and go out over the next month or three months. It helps you see trouble before it arrives, so you can prepare instead of panicking.

### Start with a One-Month Forecast

Take a sheet of paper or a spreadsheet and list the money you expect to receive: regular customers, pending orders, and money owed to you that customers promised to pay this month. Then list the money you expect to spend: rent, salaries, stock, fuel, transport, and any fixed bills. Subtract the expected money out from the expected money in. If the result is positive, you have room to breathe. If it is negative, you have a gap to close before it becomes a crisis.

### Example: A Small Fashion Shop in Lekki

Imagine a small fashion shop that expects ₦600,000 in sales this month, with ₦150,000 rent, ₦120,000 salaries, ₦200,000 for stock, and ₦70,000 for transport and other costs. Total money out is ₦540,000, leaving ₦60,000. That is workable, but tight. If sales drop to ₦450,000, the shop would be short by ₦90,000. Seeing that in advance means the owner can delay some stock purchases or push harder on sales before the month ends.

### Update the Forecast Every Week

A forecast is not a one-time document. Update it every week as real figures come in. If a customer who promised to pay ₦80,000 this week delays payment, update the forecast and adjust your spending.

### Plan for Three Months, Not Just One

Once you are comfortable with a monthly forecast, extend it to three months. This helps you prepare for known big expenses: annual rent renewal, festive season stocking, and the quiet months after Christmas when sales in many Nigerian markets slow down. A three-month view turns surprises into plans.

## Step 4: Handle Slow Months Without Panic

Every business in Nigeria has slow months. For many traders, January is slow after the December spending rush. For others, the rainy season reduces foot traffic, or a fuel scarcity week cuts sales sharply. Slow months are normal. What matters is how you prepare for them.

### Know Your Slow Season

Look at your sales records over the past year, or ask yourself honestly when sales usually drop. Is it January and February? Is it during school resumption? Write down your slow months and your busy months. This pattern is your business calendar, and it should guide your spending.

### Cut Costs Before the Slow Month Arrives

When you know a slow month is coming, reduce your spending in advance. Buy less stock, pause non-essential spending, and negotiate with suppliers for smaller, more frequent deliveries instead of one large order.

## Step 5: Manage Customer Credit Carefully

Giving goods or services on credit is common in Nigerian business. Regular customers, corporate clients, and even neighbours may ask to "pay later." Credit can build loyalty and win bigger orders, but unmanaged credit is one of the fastest ways to destroy cash flow. Your shelves may be empty and your customers happy, while your bank account is empty because nobody has paid.

### Set Clear Credit Rules

Decide in advance who gets credit and how much. Not every customer should get credit. A good rule is to offer credit only to customers with a history of paying on time, and only up to an amount you can afford to wait for. For new customers, ask for full payment or at least a deposit before you deliver.

Write your credit terms down and tell the customer clearly: how much they owe, when payment is due, and what happens if they delay. A simple written note or WhatsApp message confirming the amount and date protects both sides.

### Limit How Much Credit Is Outstanding

Set a maximum total amount of credit you are willing to carry at any time. For example, if your monthly sales are ₦500,000, you might decide that no more than ₦100,000 should be outstanding in customer debts at any time. When you hit that limit, stop giving new credit until old debts are collected. This cap protects your cash flow from being locked up in other people's hands.

### Follow Up on Payments Promptly

Do not wait for customers to remember. On the day payment is due, send a polite reminder. A short message works: "Good morning, this is a friendly reminder that your balance of ₦45,000 is due today. Thank you." Most customers are not trying to avoid payment; they simply forgot or are juggling their own cash flow. A polite, consistent reminder keeps your money coming in.

If a customer misses the date, follow up again within a few days. Be firm but respectful. If a customer repeatedly fails to pay, stop giving them credit, no matter how loyal they seem. Loyalty that does not pay is a cost, not a benefit.

## Step 6: Pay Suppliers on Time and Negotiate Smartly

Suppliers are the backbone of your business. If you pay them late or inconsistently, you lose trust, you may lose access to goods, and you may lose the chance to negotiate better prices. Managing supplier payments well is a core part of cash flow management.

### Know Your Payment Dates

List all your suppliers, what you buy from them, how much you owe, and when payment is due. Some suppliers in Lagos markets allow weekly settlement, others demand payment on delivery, and some offer 7 or 14 days of credit. Knowing these dates helps you plan your cash flow so that you are never caught without money when a supplier expects payment.

### Negotiate Payment Terms

Do not accept the first terms offered. Ask suppliers for better terms, especially if you are a regular customer who pays reliably. You can ask for a few extra days to pay, a small discount for early payment, or the option to pay in two instalments for a large order.

### Buy Stock in Line with Sales, Not Hope

One of the biggest cash flow mistakes is overstocking. Buying ₦500,000 worth of goods because you hope sales will be strong ties up cash that you may need for rent or salaries. Buy based on what you actually sell, and restock in smaller batches more often.

## Step 7: Build a Small Emergency Buffer

An emergency buffer is a small pot of money set aside for unexpected problems: a generator breakdown, a sudden rent increase, a week of low sales because of fuel scarcity, or a supplier demanding payment earlier than expected. Without a buffer, every surprise becomes a crisis.

Start small and be consistent. Start with a fixed amount each week or month, for example ₦10,000 a week or ₦40,000 a month, and move it into a separate savings account or a locked savings pocket that you do not touch for daily spending. The key is consistency, not size.

Keep the buffer separate from your daily business money, or you will be tempted to use it for routine expenses. Keep it in a separate account, or a separate savings wallet, that requires a deliberate decision to access. The small friction of transferring money out of the buffer is enough to stop casual use.

Decide in advance what counts as an emergency and write it down: generator repair, urgent restocking when a big order comes in, or covering salaries during a slow month. It is not for a new phone, a family celebration, or an impulse purchase.

## Step 8: Use Simple Tools and Spreadsheets

You do not need expensive accounting software to manage cash flow well. Simple tools, used consistently, are enough for most small businesses in Nigeria.

A simple spreadsheet on your phone or laptop can track everything: daily sales, expenses, customer debts, supplier payments, and your monthly forecast. Create columns for date, description, money in, money out, and balance. Update it daily, and review it weekly. If you prefer paper, a well-kept notebook with the same columns works just as well.

Turn on transaction alerts for your business account so you see every debit and credit immediately, and review your bank statement at the end of each month against your own records.

When you sell to corporate clients or customers who pay later, send a clear invoice with your business name, the items supplied, the total amount, your account details, and the payment due date. A proper invoice makes it easier for the customer to pay and easier for you to follow up. ## Common Cash Flow Mistakes to Avoid

Even with a good system, certain habits quietly damage cash flow. Watch out for these common mistakes.

First, confusing sales with profit. Selling goods worth ₦1,000,000 in a month does not mean you made ₦1,000,000. After subtracting the cost of goods, rent, salaries, transport, and other expenses, the real profit may be far smaller.

Second, spending windfalls immediately. When a big payment lands, it is tempting to celebrate by spending. But a large payment often has to cover weeks of expenses, supplier bills, and future stock. Before spending a windfall, check your forecast and set aside what the business needs first.

## A Simple Weekly Cash Flow Routine

Systems work when they become routine. Here is a simple weekly routine you can follow, taking less than an hour a week in total.

On Monday morning, check your bank balance and your cash on hand. Review last week's sales and expenses, update your forecast, and note any customer payments due this week and any supplier payments you must make. During the week, record sales and expenses daily and send payment reminders on due dates. On Friday or Saturday, reconcile your records with your bank balance, transfer your buffer contribution, and review the week: what sold well, what did not, and what needs to change next week.

Repeat this routine every week. After a month, it becomes a habit. After three months, you will know your business numbers better than most business owners in your area.

## Your 30-Day Cash Flow Action Plan

If you are starting from scratch, here is a simple plan for the next 30 days.

**Week 1:** Open or designate a separate business account. Set your fixed monthly salary. Start recording every sale and expense daily, even if you use a simple notebook.

**Week 2:** List all your customers who owe you money, with amounts and due dates. Start following up on the oldest debts. List all your suppliers and payment due dates.

**Week 3:** Build your first one-month cash flow forecast. Identify your slow months and your busy months. Set up a separate savings account or pocket for your emergency buffer and make your first contribution.

**Week 4:** Review your first month of records. Group expenses into categories and look for waste. Reconcile your records with your bank balance. Adjust your salary, your stock orders, and your credit rules based on what the numbers show.

After 30 days, you will have a clear picture of your business finances for the first time, and you will be able to make decisions based on facts instead of feelings.

## Conclusion

Managing cash flow in a small business in Nigeria is not about complex formulas or expensive software. It is about simple, consistent habits: separating your money, tracking every naira, forecasting ahead, handling slow months with a plan, controlling customer credit, paying suppliers on time, and building a small buffer for surprises.

Start with one step today. Open the separate account, or start the daily record, or list the money customers owe you. Then add the next step next week.
