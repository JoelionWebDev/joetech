## The Customers You Already Had Are Your Cheapest Growth

Every small business in Nigeria knows the feeling. A customer who used to buy from you every week suddenly stops. No message, no complaint, no goodbye — they simply disappear. You notice it when you check your sales book, or when you realise you have not seen Mama Ngozi's face at your shop in a month.

Most business owners respond by chasing new customers. New customers are expensive to find: you pay for adverts, you run promotions, you spend time convincing strangers to trust you. Meanwhile, the customers who already know you, already trust you, and already know how to find you are sitting quietly on your phone, one message away.

Winning back a lost customer costs far less than finding a new one, and a customer who comes back after leaving often becomes more loyal than before — because they chose you a second time. This guide shows you how to find out why they left, how to reach out without begging, and how to fix what pushed them away in the first place.

## Step 1: Find Out Who Has Actually Left

Before you can win anyone back, you need to know who is gone. Many business owners work from memory and guesswork. That is not enough. You need a list.

### Build a simple customer list

Go through your sales records, your WhatsApp chats, your bank alerts, and your POS receipts from the last three to six months. Write down every customer who used to buy regularly — weekly or monthly — and has not bought in the last 60 to 90 days. For each one, note:

- Their name and phone number
- What they usually bought
- How often they bought
- How much they typically spent
- When they last bought from you

You can do this in a notebook, but a simple spreadsheet on your phone works better because you can sort it. Even fifty names is a good start. Do not worry about having a perfect system — a rough list you actually use beats a perfect system you never open.

### Sort them into three groups

Not every lost customer is worth the same effort. Sort your list into three groups:

**Group A — your best customers.** They bought often and spent well. A hair vendor in Ikeja who bought ₦150,000 of stock every month, or a restaurant in Surulere that ordered ₦80,000 of foodstuff every two weeks. These are your priority. Losing one of them hurts, and winning one back is worth real money.

**Group B — regular customers.** They bought steadily but in smaller amounts. A neighbour who bought groceries every Saturday, or an office worker who ordered lunch twice a week. They are worth a message and a good offer, but do not spend hours on any single one.

**Group C — one-time or occasional buyers.** They bought once or twice and never came back. They may have been passing through, or they may have been testing you. A single friendly message is enough. Do not chase them.

### Count what you have lost

Add up roughly what your Group A and Group B customers used to spend each month. If ten customers who each spent ₦50,000 a month have stopped buying, that is ₦500,000 a month in lost revenue — ₦6 million in a year. Seeing the number written down changes how seriously you take the problem, and it tells you how much effort and discounting is worth spending to fix it.

## Step 2: Find Out Why They Left

Customers rarely leave for the reason you think. You might assume they found a cheaper price, when the real reason is that your shop assistant was rude to them once, or that you were out of stock three times in a row, or that a competitor opened closer to their house.

Guessing wrong means fixing the wrong thing. So ask.

### The honest conversation

For your Group A customers, pick up the phone and call them. Not a broadcast message — a real call. Keep it short, warm and honest:

"Good afternoon, Mrs. Adeyemi, this is Tunde from the shop on Allen Avenue. I noticed we have not seen you in a while, and I wanted to check in. Is everything okay? Was there anything about our service that you were not happy with?"

Then stop talking and listen. Most people will tell you the truth if you ask without being defensive. Do not argue, do not make excuses, and do not try to sell them anything on that first call. Your only job is to understand.

### The short message for everyone else

For Group B customers, a short WhatsApp message works:

"Hello, this is [your name] from [your business]. We miss seeing you and wanted to check if there is anything we can do better. Your feedback would really help us. Thank you."

Keep it personal — use their name, mention what they used to buy if you can. A message that could have been sent to a thousand people will be ignored like a message sent to a thousand people.

### The reasons you will hear

In Nigerian small businesses, the reasons customers leave usually fall into a few patterns:

- **Price:** They found the same product cheaper elsewhere, or your prices went up and they could not keep up. This is common, but it is not always the real reason — people often say "price" when they mean something else.
- **Stock problems:** You were out of what they wanted too many times. A customer who comes twice and finds an empty shelf will try the shop down the road, and if that shop has stock, they may never come back.
- **Poor service:** A rude attendant, a long wait, a wrong order, a promise that was not kept. In a market where many shops sell the same goods, service is often the only difference.
- **Convenience:** They moved house, changed jobs, or a competitor opened nearer to them. You cannot fix this with an apology, but you can fix it with delivery.
- **Quality dropped:** The product changed — the rice is not as good, the fabric is thinner, the food tastes different. Quality problems are serious because they break trust.
- **They simply forgot:** Life got busy. Nobody offended them. They just drifted away, and nobody reminded them you exist. This is the easiest group to win back, and often the biggest one.

Write down every reason you hear. After twenty or thirty conversations, the pattern will be obvious, and you will know exactly what to fix.

## Step 3: Fix the Problem Before You Invite Them Back

This step is the one most businesses skip, and it is why most win-back attempts fail. If a customer left because your attendant was rude, and you invite them back without changing anything, they will have one bad experience and leave forever. Fix first, invite second.

### If the problem was price

You do not have to become the cheapest shop in the area. But you do need to be fair and transparent. Check your prices against two or three competitors for your most popular items. If you are charging ₦12,000 for something others sell at ₦9,500, you need either to lower the price or to give the customer a clear reason to pay more — better quality, better packaging, free delivery, a guarantee.

One practical approach is to create a loyalty price for returning customers: "For our regular customers, this item is ₦10,000 instead of ₦12,000." The customer feels valued, and you keep a margin that still works.

### If the problem was stock

Fix your ordering. Keep a small notebook or a phone note of your fastest-selling items and the day of the week they usually run out. Order before you run out, not after. If a supplier is unreliable, find a backup supplier now, before the next stockout. And when something is out of stock, tell the customer honestly when it will be back — and then message them the day it arrives. That single habit brings people back.

### If the problem was service

Talk to your staff. Explain, with a real example, what went wrong and what good service looks like: greet customers, answer questions patiently, never argue, and if a mistake happens, apologise and fix it quickly. If one staff member keeps driving customers away, you have a staffing decision to make. Your staff are your business to the customer — when they are rude, the customer does not blame the staff member, they blame you.

### If the problem was quality

Go back to your supplier and check what changed. Taste the food, inspect the fabric, test the product yourself. If the supplier's quality has dropped, switch suppliers or pay a little more for the better grade. A customer who left over quality will only return if the quality is genuinely back, and they will test it on the first order.

### If the problem was convenience

Offer delivery. A customer who moved from Lekki to Ajah may still buy from you if a dispatch rider can bring the order to their door for ₦1,500 to ₦3,000. Set up a simple delivery arrangement with a rider you trust, and tell your customers about it. Delivery turns a distance problem into a non-problem.

## Step 4: Reach Out the Right Way

Now you are ready to contact the customers you want back. The goal of this message is simple: remind them you exist, show them you have listened, and give them a reason to return. It is not a sales pitch.

### The win-back message that works

A good win-back message has four parts:

1. **A personal greeting.** Use their name. Remind them who you are.
2. **An honest acknowledgement.** Show you noticed they have been away, without guilt-tripping them.
3. **What has changed.** Tell them, briefly, what you fixed.
4. **A reason to come back.** A small offer, a new product, or a service improvement.

Here is an example for a foodstuff seller:

"Good morning, Mrs. Okafor, this is Amaka from Amaka Foodstuffs in Yaba. We have missed you — you were one of our regulars for your monthly garri and crayfish. I know we were out of stock a few times, and I am sorry about that. We have fixed our supply now, and we also deliver to your area. As a welcome back, your next order comes with a free small bag of dried pepper. We would love to serve you again."

Notice what this message does not do: it does not beg, it does not offer a huge discount, and it does not pretend nothing happened. It is honest, specific and respectful.

### Timing matters

Send win-back messages at a time when people actually read them — late morning or early evening on weekdays, or Saturday morning. Avoid very early mornings and late nights. And send them one by one, not as a broadcast. WhatsApp broadcast lists feel impersonal, and customers can tell.

Do not send more than two messages to someone who does not reply. One message, then one gentle follow-up after a week. After that, leave them alone. Chasing a customer who has decided to move on will only damage your reputation.

## Step 5: Make the Offer Worth Coming Back For

A small, well-chosen offer gives a hesitant customer a reason to try you once more. The offer should be big enough to notice and small enough that you can afford it.

### Offers that work in Nigeria

- **A welcome-back discount:** 10 to 15 percent off their next order. Enough to feel real, not so much that it destroys your margin.
- **A free small item:** A free small bag of rice with a bulk order, a free sausage roll with a lunch order, a free sample of a new product. Free extras feel generous and cost you less than a straight discount.
- **Free delivery on the next order:** Delivery fees of ₦1,500 to ₦3,000 are often the reason a customer hesitates. Removing the fee for one order removes the friction.
- **A loyalty deal:** "Buy three, get the fourth at half price" for returning customers. This encourages a bigger order and gives them a reason to stay.
- **Early access to something new:** "We just got a new stock of Ankara prints, and our returning customers get first pick before we post them publicly." Exclusivity works.

### Worked example: the numbers behind a win-back offer

Imagine a customer who used to spend ₦40,000 a month with you, and has been gone for three months. That is ₦120,000 in lost sales. If you offer 15 percent off their next ₦40,000 order, the discount costs you ₦6,000. If they return and stay for another six months, you recover ₦240,000 in sales. Spending ₦6,000 to recover ₦240,000 is one of the best investments your business can make. Do the same maths for your own customers before you decide an offer is "too expensive."

## Step 6: Make the Return Visit Count

Getting a customer through the door once more is only half the job. The return visit is an audition — they are watching to see whether anything has actually changed. Make it count.

- **Recognise them.** Greet them by name. "Mrs. Adeyemi, welcome back! We have missed you." People remember being remembered.
- **Deliver perfectly.** Make sure their order is correct, the quality is good, and the service is warm. One mistake on the return visit confirms their decision to leave.
- **Do not overdo it.** Be warm, not desperate. Do not hover, do not ask five times if everything is okay, and do not remind them of the discount every two minutes.
- **Ask for feedback after.** A day or two later, send a short message: "Thank you for coming back. How was everything? Is there anything we can do better?" This shows the change is real, not a one-day performance.

## Step 7: Keep Them This Time

Winning a customer back is pointless if you lose them again in a month. The work that keeps customers is quiet, boring and consistent.

### Stay in touch without being annoying

Send a short message once or twice a month — a new arrival, a useful tip, a holiday greeting. Not a daily broadcast, not a sales pitch every morning. A fashion seller might send photos of new arrivals on the first Saturday of the month. A food vendor might send a short message when fresh stock arrives. The goal is to stay in the customer's mind without becoming a nuisance.

### Remember what they buy

Keep a simple record of each regular customer's usual order. When Mrs. Okafor calls, you should know she buys 10kg of garri and 2kg of crayfish every month. When you have something new that fits her taste, tell her first. Customers stay with sellers who know them.

### Reward loyalty quietly

You do not need a printed loyalty card. A simple system works: after every five purchases, the sixth comes with a small free item or a discount. Tell your regular customers about it once, then just do it. People notice when they are rewarded without having to ask.

## Checklist: Winning Back Lost Customers

- [ ] List of customers who have not bought in 60 to 90 days
- [ ] Customers sorted into Groups A, B and C
- [ ] Group A customers called, reasons for leaving recorded
- [ ] Top problems identified from the pattern of answers
- [ ] Problems fixed before any win-back message is sent
- [ ] Personal win-back message sent with a specific offer and deadline
- [ ] One gentle follow-up after a week for non-replies
- [ ] Returning customers welcomed by name and served perfectly
- [ ] Feedback requested after the return visit
- [ ] Simple system in place to stay in touch monthly

Your lost customers did not leave because they hate your business. Most of them left for a fixable reason, and many of them would come back if someone simply asked them to — and showed them that something has changed. Be the business that asks. Start with your list today, make the calls this week, and give your best customers a genuine reason to come home.
