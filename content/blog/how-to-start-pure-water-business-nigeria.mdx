## Why Pure Water Is a Steady Business

Pure water, or sachet water, is one of the most bought products in Nigeria. People drink it at home, at work, in school, at parties, and on the road. In hot weather, a bag of cold sachet water sells itself. Unlike fashion or electronics, the demand does not depend on trends. Everyone needs water, every day, and that makes it a strong business for someone who wants steady, repeat sales.

The business also fits Nigeria well because it serves a local area. You do not need customers across the country. You need homes, shops, offices, schools, and events within a few streets of your factory. A vendor who supplies 50 bags a day to regular customers in one neighbourhood has a reliable income without a large marketing budget.

But pure water is a regulated food product, and it is a business where quality is everything. Customers drink what you produce. If your water is not clean, or your factory is not registered, you risk people's health and your business. This guide explains the real costs, the registration steps, the production process, and how to sell profitably and legally.

## How Much Capital Do You Need?

A small pure water factory costs about ₦800,000 to ₦2,000,000 to set up properly, depending on your location and whether you buy new or fairly used machines. That is more than many small businesses, so plan carefully and do not cut corners on water treatment or registration.

Here is a realistic breakdown for a small setup of about ₦1,200,000:

Machines and equipment: ₦500,000 to ₦700,000. This includes a water treatment system, a sachet sealing machine, storage tanks, and a borehole or a connection to a clean water source. Prices vary, so get quotes from at least three suppliers before you buy.

Borehole or water source: ₦200,000 to ₦400,000 if you need to drill a borehole. A good borehole is an investment — it gives you a steady supply of water that you control. If your area already has a reliable borehole, you may pay for water by arrangement instead, but confirm the quality and the supply before you commit.

Registration and approval: ₦100,000 to ₦200,000. This covers business registration, NAFDAC registration, and laboratory tests on your water. Budget for this from the start. It is not an optional extra.

Packaging and first stock: ₦100,000 to ₦150,000. Sachet rolls, printed bags, and cartons, plus fuel and power for your first weeks of production.

Rent and setup: ₦100,000 to ₦200,000 for a small factory space, depending on your area. You need a clean, tiled space with good drainage, not a cramped room with a leaking roof.

If your budget is smaller, consider starting as a distributor first. Buy pure water bags from an existing registered factory at wholesale price and sell them to shops and homes in your area. You learn the market, build customers, and save towards your own factory without the full setup cost.

## Understanding NAFDAC Registration

Pure water is regulated by the National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control, known as NAFDAC. Selling unregistered sachet water is illegal, and enforcement officers do inspect factories and markets. Registration protects your customers and your business, and many shops and offices will only buy from a registered producer.

The process starts with registering your business name, then applying to NAFDAC for a factory inspection. NAFDAC will check your premises, your water source, your treatment system, and your hygiene practices. Your water must be tested in a laboratory and meet the required standard for drinking water. You will also need to submit samples of your sachet packaging for approval.

The process takes time — often several weeks or months — so start it early, before you buy all your machines. Talk to other pure water producers in your state, or visit the nearest NAFDAC office, to get the current list of requirements and fees. Requirements can change, so confirm them directly rather than relying on old information from another vendor.

Keep your registration documents safe and display your NAFDAC number on your sachets as required. Renew your registration when it expires. A lapsed registration can shut your factory down just as quickly as never having one.

## Setting Up Your Factory

Choose a location with a reliable water source, good road access for distribution, and enough space for production and storage. A factory on a street that floods in the rainy season, or far from your customers, will cost you money every day.

Your factory needs clean, tiled floors and walls that can be washed, good ventilation, and a layout that separates the water treatment area from the packaging and storage areas. Workers should have clean overalls, hair covers, and hand-washing facilities. These are not luxuries — they are part of what NAFDAC inspects, and they are what keep your water safe.

Your water treatment system is the most important equipment you will buy. It usually includes filtration, treatment to remove impurities, and disinfection. Buy from a reputable supplier who will install the system, train your workers, and provide spare parts. A cheap system that fails after a month is more expensive than a good system that lasts for years.

You also need a reliable power supply. Production stops when there is no light, unless you have a generator. Budget for fuel, and consider a small solar setup for lighting and basic equipment if it makes sense for your factory. Many small factories lose production days to power problems — plan for this before you open.

## The Production Process, Step by Step

### Step 1: Source and store your water

Your water comes from a borehole or a tested municipal supply. Store it in clean, covered tanks. Never use water from an untested source, and never mix treated water with untreated water.

### Step 2: Treat the water

Run the water through your treatment system — filtration to remove particles, then treatment and disinfection as your system requires. Follow the manufacturer's instructions exactly. Treatment is not a step you can rush or skip when you are in a hurry to meet an order.

### Step 3: Test regularly

Test your treated water regularly, not just once at registration. Keep a record of your tests. If a test shows a problem, stop production, find the cause, fix it, and test again before you resume. Selling water you know is unsafe is never worth the money.

### Step 4: Fill and seal the sachets

Fill the sachets on your sealing machine, check that each seal is complete, and pack the sachets into bags. A bag usually contains 20 sachets. Count carefully — a bag with 19 sachets instead of 20 will be noticed by your customers and your distributors.

### Step 5: Store and distribute quickly

Store finished bags in a clean, cool area, off the floor. Deliver them promptly, especially in hot weather. Customers want cold water, so many vendors keep bags in a freezer or a cold room before delivery. A bag that arrives warm on a hot day is a lost sale.

## Pricing and Worked Profit Example

Pure water margins are small per bag, so volume and cost control decide your profit. Here is a worked example using realistic figures — check the current prices in your area before you set your own:

Suppose your total cost to produce one bag of 20 sachets is ₦250, including water treatment, sachet material, power, fuel, labour, and packaging. You sell the bag to distributors or shops for ₦350. Your profit is ₦100 per bag. Sell 100 bags a day and your daily profit is ₦10,000. Over 26 working days, that is ₦260,000 a month before rent and other fixed costs.

If you sell directly to homes and offices at ₦400 a bag, your profit is ₦150 per bag, but direct sales take more time and delivery cost. Many factories combine both: wholesale to shops for volume, and direct sales to homes and offices for a better margin.

Your biggest costs are usually power, fuel, sachet material, and labour. Track each one weekly. If your sachet supplier raises prices, your cost per bag rises, and your selling price may need to rise too. If your generator uses too much fuel, fix it or find a cheaper power option. Small savings per bag add up when you produce hundreds of bags a day.

Do not compete by cutting your price below your cost. A factory that sells at a loss to win customers will close within months. Compete on clean water, reliable delivery, and good service instead.

## Finding Customers and Distributors

Your first customers are the shops, kiosks, and provision stores near your factory. Visit them in person with a sample bag and your price list. Offer a fair wholesale price and reliable delivery, and deliver on time every time. A shop owner who runs out of water because you did not deliver will buy from your competitor the next day.

Homes and offices are your second market. Many families buy a set number of bags each week. Offer a weekly delivery arrangement: they pay in advance or on delivery, and you deliver on agreed days. Offices, schools, and churches often buy in larger quantities and pay monthly — agree the terms in writing so there is no argument later.

Events are a third market. Weddings, burials, and parties need large quantities of water, often at short notice. Let event planners in your area know you can supply quickly. Keep extra stock during weekends and festive periods, when demand rises.

Build a small team of distributors if your area is large. A distributor buys bags from you at wholesale price and sells them in their own street or estate. They extend your reach without you hiring more staff. Choose distributors who pay promptly and treat your water well — a distributor who stores bags in the sun damages your brand as well as theirs.

## Choosing a Brand Name and Sachet Design

Your brand name and sachet design are how customers recognise your water on a shop shelf. Choose a short, easy name that people can remember and pronounce, and check that no other factory in your area uses the same name. A name tied to your street or estate can help local customers remember you, but avoid a name that limits you if you later expand to another area.

Your sachet printing should show your brand name clearly, your NAFDAC number, your factory address, and a phone number for orders and complaints. Use clear, readable text and a clean design — customers trust water that looks professional. Ask your packaging supplier for a sample print before you order a large quantity, and check the spelling of every word on the sample. A printing error on thousands of sachets is an expensive mistake.

Keep your design consistent. Do not change your colours or your bag design every month. Consistency helps shop owners and customers spot your product quickly, and it makes your brand look established even when your factory is still small.

## Delivery, Storage, and Daily Operations

Delivery is half the business. A factory that produces good water but delivers late will lose customers to a factory with average water and reliable delivery. Plan your delivery routes by area, so one trip serves several customers in the same street. Use a tricycle, a van, or a motorcycle with a carrier, depending on your volume and your roads.

Keep your delivery vehicle clean. Bags of water carried in a dirty vehicle arrive looking dirty, and customers judge the water by the bag. Cover bags from sun and dust during transport.

Start production early in the morning so water is ready for the day's deliveries. Keep a simple daily record: how many bags you produced, how many you sold, how many are left, and how much money came in. This record tells you whether you are growing, and it helps you spot waste or theft quickly.

Hire carefully. You may need one or two workers for production and one for delivery. Train them on hygiene, on the production steps, and on how to talk to customers. Pay them fairly and on time. A worker who is unhappy or untrained can damage your machines, your water quality, and your reputation in a single day.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

Skipping registration is the biggest mistake. An unregistered factory can be shut down, fined, or have its stock seized. Register first, produce second.

Using an untested water source is the second. Borehole water is not automatically safe. Test it, treat it, and test it again. Your customers trust you with their health.

Buying the cheapest machines is the third. A cheap sealing machine that leaks, or a treatment system with no spare parts, will stop your production at the worst time. Buy from a supplier who offers installation, training, and after-sales support.

Producing more than you can sell is the fourth. Sachet water does not keep forever, and storage space is limited. Produce to your orders and your regular customers' needs, with a small extra for walk-in sales. Overproduction becomes waste.

Ignoring your costs is the fifth. Power, fuel, sachet material, and labour all change in price. Review your cost per bag every month. If your cost rises and your price does not, your profit disappears quietly.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does it cost to start a pure water business in Nigeria?

A small factory costs about ₦800,000 to ₦2,000,000, including machines, a borehole or water source, registration, packaging, and rent. Starting as a distributor for an existing factory costs far less, often ₦50,000 to ₦150,000 for initial stock and a delivery arrangement.

### Do I need NAFDAC registration to sell pure water?

Yes. Pure water is a regulated food product, and NAFDAC registration is required. Selling unregistered sachet water is illegal, and shops and offices will often refuse to buy from an unregistered producer. Start the registration process early, as it can take weeks or months.

### How many bags of pure water can a small factory produce in a day?

It depends on your machines and your workers. A small factory with one sealing machine can produce 100 to 300 bags a day. Your real limit is often your customers and your delivery capacity, not your machines. Build customers first, then increase production.

### How do I keep my water clean and safe?

Use a tested water source, run it through a proper treatment system, test your treated water regularly, keep your factory clean, and train your workers on hygiene. Never skip treatment to save time or money, and stop production if a test shows a problem.

## Final Word

A pure water business can provide steady income for years, but only if you treat it as a food business, not just a trading business. Register properly, test your water, keep your factory clean, deliver on time, and price for a real profit. Start as a distributor if the full factory cost is beyond you today, learn the market, and build towards your own production. Customers will come back to the factory whose water tastes clean, whose bags are well sealed, and whose delivery never fails. Be that factory, and your neighbourhood will keep you in business.
