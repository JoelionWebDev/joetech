Chiamaka sells jollof rice from a small shop in Surulere. Blessing sews two-piece Ankara sets in Lekki. Zainab mixes body butter in her kitchen in Abuja. Emeka sells power banks from a stall in Ikeja Computer Village. Four different trades, four different customers, four different kinds of headache — and one pricing method they can all use.

That method is cost-plus. You add up everything one unit costs you, then add a percentage on top as your profit. The price is the total. It sounds almost too plain to work, and that is exactly why it works. It stops you pricing from feeling, from what the woman in the next stall charges, or from what a customer says the thing "should" cost.

The catch is that the percentage is not the same for everyone. A food vendor who sells forty plates before lunch cannot use the same markup as a fashion seller who sells eight sets in a whole month. This article follows the four traders above through their own numbers, so you can see how the same method lands in four different places — and pick the markup that fits your trade.

## The Method in One Paragraph

Take one unit of what you sell. Write down every naira it costs you to get that unit into a customer's hands: materials or supplier price, transport, packaging, delivery, payment charges, data and airtime, market levies, and a fair share of rent and fuel. Add them up. That is your true cost. Then choose a markup — a percentage of that cost — and add it. The result is your selling price, rounded to a figure customers can remember.

If the true cost is ₦5,000 and your markup is 60 percent, you add ₦3,000 and sell at ₦8,000. The markup is your profit per unit before the fixed bills you have not already folded into the cost. The only judgement in the whole exercise is the percentage, and the four traders below show how that judgement is made.

## The Food Seller: Chiamaka's Jollof Plate in Surulere

Chiamaka's best seller is a plate of smoky party jollof with a piece of chicken. She sells it from a shop off Adeniran Ogunsanya Street, mostly to office workers at lunch and families ordering for the weekend. Food spoils, so she cannot carry stock from Monday to Friday and hope. She needs a price that pays her on every plate, because a plate that goes unsold at 4 p.m. is money in the bin.

Her cost for one plate breaks down like this. Rice, pepper, tomatoes, oil, seasoning and one piece of chicken come to ₦2,150. Gas for cooking works out at ₦250 a plate. The takeaway pack and cutlery cost ₦350. Transport — her share of the morning run to Mile 12 market, spread across the day's plates — is ₦150. Market levies and the data she burns answering WhatsApp orders add ₦100. Her share of shop rent and generator fuel, divided by the plates she sells in a month, is ₦200 a plate. The true cost of one plate is ₦3,200.

Chiamaka uses a 40 percent markup. Forty percent of ₦3,200 is ₦1,280, which takes the price to ₦4,480. She rounds it to ₦4,500, a figure her regulars can say without thinking. At ₦4,500 her profit is ₦1,300 a plate.

That profit only holds if she counts everything. Last year she priced the same plate at ₦3,500 because that was what the vendor two streets away charged. She was busy every lunch hour and broke by month end, because ₦3,500 barely covered the ingredients once the pack, the gas and the rider were counted. She sold more plates at ₦3,500 than she does at ₦4,500, and kept less money. The cheaper price was the expensive mistake.

## The Fashion Seller: Blessing's Two-Piece Set in Lekki

Blessing makes ready-to-wear Ankara two-piece sets from a workroom in Lekki Phase 1. Her customers find her on Instagram, send their measurements, and wait a week. Nothing about her trade is fast, and that shapes her price more than anything else.

One set costs her ₦19,000 to make and deliver. The fabric is ₦9,000. Lining, thread, zips and buttons come to ₦2,500. She pays an assistant tailor ₦5,000 per set for the sewing. Steaming and finishing cost ₦800. The branded paper bag and a thank-you card cost ₦700. Transport to the fabric market at Balogun is ₦500 a set. Data and airtime for customer chats are ₦300, and her share of market dues is ₦200.

Blessing uses a 50 percent markup. Fifty percent of ₦19,000 is ₦9,500, so the price is ₦28,500. She leaves it there rather than rounding to ₦29,000, because her customers already pay by transfer and the exact figure looks considered rather than guessed.

Her markup is higher than Chiamaka's for reasons you can touch. She sells about eight sets in a good month, not eight in a day, so each sale has to carry more profit. Her work is skilled labour — a badly sewn set comes back, and a return costs her the fabric, the labour and the delivery. If she used Chiamaka's 40 percent, her price would be ₦26,600 and her profit ₦7,600 a set. Over eight sets that is ₦15,200 less in a month, roughly what she spends on data and transport. The extra ten points of markup are not greed. They are the difference between a workroom that pays its way and one that slowly eats her savings.

## The Cosmetics Seller: Zainab's Body Butter in Abuja

Zainab mixes whipped body butter in her kitchen in Gwarinpa, Abuja, and sells it on Instagram and at a weekend pop-up stall. A jar looks simple. The costing is not.

One 250 ml jar costs her ₦5,000 all in. Shea butter, oils and fragrance come to ₦2,600. The jar and the printed label cost ₦800. The gift box she packs it in is ₦350. Delivery, averaged across her orders, is ₦500 a jar. Payment charges are ₦150. Data for customer chats is ₦150. Her share of the pop-up stall fee, spread across the jars she sells in a month, is ₦450.

Zainab uses a 60 percent markup, the highest of the four. Sixty percent of ₦5,000 is ₦3,000, so her price is ₦8,000 a jar. Her customers pay it without haggling, and the reason is worth understanding: they are not buying shea butter, which they could buy raw in the market. They are buying a tested recipe, a clean jar, a label they can trust, and a seller who answers when the cream arrives melted and tells them how to fix it. The markup pays for the testing — the three batches she threw away before the texture held — and for the slow weeks when the stall is quiet.

## The Electronics Seller: Emeka's Power Bank in Computer Village

Emeka's stall in Ikeja Computer Village sells 20,000 mAh power banks. His customers compare prices on three stalls before they buy, and they will walk twenty steps to save ₦500. His markup is the thinnest of the four, and he knows exactly why.

One power bank costs him ₦17,000 to sell. He buys it from his supplier for ₦14,500. Transport from the supplier is ₦500. His share of shop rent works out at ₦800 per unit. Packaging is ₦300. He sets aside ₦700 per unit as a faults reserve, because about one power bank in twenty comes back with a swollen battery or a dead port, and he replaces it without a fight. POS charges and data come to ₦200.

Emeka uses a 30 percent markup. Thirty percent of ₦17,000 is ₦5,100, which gives ₦22,100. He rounds it down to ₦22,000 — a clean figure that sits just under the ₦22,500 his neighbour charges for the same capacity. His profit is ₦5,000 a unit.

Thirty percent looks small next to Zainab's sixty, but Emeka sells five or six power banks on a busy day, and he sells chargers, cables and earphones alongside them. Volume does the work that markup does for Blessing. The faults reserve is the line most gadget sellers skip, and it is the one that sinks them: without it, his true cost would look like ₦16,300, and the first faulty unit he replaced would eat the profit on four good sales.

## The Same Method, Four Different Answers

Put the four traders side by side and the pattern is plain.

| Trader | Trade | True cost | Markup | Selling price | Profit per unit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Chiamaka | Jollof plate, Surulere | ₦3,200 | 40% | ₦4,500 | ₦1,300 |
| Blessing | Ankara two-piece, Lekki | ₦19,000 | 50% | ₦28,500 | ₦9,500 |
| Zainab | Body butter jar, Abuja | ₦5,000 | 60% | ₦8,000 | ₦3,000 |
| Emeka | Power bank, Ikeja | ₦17,000 | 30% | ₦22,000 | ₦5,000 |

Nobody in that table is wrong, and nobody copied anyone else. Chiamaka's food spoils and sells fast, so a middle markup on high volume works. Blessing's sets sell slowly and carry skilled labour, so her markup has to be higher. Zainab's jars carry testing, spoilage and refunds, so hers is highest. Emeka's power banks sell in a market where customers compare prices stall by stall, so his markup is thinnest and volume carries him.

Three things decide your own markup. First, how fast the product moves: slow sellers need a bigger cut per sale. Second, how much can go wrong: perishable stock, breakages and returns all push the markup up. Third, how much of the price is your skill rather than the material: a customer paying for your hands and your judgement will accept a higher markup than one buying a sealed box they could get anywhere.

## When a Cost Moves, Move the Price

Cost-plus only protects you if you redo the maths when costs move. Chiamaka learned this in a week when tomatoes doubled at Mile 12 and her ingredient cost rose from ₦2,150 to ₦2,550 a plate. Her true cost became ₦3,600. At her old price of ₦4,500 her profit fell to ₦900 a plate — a third of it gone without her changing anything. At 40 percent on the new cost, the price should be ₦5,040, which she rounded to ₦5,000. She told her regulars on the Monday, before they ordered, and most of them stayed, because a ₦500 rise with a reason beats a silent shrink in the portion.

Do the same check every month, or the week a supplier raises a price. Write down your five best sellers, redo each true cost, and see whether the price still holds. If a cost has risen and your price has not, you are paying the difference yourself.

## Starting Tonight

Pick one product — your best seller, the one you already know the numbers for. Write down every cost on a page, in the open, where you can see it. Add the lines you usually skip: the delivery, the pack, the data, the levy, the share of rent. Add them up. Choose a markup and say why: fast or slow, risky or safe, your skill or a sealed box. Multiply, round to a clean figure, and check it against the market.

That page is your price. Not a feeling, not a neighbour's price, not a number a customer talked you down to. Yours, worked out, and defensible the next time someone asks why it costs what it costs.
