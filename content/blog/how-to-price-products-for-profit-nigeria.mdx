Pricing is where many Nigerian small businesses quietly lose money. You sell every day, your WhatsApp is busy, customers praise your products, yet at the end of the month there is little left in your account. The problem is rarely that you do not sell enough. It is usually that your prices do not cover your real costs, or that your profit on each sale is too small to sustain the business.

This happens to traders in Balogun Market, fashion designers in Lekki, food vendors in Ikeja, skincare sellers on Instagram, and online stores shipping across Nigeria. They copy a competitor's price, add a small amount on top of what they paid a supplier, or set a price that "feels right", without ever doing the maths. A price that feels right can still leave you working for free.

Good pricing is not guesswork. It is a simple calculation, plus an understanding of what your customers value and what your competitors charge. This guide walks you through it step by step, with naira examples you can apply to your own business today.

## Why Your Price Decides Whether You Survive

Your price is the only part of your business that directly creates profit. Everything else — your products, your packaging, your adverts, your delivery — costs money. Only the price brings money in. If the price is wrong, even a busy business will struggle.

Consider two sellers of the same Ankara fabric in Lagos. Ada buys a yard for ₦4,000, pays ₦500 for transport and market levies, spends ₦300 on packaging and airtime for customer calls, and sells at ₦5,500. Her profit is ₦700 per yard. Bisi buys at the same price, has the same costs, but sells at ₦6,500 because her shop is well presented, her pictures are clear, and she delivers reliably. Her profit is ₦1,700 per yard — more than double Ada's — for the same product and the same work. Over 100 yards a month, Bisi earns ₦100,000 more. That difference is pricing, not luck.

A price that is too low also attracts the wrong customers. People who buy only because you are the cheapest will leave the moment someone else is cheaper. They will also haggle over every naira, complain the most, and expect the most. A fair price, clearly explained, attracts customers who value quality and service, and those customers stay longer and buy more.

A price that is too high is equally dangerous. Customers in Nigeria compare prices quickly, especially on WhatsApp and Instagram, where three sellers are only a tap away. If your price is far above the market without a clear reason, customers will scroll past you. The goal is not the highest price or the lowest price. It is the price that covers your costs, pays you fairly, and still feels fair to the customer.

## Know Your True Costs Before You Set Any Price

You cannot price for profit until you know what each product truly costs you. Many sellers only count what they paid the supplier. That is only the beginning.

Start with the direct cost, which is what you pay for the product itself. If you make the product, this is your raw materials: fabric, thread, and zips for a dress; shea butter, oils, and containers for a cream; flour, sugar, and eggs for a cake. If you buy and resell, it is the supplier's price plus any cost of getting the goods to you.

Then add the hidden costs that most sellers forget. Transport to the market or from the supplier, delivery bike or dispatch rider fees, packaging bags and boxes, airtime and data for customer chats, bank transfer charges, and the commission taken by Paystack or Flutterwave when a customer pays online. A ₦200 transfer charge or a 1.5 percent payment fee may look small on one sale, but across 200 sales a month it is ₦40,000 or more.

Then add your running costs, shared across everything you sell. Your shop rent, your generator fuel or electricity bill, your staff salary if you have one, your phone, your internet subscription, and the money you spend on adverts. If your monthly running costs are ₦150,000 and you sell 300 items a month, each item must carry ₦500 of those costs, or you are paying them from your own pocket.

Finally, add your own pay. This is the cost most Nigerian business owners skip. If you work in the business full time, your time has a value. Decide on a monthly salary for yourself — even a modest ₦100,000 — and build it into your prices. A business that cannot pay its owner is not a business; it is a hobby that eats your savings.

Write all of this down for your best-selling product. Add the direct cost, the hidden costs, the share of running costs, and your pay. That total is your true cost. Any price below it is a loss, no matter how busy you look.

## Four Pricing Methods That Work in Nigeria

Once you know your true cost, choose a method to set the price. These four work well for Nigerian small businesses, and you can combine them.

Cost-plus pricing is the simplest. You take your true cost and add a percentage as profit. If a handmade bag costs you ₦8,000 in total and you add 40 percent, the price is ₦11,200. Many sellers in Nigeria use a markup of 30 to 50 percent on fashion, beauty, and household goods, and 20 to 30 percent on food and fast-moving items where customers buy often. The advantage is that you always know your profit. The risk is that you ignore what customers are willing to pay, so check the market too.

Competitor-based pricing means looking at what similar sellers charge and positioning yourself deliberately. Visit three competitors on Instagram or in your market, note their prices for a similar product, and decide where you belong. If your quality and service are better, you can charge slightly more — but you must show the customer why. If you are new and unknown, charging the same as the market leader is safer than undercutting them by half, because a price that is too low makes customers suspect the quality.

Value-based pricing is the most profitable method, and it suits businesses with a clear benefit. A wedding cake, a bridal dress, a corporate gift hamper, or a custom website is not bought for its materials alone. The customer pays for the result: a beautiful wedding, a confident bride, a client impressed by a gift. If your product saves the customer time, solves a stressful problem, or creates a special moment, you can charge for that value, not just for the materials. Ask yourself what the product is worth to the customer, not only what it cost you.

Bundle pricing works well for sellers with several related products. Instead of selling one item at ₦5,000, sell three related items for ₦13,500, or a set for ₦12,000 instead of ₦15,000 bought separately. The customer feels they saved money, and you sell more in one order, which reduces your delivery and packaging cost per item. Skincare sellers do this with a full routine set, food vendors with a party pack, and fashion sellers with a matching set.

Most successful small businesses in Nigeria use a mix: cost-plus to make sure they never lose money, competitor prices to stay realistic, and value or bundles to earn more on their best products.

## The Pricing Mistakes That Eat Your Profit

Even sellers who know their costs make mistakes that quietly drain profit. Watch for these five.

The first is forgetting delivery. Many sellers quote a product price, then discover the dispatch rider charges ₦3,000 to Lekki or ₦5,000 to Abuja, and they absorb the cost to avoid losing the customer. Decide your delivery policy in advance. Either charge delivery separately and state it clearly, or build an average delivery cost into your price and offer free delivery within Lagos only. Never let delivery be a surprise that comes out of your profit.

The second is the endless discount. A discount that runs every week is not a promotion; it is your real price. Customers learn to wait for the next sale and never buy at full price. Use discounts rarely, for a clear reason — a launch, a festive season, clearing old stock — and set a firm end date. A 10 percent discount for three days creates urgency. A permanent 30 percent discount just trains customers to undervalue you.

The third is copying a big competitor. A large store in Ikeja Computer Village or a big online retailer can afford a low price because they buy in bulk, get supplier discounts, and sell in huge volumes. You cannot match their price and survive on their margins. Compete on service, quality, speed, or a niche they ignore, and charge a price that keeps you alive.

The fourth is charging the same price everywhere. A customer in Lagos and a customer in Kano do not cost you the same to serve. Delivery, packaging, and payment costs differ. It is fair to have a Lagos price and a different price for inter-state delivery that includes the extra cost. State this clearly so customers understand it is about delivery, not about cheating them.

The fifth is never reviewing your prices. The naira changes, fuel prices rise, suppliers increase their prices, and your rent goes up. A price that was profitable in January may be a loss by June. Review your prices every three months. Check your supplier prices, your transport costs, and your monthly profit. If your costs have risen by 15 percent and your prices have not moved, you are already losing money.

## How Customers Judge a Price in Nigeria

Customers do not judge a price in isolation. They judge it against what they expect, what they have paid before, and what they see around them. Understanding this helps you set prices that feel fair, even when they are not the cheapest.

The first price a customer sees becomes their anchor. If your first product is a small item at ₦3,000, a second item at ₦12,000 feels expensive. If your first product is a premium set at ₦25,000, that same ₦12,000 item feels reasonable. This is why many sellers show their premium product first, then a mid-range option. The mid-range option is usually the one they want to sell, and it looks like good value next to the premium one.

Customers also trust prices that are explained. "This dress is ₦18,000 because the fabric is original Ankara, the lining is quality cotton, and it is sewn to your exact measurements" is far more persuasive than just "₦18,000". When you explain what goes into the price — the materials, the time, the guarantee, the delivery — customers stop comparing you only on price and start comparing value. A short note under your product, or a voice note on WhatsApp, can do this job.

## How to Raise Your Prices Without Losing Customers

Every business owner dreads raising prices, but costs rise, and a business that never raises its prices slowly dies. The trick is to raise them in a way customers accept.

First, raise prices for new customers before existing ones. New customers have no old price to compare with, so they accept the new price as normal. Give your loyal customers a short notice period — "from 1 December, this product will be ₦12,000 instead of ₦10,000, but for you, as a regular customer, the old price applies until the end of November." That message shows respect, gives them time, and still moves your price up.

Second, raise prices in small steps rather than one big jump. A rise from ₦10,000 to ₦11,000 is barely noticed. A jump from ₦10,000 to ₦15,000 feels like a shock. If you need a large increase, do it in two steps over two or three months, and add a small improvement at the same time — better packaging, faster delivery, a free extra — so the customer feels they are getting more, not just paying more.

Third, improve something visible when you raise the price. A new, neater package, a thank-you card, a faster delivery promise, or a clearer product photo all give the customer a reason to accept the new price. The improvement does not have to cost much; it has to be visible.

Fourth, announce the increase with honesty and a date. A short WhatsApp broadcast or Instagram post works well: "Due to rising costs of materials and delivery, our prices will change from 1 December. Thank you for your support." Customers respect honesty. They resent discovering a new price only when they are about to pay.



## A Simple Pricing Formula You Can Use Today

Here is a practical formula you can apply to any product in your business, using real naira figures.

Step one: write down the true cost of one unit. For a skincare cream, suppose the ingredients and container cost ₦3,500, packaging costs ₦400, delivery within Lagos averages ₦1,000 per order, payment charges are ₦150, and your share of monthly running costs and your own pay comes to ₦1,000 per unit. Your true cost is ₦6,050.

Step two: decide your profit margin. For a product like this, a 40 percent margin on cost is reasonable. Forty percent of ₦6,050 is ₦2,420. Add it to the true cost, and your price is ₦8,470. Round it to a clean figure your customers will remember: ₦8,500.

Step three: check the market. Search for similar creams on Instagram and in your area. If most sell between ₦7,000 and ₦10,000, your ₦8,500 sits comfortably in the middle. If most sell at ₦6,000, you need to explain why yours is worth more — better ingredients, a bigger size, a guarantee — or accept a lower margin and sell more units.

Step four: test the price for two weeks. Sell at ₦8,500 and watch what happens. Do customers buy without haggling? Do they ask for discounts? Do they buy again? If sales are strong and few people complain, your price is right, or you could even try slightly higher. If almost nobody buys, the problem may be the price, or it may be your photos, your description, or your delivery promise. Change one thing at a time so you know what made the difference.

Step five: review every three months. Write down your costs again, because supplier prices change. If your true cost has risen to ₦6,800, your price at the same margin should be around ₦9,500. Adjust it, announce it with a date, and keep selling.

This formula takes less than an hour to apply to your top five products, and it can be the difference between a business that survives and one that quietly runs out of money.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a good profit margin for a small business in Nigeria?

It depends on what you sell. Fashion, beauty, and handmade goods often aim for 30 to 50 percent on top of true cost. Food and fast-moving goods often work on 20 to 30 percent, because customers buy them often. Services can be higher, because the main cost is your time and skill. The right margin is one that covers all your costs, pays you fairly, and still lets you sell at a price customers accept.

### Should I charge less than my competitors to get customers?

Not as a long-term strategy. A lower price may bring a few quick sales, but it attracts customers who will leave for the next cheaper seller, and it leaves you with too little profit to grow. Compete on quality, service, speed, or a niche instead, and keep your price fair.



### How often should I review my prices?

Review your prices every three months, and immediately if a major cost changes — for example, if your supplier raises prices, fuel costs jump, or your rent increases. A price that was profitable three months ago may already be losing money today.

## What to Do Next

1. Pick your five best-selling products and calculate the true cost of each one, including transport, packaging, payment charges, running costs, and your own pay.

3. Write your prices down in one list, use the same price on WhatsApp, Instagram, and your website, and set a date in your calendar three months from now to review them all again.
