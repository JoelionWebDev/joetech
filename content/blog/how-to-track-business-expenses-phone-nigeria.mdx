Ask a market trader in Mile 12 how much she spent last week and she will laugh. Not because the question is funny, but because the money moved in a hundred small pieces and none of it was written down: ₦2,000 for transport, ₦15,000 for data, ₦8,500 for packaging bags, ₦3,000 for a rider, ₦25,000 for stock from a supplier who only takes transfer. By month end, the profit that should be sitting in her account has quietly disappeared into expenses nobody tracked.

Every guide she finds tells her to open a spreadsheet. She does not have a laptop. She does not want a spreadsheet. She has a phone, a busy stall, and ten minutes at the end of the day. This guide is built for her: a tracking system that runs entirely on a phone, with no spreadsheet, no accounting app, and no laptop. If you can take a photo and record a voice note, you can run it.

## The photo-receipt habit

Most expenses come with something you can photograph: a receipt, a supplier's note, a transfer confirmation screen, a rider's waybill. The habit is simple. Every time money leaves your business, take a photo of the proof before you put your phone away. It takes five seconds.

Keep those photos in one place. Create a photo album on your phone called "Business Money" and put every receipt photo in it. On WhatsApp, you can also send the photo to yourself — open your own chat, or create a group with only yourself in it, and send every receipt photo there. Either way, the photos are dated automatically, so you always know when each expense happened.

The trick is to photograph everything, including the expenses that have no receipt. Bought sachet water for the shop? Photograph the shop, or the water, or just write the amount on a scrap of paper and photograph that. Paid ₦1,500 to a rider in cash? Photograph the rider's bike, or the parcel, or write "Rider to Ikeja ₦1,500" on paper and photograph it. A photo of a note is still a record. A memory is not.

At the end of each week, scroll through the album. You will see the whole week in pictures, with dates attached. That weekly scroll is your expense report — no typing required.

## The two-minute voice note at closing time

Photos catch the expenses with proof. The voice note catches everything else. Every day, at closing time, record a voice note to yourself. Two minutes, no more. Say four things: how much came in today, how much went out, what the biggest expense was, and whether anything unusual happened.

It might sound like this: "Today is Saturday. Sales were about ₦85,000, mostly transfers. I spent ₦30,000 on new stock from Alhaja, ₦3,000 on transport, ₦2,000 on packaging bags, and ₦1,000 on pure water for the shop. The freezer made a strange noise today — I need to get someone to check it before it spoils the stock."

Send that voice note to your own WhatsApp chat, next to the receipt photos. Now you have a daily diary of your business, in your own voice, with dates attached, and you created it in two minutes while packing up.

Once a week, listen to the seven voice notes back to back. It takes fifteen minutes. You will hear patterns you never noticed: the transport cost that appears every single day, the packaging you buy twice a week, the small daily expense that adds up to ₦20,000 a month. Hearing your own voice say it makes it real in a way a number in a spreadsheet never does.

## Separate the money with separate wallets

No tracking method works if business money and personal money live in the same place. You do not need a corporate account. You need a second wallet, and your phone already has several: a Moniepoint account, an Opay account, a Kuda account, or a second savings account with your bank. Pick one and dedicate it to the business.

The rule is simple. All business income goes into the business wallet. All business spending comes out of it. Your personal spending — food, fuel, family — stays in your old wallet. If you need money from the business for personal use, transfer a fixed amount to yourself on a set day, for example every Friday, and say so in that day's voice note: "I took ₦20,000 for the house today."

When the business wallet runs low, you know the business is spending more than it earns. When it grows, you know the business is healthy. You do not need a spreadsheet to see it. You can see it in the balance every time you open the app.

## The Friday fifteen-minute cash count

Cash is where most small businesses lose track, because cash leaves no alert on your phone. If your business handles a lot of cash, add one weekly habit: every Friday, before you close, count the cash in your till or drawer and compare it with what your photos and voice notes say should be there.

Count the notes and coins. Add up the week's cash sales from your voice notes. Subtract the week's cash expenses. The two numbers should match, or come close. If ₦5,000 is missing, find out why that same day, while you still remember the week. Usually it is a forgotten expense — the ₦3,000 you paid a rider on Tuesday, the ₦2,000 for pure water on Wednesday. Find it, photograph the note, record a voice note about it, and move on. A missing ₦5,000 found on Friday is a lesson. A missing ₦5,000 discovered at month end is a mystery.

Put the counted cash in the business wallet the next morning. Do not carry days of cash around. Cash in a drawer is cash that can be spent without a record.

## The one-page month end

At the end of the month, sit down for thirty minutes with your phone. Scroll through the month's receipt photos and listen to a few of the voice notes. Then write one page in your notes app — just five lines:

- Total money in this month.
- Total money out this month.
- What is left.
- The three biggest costs.
- One thing to cut or change next month.

That page is your monthly account. It will not impress a bank, and it is not a substitute for proper books if you ever need a loan or a tax clearance. But it will tell you, honestly, whether your business made money this month or lost it — and that is the question that matters most, answered without a single spreadsheet.

## When to graduate to a spreadsheet

This method has a limit, and you should know it. It works beautifully up to a point: one person, one stall or shop, a few dozen transactions a day. It starts to break when you hire staff who handle money, when you have two locations, or when a bank or a grant programme asks for proper records. Those are good problems — they mean the business has grown.

When that day comes, you will not be starting from zero. You will have months of photos, voice notes and one-page summaries. Handing those to an accountant, or typing them into a spreadsheet, is a weekend's work, not a year's reconstruction. The phone method is not a replacement for real bookkeeping forever. It is the bridge that gets you from guessing to knowing — and knowing is what keeps a small business alive.
