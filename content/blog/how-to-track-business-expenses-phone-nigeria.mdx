Ask a Nigerian small business owner how much they spent last month and you will often get a shrug. Not because they are careless, but because the money moved in a hundred small pieces: ₦2,000 for transport to Mile 12 market, ₦15,000 for data, ₦8,500 for packaging bags, ₦3,000 for a rider, ₦25,000 for stock from a supplier who only takes transfer. None of it was written down. By month end, the profit that should be sitting in the account has quietly disappeared into expenses nobody tracked.

This is the single most common reason small businesses in Nigeria feel broke while selling well. The sales are real. The profit is real too — it is just leaking through untracked spending. The fix does not require an accountant, a laptop, or expensive software. Your phone is enough. This guide shows you a simple system you can start today, in ten minutes a day, and keep going for years.

## Why tracking expenses changes everything

When you track expenses, three things happen. First, you finally know your real profit. A fashion seller in Yaba who sells ₦400,000 worth of clothes in a month might be spending ₦320,000 on fabric, transport, tailors, data, rent share and packaging — leaving ₦80,000. That is useful knowledge. Without tracking, she might think she made ₦400,000 and wonder why her account is empty.

Second, you spot waste quickly. The ₦5,000 you spend every week on "urgent" deliveries adds up to ₦20,000 a month. The subscription you forgot about charges ₦3,900 every month. The supplier whose prices crept up by ₦500 per item is quietly eating your margin. Tracking makes these visible, and visible waste can be cut.

Third, you can make real decisions. Should you raise your price? Can you afford a shop? Is that Instagram advert worth it? Should you hire a rider or keep using dispatch? Every one of these questions is a maths question, and you cannot do the maths without expense records. A business that tracks expenses can grow on purpose. A business that does not can only guess.

## The golden rule: separate business money from personal money

Before any app or spreadsheet will help you, you need one separation: business money and personal money must live apart. If your business income lands in the same account you use for food, fuel and family expenses, no tracking system in the world will make sense of it.

You do not need a corporate account to start. Open a second personal savings account — most Nigerian banks let you open one from your phone in minutes, with no opening balance — and dedicate it to the business. All business income goes in. All business spending comes out. Your personal spending stays in your old account.

Then pay yourself a salary. Decide a fixed amount — say ₦60,000 a month — and transfer it from the business account to your personal account on the first of the month. That is your money. Everything left in the business account belongs to the business: stock, rent, data, transport, and growth. This one habit does more for small business finance than any app ever will, because it stops the daily habit of dipping into business money for personal needs and then wondering where the profit went.

If you take cash sometimes, keep a small cash float — say ₦20,000 in a labelled envelope or a separate wallet — and record every naira that leaves it. Cash is where most Nigerian small businesses lose track, because a ₦1,000 note spent at the market leaves no bank alert behind. The rule is simple: no receipt, no spending without a note. Write it down the moment it happens, or it will be forgotten by evening.

## Set up your phone system in 30 minutes

You need only three things on your phone: a notes app or spreadsheet app, your bank app, and your camera. That is the whole toolkit.

Start with a spreadsheet. Google Sheets is free, works on any Android or iPhone, backs up automatically, and opens on any other device. Create a new sheet and name it "Business Expenses 2026." Make six columns: Date, What I bought or paid for, Category, Amount (₦), Payment method, and Receipt or note. That is all. Do not build a complicated accounting system with twenty columns. A simple sheet you actually use beats a perfect sheet you abandon after three days.

Next, create a folder on your phone called "Business Receipts." Every time you pay for something — a market purchase, a dispatch rider, a data bundle, a POS charge — take a photo of the receipt, or screenshot the transfer confirmation, and save it in that folder. If there is no receipt, take a photo of the item or write a quick note. This folder is your proof. If a supplier disputes a payment, if you need to check what you paid last month, or if you ever need records for tax, the folder has your back.

Set up your bank alerts properly. Make sure transaction alerts are switched on for your business account, and that you can see them even when your data is off — SMS alerts still arrive without internet. Each alert is a free expense record. When you see "₦12,000 debited," you can check it against your sheet in seconds.

Finally, put a daily reminder on your phone for 8pm: "Record today's expenses." Ten minutes in the evening is all it takes, and the reminder is what turns a good intention into a habit.

## The categories that matter for a Nigerian business

Categories are what turn a long list of payments into information you can act on. Use no more than eight categories, and use the same ones every time. Here is a set that fits most Nigerian small businesses:

Stock and materials — everything you buy to sell or to make what you sell: fabric, ingredients, phone accessories, raw materials, goods from your supplier.

Transport and delivery — fuel, bus fare, keke, dispatch riders, waybill charges, courier fees. In Lagos, this category surprises almost everyone with how big it is.

Rent and utilities — shop rent, electricity bills, generator fuel, water, and your share of any shared space.

Data, airtime and internet — your monthly data subscription, airtime, and any internet bill. For an online business, this is a real cost of doing business, not a personal luxury.

Marketing and adverts — Instagram and Facebook ad spend, flyers, banners, influencer payments, and WhatsApp Business costs.

Packaging — nylon bags, boxes, branded stickers, thank-you cards, and tape. Small per item, large per month.

Salaries and helpers — wages for staff, your rider, your sales girl, or the person who helps you on market days.

Other and miscellaneous — everything that does not fit above, but keep this category small. If "other" becomes your biggest category, your categories are wrong.

Some businesses need one extra category of their own — a food vendor might add "gas and cooking fuel," a photographer might add "equipment." Add it if you need it, but resist the urge to create a new category for every unusual purchase. Eight categories is the limit that keeps the system simple enough to maintain.

## The ten-minute daily habit

The system only works if you feed it daily. Here is the routine.

During the day, every time you spend business money, take a photo of the receipt or screenshot the transfer alert. This takes five seconds and needs no typing. If you pay cash with no receipt, open your notes app and type one line: "2pm — ₦3,500 transport to Oshodi market." That is enough for now.

In the evening, when your 8pm reminder sounds, open your spreadsheet and enter the day's expenses. Date, what it was, category, amount, payment method, and a short note. Five expenses take about five minutes. While you are there, glance at the week's total. Is transport already at ₦18,000 by Wednesday? Did you spend ₦9,000 on data this week alone? Seeing the numbers every evening keeps spending honest in a way that a monthly review never can.

On Sunday evening, spend fifteen minutes on a weekly review. Add up each category for the week. Compare it with last week. Ask two questions: what was my biggest expense this week, and was it worth it? Then check your income for the week — your sales — and subtract your expenses. That number is your weekly profit, and it is the most important number in your business.

At the end of the month, do a one-hour review. Total each category for the month. Total your income. Subtract. That is your monthly profit. Write it down at the top of a new sheet tab for the month, with one line underneath: "One thing I will spend less on next month." Pick the easiest win — maybe the ₦12,000 a month on emergency dispatch rides that could be batched into two planned trips. Small cuts, repeated monthly, are how margins grow.

## Free apps that make it easier

A spreadsheet is enough, and it is what I recommend you start with, because it forces you to understand your own numbers. But if you want an app to do some of the work, several free options work well in Nigeria.

Google Sheets remains the best free choice for most people. It is free, it syncs, and you can share it with a partner or accountant. Make a simple template once, copy it each month, and you are set.

Wave is a free accounting app that handles invoicing and expense tracking, and it works on a phone browser. It is more powerful than a spreadsheet but takes longer to learn. Consider it once your business is doing steady volume and you want proper invoices and reports.

For cash-heavy businesses, a simple expense tracker app like Money Manager or Monefy can work. You tap a category, type the amount, and it is recorded. They are fast, which matters when you are at a busy market stall. Just make sure you export or back up the data regularly, and keep the categories matched to your spreadsheet so the two do not drift apart.

Some Nigerian fintech apps now include business expense features — a business account with built-in categorisation and statements. If your bank offers a free business account with expense tagging, use it. But do not pay for a premium accounting subscription until your business is earning enough that the cost is a small fraction of your profit. A ₦5,000-a-month subscription to track a business making ₦80,000 a month is a bad trade.

Whatever tool you choose, the rule is the same: the tool is not the system. You are the system. An app cannot record the ₦2,000 cash you paid a rider unless you tell it to.

## Tracking income too — the other half of the picture

Expenses are only half of profit. You also need to know what came in. Track income in the same spreadsheet, on a second tab called "Income," with columns for date, customer or order, item sold, amount received, and payment method. Record every sale: transfer, cash, POS, or payment link. At the end of each day, your income total should roughly match your bank alerts plus your cash float. If it does not, something is missing — a sale you forgot, or a transfer that went to the wrong account.

This income record does double duty. It tells you which products sell best, which days are strongest, and which customers buy most often. A hair vendor in Abuja discovered from her income sheet that 60 percent of her sales came from just two wig styles. She stopped stocking the slow styles, and her profit jumped without selling a single extra unit.

Reconcile weekly: income minus expenses equals profit. If the profit number does not match the change in your business account balance, find the gap before it grows. Usually the gap is a forgotten cash expense or a personal expense that slipped into the business account. Finding it weekly takes five minutes. Finding it at year end takes days.

## Common expenses Nigerian businesses forget to track

Some costs hide because they feel too small or too normal to count. Count them anyway.

Bank charges. Transfer fees of ₦10 or ₦25, SMS alert charges, POS charges, and account maintenance fees add up. A business doing fifty transfers a month can lose ₦2,000 to ₦3,000 in fees alone. Check your monthly bank statement and add the total to your sheet.

Airtime and data used for business calls and WhatsApp. If you spend ₦10,000 a month on data and most of it goes to customer chats, product photos and adverts, that is a business expense.

Transport to the market, to the supplier, to the customer, to the bank. Every trip counts. A trader who goes to the market three times a week at ₦1,500 per trip spends ₦18,000 a month on market runs alone.

Food and drinks bought while working — the bottle of water and the plate of food during a long market day. This one is borderline personal, but if you only eat out because you are out selling, a fair share of it is a business cost. Be honest and consistent rather than generous.

## Using your records to price better and grow

After two or three months of tracking, your records become a pricing tool. Add up everything it costs to deliver one unit of your product — the stock, the packaging, the delivery share, the data share, the market transport share. That is your true cost. Your selling price must sit comfortably above it, or you are working for free.

A baker in Enugu tracked her costs and discovered that each cake cost her ₦14,500 to make and deliver, while she was selling it for ₦15,000. She was earning ₦500 per cake for hours of work. She raised her price to ₦20,000, lost a few price-sensitive customers, and doubled her monthly profit. She could only make that decision because she had the numbers.

Your records also make you bankable. If you ever apply for a loan, a grant, or a supplier credit line, the first thing anyone asks for is records. A business with six months of clean income and expense sheets looks serious. A business with no records looks like a gamble. Your phone spreadsheet, backed up and consistent, is a real business asset.

And when tax season comes, your records save you stress. Nigeria's tax system for small businesses is far simpler when you can show what you earned and what you spent. Keep your records for at least a few years, backed up to Google Drive, and you will never have to reconstruct a year from memory.

## Mistakes to avoid

The most common mistake is tracking for one week and stopping. The system pays off in months two and three, not week one. Commit to ninety days before judging it.

The second mistake is mixing accounts again. One "small" personal withdrawal from the business account, unrecorded, breaks the reconciliation and teaches you that the separation does not matter. It does. Protect it.

The third mistake is making the system too complicated. Twenty categories, three apps, a daily report format nobody reads — complexity kills habits. Six columns, eight categories, ten minutes a day. That is the whole system. If it feels too heavy, simplify it until it feels easy, because an easy system you keep is worth more than a perfect system you quit.

The fourth mistake is recording expenses but never reviewing them. Data you never look at is just typing. The weekly and monthly reviews are where the value lives. Put them on your calendar and treat them like appointments with your most important client — your own business.

## Your first week: a simple start

Today, open the second bank account or designate the business account. Tonight, create the spreadsheet with the six columns and the eight categories. Tomorrow, photograph every receipt and record every expense in the evening. On Sunday, do your first weekly review, even if the numbers are small and incomplete. Next month, do your first full monthly review and write down your profit and your one spending cut for the following month.

Ninety days from now, you will know exactly what your business earns, exactly what it spends, and exactly where the leaks are. That knowledge is worth more than any loan, any advert, or any new product idea, because it turns your business from a hustle you hope is working into a business you know is working. And it all started with your phone, a spreadsheet, and ten honest minutes a day.
