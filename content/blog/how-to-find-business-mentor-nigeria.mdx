A young woman in Ibadan starts a hair business. She is good at styling, but she does not know how to price her work, how to keep customers, or when to rent a shop. She makes mistakes that cost her money — she buys too much stock, she undercharges, she trusts the wrong supplier. Two years later, she meets a woman who has run a successful salon for fifteen years. In one conversation, the older woman tells her how to price properly and how to handle difficult customers. That single conversation saves her more money than a whole year of trial and error.

That is what a mentor does. A mentor is someone who has already walked the road you are walking. They have made the mistakes, learned the lessons, and can help you avoid the worst ones. They do not run your business for you. They guide you, warn you, encourage you, and connect you to people who can help.

Many Nigerian small business owners want a mentor but do not know how to find one. They think mentors are only for rich people, or that a busy successful person will never have time for them. That is not true. Many successful Nigerian business owners are willing to guide a serious, respectful young person — especially one who listens and acts on advice. The challenge is finding the right person and approaching them the right way.

This guide explains how to find a business mentor in Nigeria, even with a small budget. You will learn what a mentor really does, where to look, how to approach someone without being a burden, what to ask, and how to keep the relationship strong.

## What a Mentor Actually Does — and Does Not Do

Before you look for a mentor, be clear about what you need. A mentor is not a sponsor who gives you money. A mentor is not an employee who does your work. A mentor is not a magician who fixes your business overnight.

A good mentor does four things. First, they share experience. They tell you what worked for them and what failed, so you can learn without paying the same price they paid. Second, they give honest feedback. They tell you when your prices are wrong, when your product needs work, or when you are wasting time on the wrong thing. Third, they open doors. They introduce you to suppliers, customers, or partners you could not reach on your own. Fourth, they encourage you when business is hard and keep you honest when business is going well.

A mentor does not make decisions for you. You still run your business. You still take the risks. The mentor helps you think clearly before you decide.

You do not need a famous mentor. You need someone a few steps ahead of you in your own field, or in a field close to yours. A tailor with three shops is a better mentor for a new tailor than a billionaire who has never sewn a dress. Look for real, practical experience, not fame.

## Step 1: Know What You Need Help With

People who ask for a mentor without knowing what they need usually waste the opportunity. "Please mentor me" is too vague. A busy person hears that and does not know where to start.

Before you approach anyone, write down your three biggest challenges right now. Are you struggling with pricing? With finding customers? With managing money? With hiring staff? With moving from online sales to a physical shop? Be specific.

Then write down what you want to achieve in the next six months. Do you want to double your sales? Open a shop in Lekki? Start supplying a supermarket? A clear goal helps a mentor know how to help you, and it shows them you are serious.

Also write down what you have already tried. A mentor respects someone who has tried and failed more than someone who has only complained. When you can say, "I tried selling on Instagram for six months and got only ten customers, and I do not know why," a mentor can give you useful advice. When you say nothing, they have nothing to work with.

## Step 2: Look in the Right Places

Mentors are all around you. You do not need to travel to Lagos or pay for an expensive programme to find one. Start close to home.

Look first at successful business owners in your own area and industry. The woman who runs the busiest provisions shop in your market, the man whose pharmacy has been open for twenty years, the caterer who supplies every wedding in your town — these people have knowledge that no book can teach you. Visit their shops. Watch how they work. Notice who treats customers well and keeps good records.

Look at your church, mosque, or community group. Many religious and community groups in Nigeria have business owners who are happy to guide younger members. Ask the leaders if they know someone in your line of business who might be willing to talk with you.

Look at professional associations and trade groups. Tailors, bakers, photographers, and many other trades have associations in Lagos, Abuja, and other cities. Join one. Attend meetings. The experienced members you meet there are natural mentors.

Look online, especially on LinkedIn. Search for people in your industry in Nigeria. Read their posts. Comment thoughtfully. Many experienced professionals share advice freely online, and a respectful conversation there can grow into a real relationship.

Finally, look at free and low-cost programmes. Organisations like the Tony Elumelu Foundation, Fate Foundation, and various state and federal enterprise programmes run training and mentorship for Nigerian entrepreneurs. Some are free. Check their websites and apply when applications open. Even if you do not get a personal mentor, the training and the network are valuable.

## Step 3: Approach Them With Respect and Clarity

The way you approach a potential mentor matters as much as who you choose. Busy people are approached by many people. Most of those people want money, a job, or a favour. You need to stand out by being respectful, specific, and easy to help.

Do not start with "Please be my mentor." That is a big request to make of a stranger. Start with a small, specific request instead. Ask for twenty minutes of their time. Ask one clear question. For example: "Good afternoon, Ma. I run a small bakery in Yaba, and I admire how you have grown your business. Please, may I have twenty minutes of your time to ask how you priced your products when you started? I would be grateful."

Introduce yourself properly. Say your name, your business, and what you do. Say why you chose them in particular. People like to know they were chosen for a reason, not at random.

Meet them where they are comfortable. Offer to visit their shop at a time that suits them. Offer to buy them lunch or a drink if they agree to meet. Do not demand a meeting at your own convenience. Do not send long voice notes to a stranger. Keep your first message short and polite.

If they say no, or they do not reply, accept it gracefully. Thank them and move on. Do not beg, and do not message them every day. A polite person who accepts no gracefully is remembered. A pushy person is blocked.

## Step 4: Make the First Meeting Count

If someone agrees to meet you, prepare well. This meeting decides whether they will ever meet you again.

Write down three questions before you go. Choose questions they can answer from experience, not questions you could answer with a Google search. Good questions sound like: "What was your biggest mistake in your first two years, and how did you recover?" or "How did you find your first big customer?" or "When did you know it was time to hire your first staff?" Bad questions sound like: "How do I make money?" — too vague to answer.

Bring a notebook and write down what they say. People take you more seriously when you write. It shows you value their words. It also helps you remember and act on the advice later.

Listen more than you talk. Do not spend the meeting talking about yourself. Ask, listen, and take notes. If they give you advice, do not argue. Even if you disagree, thank them and think about it later.

At the end, thank them warmly. Ask if you may come back in a month to tell them what you did with their advice. That question is powerful. It shows you plan to act, not just to collect advice. Most mentors are happy to meet someone who actually follows through.

## Step 5: Follow Through — This Is What Keeps Mentors

Here is the secret most people miss: mentors keep helping the people who act on their advice. They stop helping the people who ask the same questions every month and never change anything.

After your meeting, act on at least one piece of advice within one week. Then report back. Send a short message: "Good evening, Ma. Thank you for your advice on pricing. I increased my prices as you suggested, and I have not lost any customers. My profit this week is better. Thank you." That message will make your mentor smile. It tells them their time was not wasted.

Keep your mentor updated, but do not overwhelm them. One short message every two or three weeks is enough. Share a small win, ask a short question, or tell them about a problem you solved. Do not message them every day. Do not call them late at night unless it is urgent.

Respect their time. If they give you thirty minutes, do not take two hours. If they reply to your message after two days, do not complain. They are busy running their own business.

And always give back where you can. You may not have money, but you can help in other ways. You can recommend their business to your customers. You can help them with something you know how to do, like setting up their WhatsApp Business profile or taking photos of their products. Mentorship works best when both sides give, even if what you give is small.

## Step 6: Use Free and Low-Cost Mentorship Programmes

You do not have to find a mentor alone. Several programmes in Nigeria connect small business owners with experienced guides, often for free or at low cost.

The Tony Elumelu Foundation runs a well-known entrepreneurship programme with training and mentorship for African entrepreneurs. Applications open once a year. The Fate Foundation runs programmes for Nigerian entrepreneurs, including training and access to mentors. The Lagos State Employment Trust Fund and similar state programmes sometimes include business support and mentorship. The Bank of Industry and the Small and Medium Enterprises Development Agency of Nigeria also run training programmes that include guidance from experienced business owners.

Your local chamber of commerce, your trade association, and your alumni group can also connect you with mentors. Ask around. Many of these opportunities are not advertised widely. They spread by word of mouth, so the people who ask are the people who hear about them.

When you join a programme, take it seriously. Attend the sessions. Do the assignments. Ask questions. The mentors in these programmes notice the serious participants, and they often keep helping those people long after the programme ends.

Be careful with anyone who charges a large fee to be your mentor without proof of real business success. A genuine mentor earns money from their own business, not from selling mentorship. If someone demands a large payment before they will talk to you, walk away. There are honest mentors who will help you for free, or for the price of lunch.

## Step 7: Build a Circle, Not Just One Mentor

One mentor is good. A small circle of advisers is better. No single person knows everything. A tailor can teach you about fashion, but an accountant can teach you about money, and a digital marketer can teach you about selling online.

Build your circle slowly. Your mentor might be an experienced business owner. Your second adviser might be a friend who is good with numbers and helps you check your accounts. Your third might be someone in your trade association who knows suppliers. Together, they cover more ground than one person can.

Join a group of business owners at your level too. A peer group — three or four people running small businesses like yours — is a powerful thing. You can share suppliers, warn each other about bad customers, and encourage each other when sales are slow. Your peers understand your daily struggles in a way a big mentor may have forgotten.

Meet your circle regularly, even if it is only once a month over lunch or a WhatsApp call. Business is lonely in Nigeria when you try to do it alone. A good circle makes it less lonely and much easier.

## A Real Example: A Baker Finds a Mentor at Her Church

Consider a baker in Surulere who sells cakes from home. She is good at baking, but her prices are too low, and she often runs out of money for ingredients before the end of the month. At her church, she notices a woman who runs a successful catering company and supplies food for large events. After service one Sunday, she introduces herself, compliments the woman's work, and asks if she could have twenty minutes to ask about pricing.

The caterer agrees to meet her at her shop on a Tuesday morning. The baker arrives on time, with a notebook and three questions. The caterer tells her how she prices her food: she calculates the cost of ingredients, adds the cost of gas and transport, adds her labour, and then adds a profit margin. She tells the baker that her own prices were too low for years, and that raising them did not lose her customers — it made customers respect her work more.

The baker goes home and recalculates her prices. She raises them by a small amount and explains the quality of her ingredients to her customers. She messages the caterer two weeks later to say her profit has improved and she can now buy ingredients in bulk. The caterer is pleased and invites her to a catering association meeting. There, the baker meets suppliers who sell ingredients cheaper, and she meets other bakers who become her friends and advisers.

One twenty-minute meeting, followed by action, changed the course of her business. That is how mentorship works in real life. It is not a formal contract. It is a respectful request, a good conversation, and the discipline to act on what you heard.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

First, do not choose a mentor because they are rich or famous. Choose them because they have done what you want to do, and they are willing to teach.

Second, do not expect your mentor to fund your business. If they offer help with money, that is a bonus, not the point. The point is their knowledge.

Third, do not collect advice without acting on it. A notebook full of advice you never used is worth nothing.

Fourth, do not be a burden. Respect their time, their family, and their business. A mentor who feels used will quietly stop replying.

Fifth, do not depend on one person for everything. If your mentor is unavailable, keep working. Your business is yours, and you must be able to move without waiting for permission.

## Your First Month Plan

Here is a simple plan for your first month. In week one, write down your three biggest challenges and your goal for the next six months. In week two, list five people who could mentor you — from your area, your church or mosque, your trade association, or LinkedIn. In week three, approach two of them with a short, respectful message asking for twenty minutes. In week four, meet the one who replies, ask your three prepared questions, take notes, and act on one piece of advice within a week.

Finding a mentor is not about luck. It is about knowing what you need, looking in the right places, asking respectfully, and following through. The right mentor will not make your business successful for you. They will help you make fewer mistakes, move faster, and stay encouraged when things are hard. That is worth more than money. Start looking this week — your mentor may be closer than you think.
