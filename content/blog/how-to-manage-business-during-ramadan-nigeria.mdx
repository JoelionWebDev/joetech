Ramadan is one of the most important months in the Nigerian business calendar, and one of the most misunderstood by businesses that do not plan for it. For roughly a month each year, millions of Muslim customers in Lagos, Abuja, Kano, Kaduna, Ibadan, Ilorin, Maiduguri and Port Harcourt change how they live, how they eat, when they shop, and what they buy. They fast from dawn to sunset, they gather for Iftar in the evening, they wake early for Suhoor, they pray more, they give more, and they prepare for Eid at the end of the month. A business that understands that rhythm can serve its customers better, sell more, and build loyalty that lasts all year. A business that ignores it will wonder why sales dropped, why staff are tired, and why customers went elsewhere.

Managing a business during Ramadan is not about stopping work. It is about adjusting work to fit the month. Your opening hours, your stock, your marketing, your delivery times, and how you treat your staff all need small, thoughtful changes. Those changes do not require a large budget. They require planning, respect, and a willingness to see the month through your customers' eyes.

This guide is for every Nigerian small business owner, whether you are Muslim or not, whether your customers are mostly Muslim or only partly Muslim, and whether you sell food, fashion, groceries, skincare, electronics or services. It shows you how to plan before Ramadan begins, how to operate during the fasting weeks, how to prepare for the Eid rush, and how to look after your team so that everyone comes through the month stronger.

## Understand How Ramadan Changes Customer Behaviour

The first step is to understand what actually changes during Ramadan, because the changes are specific and predictable, and they affect different businesses in different ways.

During the day, while people are fasting, foot traffic in many markets and shops drops, especially in the hot afternoon hours. People are conserving energy, they are not browsing casually, and they are less likely to make impulse purchases. Food businesses see very low daytime sales, because customers are not eating or drinking. Restaurants that normally do well at lunchtime will be quiet. Shops that sell snacks and drinks will sell less during the day.

In the late afternoon and evening, the pattern reverses. As Iftar approaches, demand surges. People buy dates, fruits, drinks, small chops, cooked food, and groceries for the evening meal. Food vendors, supermarkets, bakeries and fruit sellers see their busiest hours between 4 p.m. and 7 p.m., and again after the evening prayers. Online orders spike in the afternoon, as people order Iftar food for delivery before sunset. A food business that closes at 5 p.m. during Ramadan will miss its best sales window of the day.

At night, after Iftar and prayers, there is another wave of activity. People go out to shop, visit markets, and browse online. Night markets and evening shopping become more popular, especially in northern Nigeria and in Muslim-majority neighbourhoods in Lagos and Abuja. Fashion, electronics and home goods sell well in the evening, as people prepare for Eid and buy gifts for family.

In the early morning, before dawn, there is a smaller but real demand for Suhoor foods: light, filling foods that people eat before the fast begins. Bread, akara, pap, tea, milk, dates, and ready-made Suhoor packs sell well if they are available early. A bakery or food vendor that opens early or offers pre-ordered Suhoor packs can serve a need that many businesses ignore.

Spending patterns also change. Many families spend more on food during Ramadan, because they cook larger evening meals and host guests for Iftar. They also spend more on charity, giving food, money and gifts to those in need. Towards the end of the month, spending shifts sharply to Eid: new clothes, shoes, gifts, food for celebration, and home items. A fashion business that stocks Eid collections early will sell well. A business that waits until the last week to stock Eid items will find that suppliers are out of stock and customers have already bought elsewhere.

Understanding these patterns helps you make better decisions: when to open, what to stock, when to run promotions, and when to rest. Do not fight the rhythm of the month. Work with it.

## Plan Before Ramadan Begins

Good Ramadan management starts two to three weeks before the month begins. That planning time is when you make the decisions that will save you stress later.

Start by checking the expected start date. Ramadan follows the Islamic lunar calendar, so the start date shifts each year and is confirmed by moon sighting. Check the announcement from the Nigerian Supreme Council for Islamic Affairs or your local mosque, and follow a reliable source for the exact date. Mark the start date and the expected Eid date on your calendar, and plan your stock, your staff rota and your promotions around those dates. Do not wait until the first day of fasting to decide your hours.

Talk to your suppliers early. Tell them what you will need during Ramadan and when you will need it. If you sell dates, fruits, rice, cooking oil, flour, sugar, milk, or other Ramadan staples, order early, because prices often rise and supply becomes tight as the month approaches. If you sell fashion, order your Eid collection early, and ask your supplier for the delivery date in writing. If you sell food, confirm that your ingredients will be available throughout the month, and agree on delivery days with your supplier. A supplier who knows your plan can help you. A supplier you call in a panic on the third day of Ramadan cannot.

Review your stock and your prices. Look at what sold well during the last Ramadan, if you have that record, and stock more of it. If you do not have a record, ask your regular customers what they usually buy during Ramadan, and stock accordingly. Check your prices against your costs, and decide whether you will offer Ramadan discounts, bundles or special packs. A Ramadan bundle — for example, a dates and drink pack, an Iftar family pack, or an Eid outfit set — is easier to sell than individual items, and it helps customers who are buying for a family.

Plan your cash flow. Ramadan often means higher spending on stock before the sales come in, especially for food and fashion businesses. Make sure you have enough cash or credit to buy stock early, pay your staff, and cover delivery costs during the busy evening hours. If you need a short-term loan or an advance from a supplier, arrange it before the month starts, not when you are already out of stock.

Finally, plan your marketing. Decide what you will post, when you will post it, and what offer you will make. Plan content for the start of Ramadan, for the middle weeks, and for the Eid rush. A business that plans its Ramadan content in advance will post consistently. A business that improvises daily will post sporadically and miss the best moments.

## Adjust Your Hours to Fit Fasting Life

Your normal opening hours may not work during Ramadan. A shop that opens at 8 a.m. and closes at 6 p.m. will be quiet in the morning, busy in the late afternoon, and closed when many customers want to shop in the evening. Adjusting your hours, even slightly, can make a large difference to your sales and to your staff's wellbeing.

Consider opening a little later in the morning and staying open later in the evening. For many retail businesses, opening at 10 a.m. and closing at 9 p.m. or 10 p.m. during Ramadan works better than the usual 8 a.m. to 6 p.m. That shift matches when customers are actually shopping, and it gives fasting staff a chance to rest in the morning and work when they have more energy after Iftar. For food businesses, the evening is essential: stay open through Iftar and into the night, because that is when your customers are buying.

If you run an online business, adjust your response and dispatch times to match the Ramadan rhythm. Tell customers clearly when you reply to messages and when you dispatch orders. For example: "We reply from 10 a.m. to 10 p.m. during Ramadan. Orders placed before 3 p.m. are dispatched the same day for Iftar delivery." That clarity prevents frustration and helps customers plan their orders. Use your WhatsApp Business away message and your Instagram bio to communicate the Ramadan hours, and pin the hours to your profile so new visitors see them immediately.

For service businesses — salons, tailors, photographers, event planners — offer evening appointments during Ramadan. Many customers prefer to visit a salon or a tailor after Iftar, when they are not fasting and have more energy. A tailor who offers evening fittings in the weeks before Eid will be far busier than one who only works during the day. A salon that opens in the evening for Eid preparations will attract customers who are getting ready for the celebration. Advertise those evening slots clearly, and allow customers to book in advance.

Be flexible where you can. If a customer asks for an early morning appointment for Suhoor-related shopping, or a late evening delivery, try to accommodate them if it is safe and practical. Flexibility during Ramadan is remembered, and it builds loyalty. But do not promise hours you cannot keep. It is better to say you close at 9 p.m. and close at 9 p.m., than to say you close at 10 p.m. and close early because your staff are exhausted.

## Stock the Right Products for Iftar, Suhoor and Eid

Stocking for Ramadan is not about stocking more of everything. It is about stocking more of what people actually need during the month, and less of what they will not buy.

For food and grocery businesses, the Ramadan staples are clear. Dates are the most important Iftar item, and they sell in large quantities throughout the month. Stock different types and price points, from affordable local dates to premium imported dates, and offer them in small packs for daily use and large packs for families. Fruits such as oranges, watermelon, pineapple, bananas and apples sell well for Iftar. Drinks such as zobo, kunu, tigernut drink, fresh juice, and bottled water are in high demand in the evening. Small chops, samosa, spring rolls, puff puff, meat pie and other finger foods are popular for Iftar gatherings. Rice, cooking oil, tomatoes, pepper, onions, flour, sugar, milk, tea and bread are the everyday staples that families buy in larger quantities during the month.

Create Ramadan packs and bundles that make shopping easy. An Iftar pack with dates, fruits and a drink. A Suhoor pack with bread, tea, milk and akara ingredients. A family cooking pack with rice, oil and tomatoes. Bundles save the customer time, they increase your average order value, and they are easy to advertise on WhatsApp and Instagram. Price them fairly, and make sure the bundle actually saves the customer money compared to buying the items separately, or at least saves them time and effort.

For fashion and beauty businesses, the key period is the run-up to Eid. Stock new arrivals early, and focus on outfits that people wear for Eid prayers and celebrations: elegant kaftans, abayas, jalabiyas, senator wear, ankara styles, and children's outfits. Stock shoes, bags, hijabs, caps and accessories that complete the outfit. Offer matching family sets, which are popular for Eid photos. If you are a tailor, take Eid orders early and set a clear cut-off date for orders you can guarantee before Eid. Tell customers that date clearly, and do not accept orders after it if you cannot deliver them on time. A late Eid outfit is a ruined celebration, and the customer will remember it.

For skincare, perfume and gift businesses, Ramadan and Eid are strong seasons. Perfumes, oud, body oils, skincare sets and gift boxes sell well as Eid gifts and as personal treats. Create gift sets at different price points — for example, ₦10,000, ₦25,000 and ₦50,000 — so customers can buy a gift that fits their budget without asking for a custom quote. Gift wrapping and a handwritten card make a simple product feel special, and they cost very little.

For all businesses, watch your stock daily during Ramadan. The evening surge can clear a shelf in hours, and a product that is out of stock at 5 p.m. is a lost sale that evening. Check your stock every morning, reorder early, and keep a small buffer of your best sellers. If a product sells out, tell customers when it will be back, and offer a similar product in the meantime. Never take money for a product you do not have.

## Treat Fasting Staff with Respect and Flexibility

Your staff are the heart of your business during Ramadan, and many of them will be fasting. How you treat them during the month will affect their energy, their morale, and the quality of service your customers receive. A tired, hungry, unsupported staff member cannot give good service, no matter how good your products are.

Start by talking to your staff before Ramadan begins. Ask who will be fasting, and ask what support they need. Do not assume. Some staff may want shorter shifts, some may want a later start, some may want a break for Iftar, and some may want to work through and take time off for Eid instead. Listen to them, and create a rota that is fair to everyone, fasting and non-fasting alike. Share the rota in writing, so there are no misunderstandings.

Reduce physically demanding tasks during the fasting hours where possible. Heavy lifting, long hours in the sun, and non-stop standing are harder when a person has not eaten or drunk water since dawn. If you can, schedule the heaviest work for the morning when energy is higher, or for after Iftar when staff have eaten. Give staff regular short breaks, and make sure there is a clean, comfortable place for them to rest and pray. A small prayer space, a chair, and access to water for breaking the fast are simple provisions that show respect.

Provide Iftar for your staff, or at least allow them a proper break to break their fast. If your business is open during Iftar time, arrange for staff to take turns breaking their fast, so the business stays open while each person eats. Provide dates and water, or a simple meal, for staff who are fasting. That gesture costs little and means a great deal. It tells your team that you see them as people, not just workers.

Be understanding about energy levels. Fasting staff may be slower in the afternoon, and they may need a little more patience. Do not punish tiredness. Instead, plan for it: schedule fewer demanding tasks in the afternoon, pair a fasting staff member with a non-fasting colleague for heavy work, and keep the atmosphere calm. A kind word and a flexible approach will get far more from your team than pressure.

Plan for Eid leave early. Many staff will want time off for Eid prayers and family celebrations. Ask them in advance which days they need, and create a rota that covers the business while respecting their holiday. If you will be closed for Eid, tell customers well in advance. If you will be open, make sure you have enough staff to serve customers properly. Announcing your Eid hours early prevents confusion and shows that you have planned.

Ramadan rewards businesses that plan early, respect fasting customers and staff, and stock what families need. Adjust your hours, prepare for Iftar and Eid, and market with sensitivity. Do that, and the holy month can become one of the strongest and most rewarding months in your business year.
