Every Nigerian business owner knows the feeling. December was busy — customers buying, money coming in, the shop full. Then January arrives, and the phone goes quiet. The same thing happens in the middle of the rainy season, when movement slows and customers stay home, or in any month when the economy tightens and people hold on to their money. Sales drop by half, but rent, salaries, data, and generator fuel do not drop at all.

Slow months do not kill most businesses. Running out of cash during slow months does. The businesses that survive are rarely the ones with the most sales in good months. They are the ones that planned for the quiet months, kept a buffer, controlled their costs, and found ways to bring money in when customers were scarce.

This guide shows you how to do that — practically, with naira figures and methods that work for a small business in Lagos, Abuja, Onitsha, or Port Harcourt.

## Know Your Slow Months Before They Arrive

The first step is to stop being surprised. Most Nigerian businesses have a pattern, and if you look at your own sales records you will see it. For many retailers, January and February are slow because customers spent everything in December. For fashion and event businesses, the rainy season from June to September is quiet because there are fewer weddings and parties. For foodstuff sellers, the months before harvest can be expensive and slow. For businesses that serve salary earners, the last week of the month is always slower than the first week.

Take one hour this week and do this exercise. Write down your monthly sales for the last twelve months, even roughly. Circle your three slowest months and your three busiest months. Then write down your fixed monthly costs: rent divided by twelve, salaries, data and airtime, generator fuel or electricity, transport, and subscriptions. The gap between your slowest month's income and your fixed costs is the amount you need to prepare for. If your slowest month brings in ₦300,000 and your fixed costs are ₦450,000, you need ₦150,000 of buffer for that month — and you need to build it during the good months.

Businesses that do not track this are flying blind. They spend December's profit on a new phone or a party, and then borrow at terrible rates in January to pay rent. A simple notebook or a Google Sheet with monthly income and expenses is enough. The habit matters more than the tool.

## Build a Cash Buffer in the Good Months

A cash buffer is money set aside for the months when sales are low. It is not savings for expansion, and it is not money for a new car. It is survival money, and it should be treated as untouchable until a slow month arrives.

How much buffer do you need? A good target is two to three months of fixed costs. If your fixed costs are ₦400,000 a month, your buffer target is ₦800,000 to ₦1,200,000. That sounds like a lot, and it is — but you do not build it in one month. You build it in small pieces during the good months. If December brings an extra ₦600,000 in profit, move ₦300,000 into the buffer before you spend anything else. Pay the buffer first, the way you pay rent first.

Where should the buffer sit? Keep it in a separate account from your daily trading money — a different bank account, a Moniepoint or Opay savings wallet, or a fixed deposit you cannot touch impulsively. If the buffer sits in the same account as your daily money, you will spend it. Separation is what makes it real. Many Nigerian banks and fintech apps now offer savings pockets or target savings with small interest. Use them. Even a small return is better than zero, and the lock-in feature protects you from yourself.

Set a rule: the buffer is only used for fixed costs in a slow month — rent, salaries, and essential operations. It is never used for new stock, new equipment, or personal spending. When a slow month ends and sales recover, the first job is to rebuild the buffer before anything else.

## Cut Costs Smartly — Not Blindly

When sales fall, the instinct is to cut everything. That instinct is wrong. Cutting the wrong costs kills the business faster than the slow month does. Cut costs that do not bring in customers, and protect the costs that do.

**Costs to cut or pause first:**

- Subscriptions you barely use — extra software, unused data plans, a second phone line for the business that nobody calls.
- Wasteful spending on packaging or printing — buy in bulk, use simpler packaging for the slow season.
- Unnecessary travel — combine trips to the market, buy stock in fewer, larger trips to save transport money.
- Eating out and personal spending from the business account — this is where many small businesses bleed cash without noticing.
- Discounts that are too deep — a 50 percent discount in a slow month may bring customers but destroy your margin. A smaller, smarter offer is better.

**Costs to protect:**

- Your best staff. Losing a good staff member in a slow month costs far more to replace than the salary you save.
- Marketing that brings customers. Stopping all advertising in a slow month is like stopping eating because you are hungry. Reduce it, target it better, but do not stop it completely.
- Product quality. Cutting corners on quality to save money in a slow month destroys the trust that brings customers back in the good months.
- Your phone and data. In a business that runs on WhatsApp, your phone is your shop. Keep it charged and connected.

Negotiate with your suppliers and landlord before you are desperate. A supplier who knows you are a loyal customer may give you an extra week to pay. A landlord may accept rent in two instalments if you ask early and honestly. People help businesses that communicate early. They do not help businesses that disappear and then beg at the last minute.

## Collect the Money People Already Owe You

One of the fastest ways to improve cash flow in a slow month costs nothing: collect outstanding debts. Many Nigerian small businesses are owed surprising amounts of money by customers who bought on credit, friends who took goods "on trust", and corporate clients who pay late. In a slow month, that money is the difference between paying salaries and not paying them.

Make a list of everyone who owes you, with amounts and dates. Then work through it systematically:

- Start with the largest and oldest debts. A polite but firm WhatsApp message: "Good morning, I hope you are well. I am doing my monthly accounts and I noticed a balance of ₦85,000 from your order on 12 October. Could you please help me clear it this week?"
- Offer a small incentive for immediate payment: a 5 percent discount for paying in full this week, or free delivery on their next order.
- For corporate clients, send a formal reminder on your letterhead with an invoice attached. Many companies pay slowly because nobody followed up, not because they cannot pay.
- Accept part payment. ₦50,000 today is better than ₦100,000 promised for "next month" that never comes.
- Stop giving new credit to anyone who has not cleared old credit. This rule alone prevents the debt pile from growing.

Going forward, reduce credit sharply. Cash on delivery, payment before dispatch, or a deposit of at least 50 percent should be the default. Credit is a loan you give without interest, without security, and often without a repayment date. In a slow month, that generosity can sink you.

## Find Revenue When Customers Are Scarce

Slow months are not zero months. Customers are still buying — they are buying less, buying cheaper, and buying from whoever makes it easiest. Your job is to meet them where they are.

- **Offer smaller packs and lower-priced options.** If customers cannot afford your ₦25,000 package, offer a ₦8,000 starter version. A skincare seller can offer a small size. A food vendor can offer a half portion. A fashion seller can push ready-to-wear instead of custom orders. Lower ticket sizes keep money coming in.
- **Run a limited, honest promotion.** A "slow season sale" with a clear end date works better than a permanent discount. Offer 10 to 15 percent off, or buy-two-get-one on selected items, or free delivery within your city for orders above a set amount. Make the offer specific and time-bound so customers act now instead of waiting.
- **Sell to your existing customers first.** It costs far less to sell to someone who already trusts you than to find a new customer. Message your past buyers on WhatsApp with something genuinely useful: a new arrival, a restock, a small loyalty discount. One personal message to fifty past customers can produce more sales than a week of posting to strangers.
- **Take prepayments and bookings.** If your business is seasonal — events, fashion, catering — sell ahead. Offer a discount for customers who book and pay a deposit now for December, for a wedding next month, or for a service they will need soon. Prepayment turns future revenue into cash today.
- **Add a complementary service.** A tailor can offer ready-made shirts while custom orders are slow. A baker can sell small chops for offices. A hair vendor can offer installation services. A phone seller can offer repairs. Look at what your existing customers already ask for and charge for it.
- **Sell what is gathering dust.** Slow stock is dead money. Identify items that have not sold in three months, mark them down, bundle them, or sell them at cost to free up cash. Cash in your account is worth more than stock on a shelf that nobody wants.

## Manage Stock Like Cash — Because It Is

For many Nigerian traders, most of their money is sitting on shelves as stock. In a slow month, that is a problem. Every naira tied up in stock that is not selling is a naira that cannot pay rent or salaries.

In a slow month, buy less and buy smarter. Order smaller quantities more often instead of one large order. Focus on your fastest-selling items and pause the slow ones. Check your records: which ten items bring in most of your sales? Those are the ones to keep in stock. The other forty items can wait until sales pick up.

If you run a shop in a market like Balogun or Onitsha Main Market, talk to your supplier about consignment or sale-or-return for some items. Not all suppliers agree, but regular customers with a good payment history can often negotiate it. And avoid buying stock on credit in a slow month unless you are certain it will sell quickly. New debt taken in a slow month is the hardest debt to repay.

## Keep Your Team Honest and Motivated

Slow months are hard on staff too. Salaries feel heavy, morale drops, and some staff start looking for other jobs. Handle this openly. If sales are down, tell your team plainly and share the plan: "This is our slow season. Here is what we are doing to bring in sales, and here is what I need from each of you." People work harder when they understand the situation than when they sense trouble but nobody explains it.

Avoid cutting salaries suddenly without discussion — it destroys trust and pushes your best people out. If you must reduce hours or pay temporarily, agree it openly, put a date on it, and restore it as soon as sales recover. Consider non-cash motivation: a small bonus for every sale, a free lunch on Saturdays, or public praise for the staff member who brings in the most customers. In a slow month, a motivated team that hustles for every sale matters more than any advert.

Also use slow months for training. Teach your staff new skills, improve your WhatsApp replies, update your product catalogue, clean the shop, and fix the things you never have time for when you are busy. A slow month used well makes the next busy month more profitable.

## Avoid the Borrowing Trap

When cash is tight, borrowing feels like the answer. Sometimes it is — but usually it is not, and the wrong loan makes a slow month into a crisis.

Be careful with:

- **Loan apps with very high interest.** Borrowing ₦100,000 and repaying ₦140,000 in a month is a hole that gets deeper every time you do it.
- **Borrowing to pay for non-essentials.** A loan for rent or salaries during a genuine slow month is understandable. A loan for new stock you are not sure will sell, or for personal spending, is dangerous.
- **Borrowing from multiple sources at once.** One loan is manageable. Three loans from three apps is a spiral.

If you must borrow, borrow from the cheapest source available: a cooperative society, a trusted family arrangement with clear terms, or a bank facility with a reasonable rate. Borrow only the amount you need for fixed costs, write down the repayment plan, and repay it first when sales recover. And never borrow to cover a loss you could have avoided by collecting debts or cutting waste. Fix the leak before you add water.

## Use Slow Months to Fix the Business

The busiest months leave no time for improvement. Slow months are your workshop. Use them to:

- **Review your prices.** Are you charging enough? Many Nigerian SMEs undercharge because they fear losing customers, and then suffer in slow months because margins are too thin. A small price increase on your best sellers, done carefully, can matter more than ten new customers.
- **Clean up your customer list.** Remove dead numbers, label your best customers, and note who buys what. A clean list makes your WhatsApp marketing far more effective.
- **Improve your online presence.** Update your Google Business Profile, post consistently on Instagram and Facebook, ask happy customers for reviews. This work pays off when the busy season returns.
- **Plan the next busy season.** December, wedding season, back-to-school — whatever your peak is, prepare for it now: order stock early, plan promotions, and set your budget while you have time to think.
- **Learn one new skill.** A short course on pricing, bookkeeping, or digital marketing can change your margins permanently. Free and low-cost learning is available online; the slow month gives you the time to use it.

## Your Slow-Month Action Checklist

- [ ] Write down your slowest months and your fixed monthly costs
- [ ] Set a buffer target of two to three months of fixed costs
- [ ] Move buffer money into a separate account during good months
- [ ] List everyone who owes you and start collecting, largest first
- [ ] Cut wasteful spending and protect marketing, quality, and your best staff
- [ ] Offer smaller packs, a limited promotion, and prepayment deals
- [ ] Message past customers with something useful, not spam
- [ ] Reduce stock orders and focus on your fastest sellers
- [ ] Talk to your team honestly and agree the plan together
- [ ] Avoid high-interest loans; borrow only for essentials if you must
- [ ] Use the quiet time to fix prices, clean your customer list, and prepare for the next busy season

Slow months are part of business in Nigeria, not a sign that your business has failed. The traders who last ten, twenty, thirty years are not the ones who never had a slow month. They are the ones who expected it, prepared for it, and used it. Build the buffer, collect what you are owed, spend wisely, and keep selling — even in small amounts — and the slow month becomes a season you survive with your business, your staff, and your reputation intact.
