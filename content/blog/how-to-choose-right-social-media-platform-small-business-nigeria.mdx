Every Nigerian small business owner has heard the same advice: "You need to be on social media." So you open Instagram, Facebook, TikTok, X, and WhatsApp Business, post a few times, and then nothing happens. No orders, no enquiries, just silence and the feeling that you are wasting your evenings. The problem is rarely that social media does not work. The problem is that you are on the wrong platform, or on too many of them at once.

The right platform is not the most popular one. It is the one where your own customers already spend their time, in the mood to buy what you sell. A caterer in Ikeja and a fashion vendor in Lekki need different platforms. A B2B printing company in Yaba needs something different again. This guide will help you choose wisely, start small, and put your limited time where it actually brings in orders.

## Start With Your Customers, Not the Platform

Before you open any app, answer one question: who buys from you, and where do they spend their time online? Everything else follows from that answer.

Write down a simple profile of your best customer. How old are they? Are they mostly women or men? Do they live in Lagos, Abuja, Port Harcourt, or across the country? Do they buy on impulse, or do they compare and think for weeks? Do they have money to spend quickly, or do they save up for a big purchase?

A woman selling luxury hair in Lekki serves customers who are mostly women between 20 and 45, active on Instagram and TikTok, and comfortable paying ₦40,000 or more through a bank transfer after seeing a video. A man selling building materials in Onitsha serves contractors and site engineers who are mostly men, busy during the day, and reachable on WhatsApp and Facebook groups.

Ask your existing customers directly. Send a short WhatsApp message to ten of your best buyers: "Quick question — apart from WhatsApp, which app do you use most: Instagram, Facebook, or TikTok?" Their answers are worth more than any article, because they describe your real market, not the average Nigerian internet user.

Look at where your current orders already come from. If eight out of ten enquiries arrive through WhatsApp, your customers are telling you where they are comfortable. Build on that strength instead of chasing a platform where you have no audience yet.

## WhatsApp: Your Shop Front and Your Cash Register

For most Nigerian small businesses, WhatsApp is not just another platform. It is the shop itself. Customers discover you elsewhere, but they buy on WhatsApp. They ask questions, negotiate, send proof of payment, and share their delivery address — all in one chat.

WhatsApp Business, the free app, gives you tools that the ordinary app does not. You can set up a business profile with your address, opening hours, and a catalogue of your products with prices. You can save quick replies for questions you answer every day, like "Do you deliver to Abuja?" and set an away message for when you are asleep. You can label chats — new customer, paid, pending delivery — so no order slips through.

WhatsApp Status is your free daily advert. Post your new stock, a customer review, or a short video of your product every morning, and your contacts see it before they open Instagram. A food vendor in Surulere who posts her menu on Status by 8 a.m. gets lunch orders before 10 a.m.

The weakness of WhatsApp is discovery. People can only find you if they already have your number or someone shares it. That is why WhatsApp works best paired with one public platform — Instagram, Facebook, or TikTok — that brings strangers in, while WhatsApp closes the sale. Use the public platform to attract, and WhatsApp to convert.

## Instagram: Where Visual Products Win

Instagram is the home of visual selling in Nigeria. If your product looks good in a photo or a short video — fashion, hair, food, cakes, skincare, interior decor, event planning — Instagram is probably your strongest public platform.

Reels are the engine of growth. A 15-second video of a dress twirling, a cake being decorated, or a wig being installed reaches far more people than a still photo. You do not need a professional camera. A clean background, good daylight, and a steady phone are enough to start. Post Reels three to four times a week and watch which ones get saved and shared; those are the products people want.

Your bio is your shop sign. In one line, say what you sell, who it is for, and where you deliver. "Luxury wigs, Lekki | Nationwide delivery | WhatsApp to order" tells a visitor everything in seconds. Link your WhatsApp number so a buyer can tap and chat without hunting for it.

Stories keep you in front of your followers every day. Polls, questions, behind-the-scenes clips of you packing an order, and customer reviews all build trust. A follower who watches your Stories for a week is far more likely to buy than one who only saw a single post.

## Facebook: The Biggest Audience, Especially Beyond Lagos

Facebook has the largest audience in Nigeria, and it reaches people Instagram misses: older buyers, customers outside Lagos and Abuja, and whole communities organised around groups. If your customers are over 35, or if you sell to people in smaller towns, Facebook deserves a serious look.

Facebook Groups are the hidden strength. There are active groups for buying and selling in almost every Nigerian city, for mums, for traders, for church communities, for estate residents. A seller of children's clothes in Ibadan who posts in two or three local mums' groups can reach thousands of real buyers without paying for adverts. Join the groups as a person first, answer questions, and post your products with clear prices and delivery terms.

Your Facebook Page is your public shop window. Keep it updated with your products, your prices, your location, and your WhatsApp link. A Page that has not posted since last year tells visitors the business may be closed. Even two or three posts a week keep it alive.

Facebook adverts are the cheapest way to reach a precise audience in Nigeria. With as little as ₦5,000, you can show your product to women aged 25 to 45 in Lekki who like fashion pages. Start small, test one advert at a time, and only increase the budget on adverts that bring real WhatsApp messages, not just likes.

## TikTok: Fast Growth, Young Buyers, Real Personality

TikTok is the fastest way for a small Nigerian business to be discovered by strangers. Its algorithm shows your video to people who have never heard of you, based on what they watch, not on how many followers you have. A new account can reach thousands of people with its third video.

TikTok buyers are mostly young — teenagers to early thirties — and they buy on emotion and entertainment. They do not want a polished advert. They want a real person showing a real product, a transformation, a funny moment, or a useful tip. A skincare seller in Abuja who films her morning routine, a tailor who shows a dress from fabric to finished outfit, a food vendor who films the steam rising from a pot — these videos sell because they feel honest.

Live selling works well on TikTok. Go live, show your products, answer questions in real time, and offer a small discount for orders placed during the live session. Even a modest audience of 30 or 40 viewers can produce several orders if they trust you.

The weakness of TikTok is that its audience is price-sensitive and quick to scroll past anything that feels like a hard sell. Luxury products and high-ticket services convert more slowly here than on Instagram. And TikTok demands frequent posting — several short videos a week — which is a lot of work for a one-person business.

## X and LinkedIn: For Conversations and Corporate Clients

X, formerly Twitter, and LinkedIn serve a narrower purpose for most small businesses, but for the right business they are the best choice of all.

X is where Nigerians talk. News, jokes, complaints, and debates move fast, and a business that joins the conversation with a real personality can build a loyal following. X works well for tech products, digital services, and food brands with a sense of humour. But X is a poor shop window: it is hard to display products, and posts disappear quickly, so it suits services and ideas more than physical goods.

LinkedIn is where corporate Nigeria lives. If you sell to companies — printing, branding, training, catering for offices, software, consulting — LinkedIn is your platform. Decision-makers, HR managers, and procurement officers are there, reading posts during work hours. A printer in Ikeja who posts photos of a corporate job well done, or a trainer who shares a short lesson every week, can attract clients that Instagram would never deliver. LinkedIn users also expect a professional tone, a complete profile, and useful content rather than sales posts.

Choose X or LinkedIn only if your customers are actually there. A fashion vendor chasing LinkedIn, or a corporate trainer chasing TikTok, is fishing in the wrong river.

## Compare the Platforms Side by Side

When you have a shortlist, compare them on five simple questions. First, are my customers there? Second, does the platform suit my product — visual, conversational, or professional? Third, can I create the content it needs with the time and phone I have? Fourth, can customers buy easily from it, or does it lead them to WhatsApp? Fifth, can I afford to be consistent there for at least three months?

Score each platform honestly. A platform that scores well on customers but badly on your available time is a bad choice right now. A platform that is easy for you but has none of your buyers is a hobby, not marketing.

Beware the trap of being everywhere. A business that posts once a month on five platforms looks dead on all five. A business that posts four times a week on one platform looks alive, reliable, and worth buying from. Depth beats breadth every time for a small business.

## Pick One Main Platform and One Support Platform

The practical answer for most Nigerian small businesses is simple: pick one main public platform and let WhatsApp be your support platform. That is two platforms, not five, and it is enough to grow.

A fashion seller in Lekki might choose Instagram as her main platform and WhatsApp as her shop. A food vendor in Enugu might choose Facebook and WhatsApp. A young skincare brand might choose TikTok and WhatsApp. A branding agency in Lagos might choose LinkedIn and WhatsApp.

Commit to your main platform for at least 90 days before you judge it. Post consistently, reply to every comment and message within a few hours, and track where your orders come from. Ninety days is long enough to learn what your audience likes and short enough that you have not wasted a year on the wrong choice.

Set up the basics properly on day one. A clear profile photo, usually your logo. A bio that says what you sell and how to order. Your WhatsApp link. Your location and delivery areas. A pinned post or highlight showing your best products and your prices. These small details decide whether a visitor becomes a customer or scrolls away.

## Create Content You Can Sustain

The best platform is the one you will actually post on. Before you commit, ask yourself what content you can make every week without burning out.

If you love taking photos, Instagram will be easy. If you love talking to the camera, TikTok will be easy. If you love writing short tips, X or LinkedIn will be easy. If you hate all of it, start with WhatsApp Status, which takes two minutes a day, and add one public platform when you are ready.

Build a simple weekly routine. For example, on Instagram: a Reel on Monday, a product photo on Wednesday, customer reviews on Stories every day, and a live session on Saturday. Write the plan down, prepare your photos on Sunday evening, and stick to it. Consistency matters more than perfection — a decent post every week beats a perfect post once a month.

Reuse your content. One short video can go on TikTok, Instagram Reels, Facebook, and your WhatsApp Status. One customer review can be a screenshot on all of them. You do not need new ideas for every platform; you need one good idea used well.

## Avoid the Common Mistakes

The first mistake is buying followers. A page with 20,000 bought followers and no engagement looks fake, and real customers can tell. Worse, the platform shows your posts to those fake followers first, so your real customers never see them. Grow slowly and honestly.

The second mistake is posting only adverts. If every post says "Buy now, price in DM," people will mute you. Mix in useful tips, behind-the-scenes moments, customer stories, and answers to common questions. A good guide is four useful or entertaining posts for every one direct sales post.

The third mistake is hiding your prices. Nigerian customers are tired of "DM for price." Many will simply move to a seller who states the price. You do not need to publish every price, but give price ranges or starting prices so serious buyers can decide quickly.

The fourth mistake is copying a big brand's strategy. A large company can afford a team, adverts, and a studio. You cannot, and you do not need to. Your advantage is personality, speed, and personal service. Use it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Which social media platform is best for a small business in Nigeria?**
There is no single best platform. WhatsApp is essential for almost every Nigerian small business because that is where sales close. Your main public platform depends on your customers: Instagram for visual products, Facebook for older and nationwide audiences, TikTok for young buyers, LinkedIn for corporate clients.

**Should I be on every platform?**
No. Being on five platforms badly is worse than being on one platform well. Pick one main public platform plus WhatsApp, commit to it for 90 days, and only add another platform when the first one is working.

**How much time should I spend on social media each day?**
Aim for 30 to 60 minutes a day: 20 minutes creating or posting content, and the rest replying to messages and comments. Batch your content creation — prepare several posts on Sunday evening — so weekdays stay manageable.

**Do I need to pay for adverts to succeed?**
No, but a small budget helps. You can grow organically with good Reels, TikTok videos, and Facebook Groups. When you do advertise, start with as little as ₦5,000, test one advert at a time, and only increase spending on adverts that bring real WhatsApp enquiries.

**What if my customers are not on social media?**
Some customers, especially older buyers and businesses in certain trades, are reached better by phone calls, referrals, and physical presence in markets. Use WhatsApp at minimum, since almost every Nigerian buyer has it, and let referrals and word of mouth do the rest.

**How do I know if a platform is working for me?**
Track where your orders come from. Ask every new customer, "Where did you find us?" After 90 days, the platform that brings the most paying customers — not the most likes — is your winner.

## What to Do Next

1. Write down a simple profile of your best customer: age, location, what they buy, and how they decide.
2. Ask ten of your best customers which app they use most apart from WhatsApp.
3. Set up WhatsApp Business with a catalogue, quick replies, and an away message if you have not already.
4. Pick one main public platform based on your customers and your product, and set up the profile properly with a clear bio and WhatsApp link.
5. Plan a simple weekly posting routine you can sustain, and prepare your first week of content in advance.
6. Post consistently for 90 days, reply to every message within a few hours, and track where each order comes from.
7. After 90 days, keep what brings paying customers, drop what does not, and only then consider adding a second public platform.
