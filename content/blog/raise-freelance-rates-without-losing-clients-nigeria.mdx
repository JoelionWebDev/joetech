If you have been freelancing for a while and your rates have not changed, you are probably earning less than your work is worth. Prices of data, transport, food and software subscriptions have all gone up, and your skills have grown too. Yet many freelancers in Nigeria keep charging the same fees they set when they started, simply because they are afraid of losing clients.

The fear is understandable. Your clients pay your bills, and nobody wants to scare them away. But raising your rates does not have to mean losing clients. Done well, it strengthens your business: you earn more from the work you already do, attract clients who value quality, and stop burning out on too many cheap jobs. This guide walks you through how to do it.

## Signs It Is Time to Raise Your Rates

Before you write a single message to a client, confirm that a rate increase is genuinely due. Here are the clearest signs:

- **You are fully booked.** If your calendar is full for weeks and you keep turning work away, your price is too low. Demand has outgrown your supply.
- **Your skills have grown.** You can deliver faster, better work than you could a year ago. New skills, new tools and more experience are all worth paying for.
- **Your costs have risen.** Subscriptions, internet, power and transport all cost more than they did when you set your current rates.
- **Clients keep coming back.** Repeat clients are a strong signal. People who return are already convinced of your value.
- **You feel resentful doing the work.** If you sigh before opening a project file because the pay does not match the effort, that feeling is your business telling you something.

If two or more of these apply to you, the time to raise your rates is now, not next year.

## Step 1: Decide Your New Rate Properly

Do not pick a new number out of thin air, and do not copy another freelancer's rate without thinking. Work it out from your own situation:

1. **Add up your monthly business costs.** Include internet, subscriptions, power, transport, phone and any tools you pay for.
2. **Decide how many billable hours you realistically work.** Most freelancers can bill 15 to 25 hours a week once admin, learning and marketing are counted.
3. **Set your target monthly income.** Add your costs to the income you need to live well and save a little.
4. **Divide the total by your billable hours.** That gives you a rough hourly floor. Project rates should be built from this, not from guesswork.

Also look at what others in your field charge, not to copy them but to know the range. If your new rate sits within a fair range for your experience level, you can defend it with confidence.

## Step 2: Raise Rates for New Clients First

The easiest place to start is with people who have never worked with you. New clients have no old price to compare against, so they simply accept your rate or they do not. Quote your new rate on every fresh enquiry from today onwards.

This does two things. First, it immediately improves your income on new work. Second, it gives you proof: when new clients accept the higher rate, you gain the confidence to bring existing clients up to the same level. If a new client says the price is too high, thank them politely and move on. Not every enquiry is your client.

## Step 3: Give Existing Clients Notice and a Reason

For clients you already work with, a sudden price jump feels like a shock. Give them notice of at least two to four weeks, and explain the change in plain, respectful language. A short message like this works well:

> "Hello, I wanted to let you know that from [date], my rate for [service] will change from [old rate] to [new rate]. Over the past year I have invested in new tools and training so I can deliver better results for you, and my costs have also increased. I value our work together and I am happy to discuss how we can keep things running smoothly."

Notice what this message does: it states the change clearly, gives a date, offers one honest reason, and keeps the door open for conversation. It does not apologise excessively or beg for permission. You are informing a client of a business decision, not asking for a favour.

## Step 4: Add Value Before You Raise the Price

A rate increase lands much better when the client can see something extra in return. Before you send that message, think about what you can genuinely improve:

- Faster turnaround on regular work
- A short monthly summary of what you delivered
- Clearer communication, such as a weekly progress update

You do not need to give away free work. The point is that the client feels the higher price comes with a better service, not the same service at a higher cost.

## Step 5: Handle Objections Calmly

Some clients will push back. That is normal, and it does not mean you have failed. Here is how to handle the most common responses:

- **"That is too expensive."** Ask what budget they have in mind and whether the scope can be reduced to fit it. A smaller package at your new rate is often a fair compromise.
- **"We will have to think about it."** Give them space and a date to decide by. Do not chase them every day.
- **"We can get it cheaper elsewhere."** Agree politely. Cheaper options exist for everything. Your clients stay because of your reliability, quality and understanding of their business, not because you are the cheapest.
- **Silence.** Some clients simply stop replying. That is their answer, and it is fine. A client who leaves over a fair rate increase was unlikely to stay long term anyway.

Never lower your new rate in a panic during the first conversation. Sleep on it. If you truly need the work, negotiate scope or payment terms, not a discount on your worth.

## Step 6: Consider a Phased Increase for Big Clients

If one client provides a large share of your income, spread the change over time: raise the rate by half now and the rest in three months, with both dates stated clearly upfront. Set the final date in writing so the increase actually happens.

## What If You Lose a Client?

It may happen, and it is not a disaster. Here is the honest maths: if you raise your rates by 25 percent and lose one client out of five, you earn the same from four clients as you did from five, and you gain back the hours that fifth client used to take. Those hours can go to a better-paying client, to learning, or to rest. Clients who respect your work will respect a fair price.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Raising rates without telling anyone.** Clients who discover a higher invoice by surprise will not trust you again.
- **Apologising too much.** One polite sentence of explanation is enough. Over-apologising signals that even you do not believe the new rate is fair.
- **Raising rates but keeping the same poor boundaries.** New rates should come with clear terms: revision limits, working hours and payment deadlines.

## FAQ

### How often should I raise my freelance rates?

Review your rates at least once a year. If your skills, demand or costs have grown, an annual review keeps your pricing honest.

### Should I tell clients why my rates are going up?

Yes, briefly. One or two honest reasons, such as rising costs and improved skills, are enough. Clients understand that prices change. What they dislike is a change with no explanation.

### What if all my clients leave after I raise my rates?

That is very unlikely if the increase is fair and communicated well. If it happens, it usually means the rate jumped too far at once or the clients were only there for the low price.

### Can I raise rates for only some clients?

Yes. Move new clients to the new rate immediately and phase existing clients in over time. Just make sure every client eventually reaches the same rate for the same work.

## Final Thoughts

Start today: work out your new rate, quote it to the next new enquiry, and write the message to your existing clients. Your future self will thank you for it.
