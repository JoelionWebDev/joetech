Mrs Okafor runs a small restaurant off Allen Avenue in Ikeja. Her rice is good, her prices are fair, and her Google Business Profile has been sitting untouched for eight months — same photo, same hours, last post from February. Two doors down, a newer restaurant with blander food posts every Monday. When a hungry office worker nearby searches "restaurant near me" at lunchtime, whose name does she tap? The one that looks alive.

A Google Business Profile post is a short message pinned to your listing, visible on Search and Maps, free, and gone in seven days unless it is an offer with an end date. That short life is the whole point. A post says: this business is open, busy, something is happening here today. A listing with no posts says the opposite.

## The person reading your post is already looking for you

A Google post is not an Instagram caption. Nobody is scrolling past it for fun. The person reading it searched for something — "salon near me", "phone repair open now", "cake shop Surulere" — and your listing appeared. She is already halfway to buying. Your post has one job: give her a reason to choose you today instead of the shop down the road.

So the first sentence has to carry the news. "New Ankara fabrics just arrived at our Yaba shop" is a first sentence. "Good morning our dear esteemed customers, we wish to inform you..." is not — it is a greeting, and greetings do not sell fabric. Put the product first, the price second, and the place third. What it is, what it costs, where to get it.

Keep it short. A post that fits on one phone screen without scrolling gets read. Aim for two to five short sentences, one clear photo, and one thing to do next: call, send a WhatsApp message, or visit the shop. One action, not four.

## The four kinds of post, and when each one earns its place

Google gives you four types, and each one suits a different moment.

The update is the workhorse. Use it for news: new stock arrived, you now deliver to a new area, opening hours extended for December, a new service added. One or two a week is plenty, and they should be about something real. An update that says nothing new — "We are still open! We sell quality products!" — teaches the reader to ignore your next post too.

The offer is the one with a price cut, a bundle, or a free extra, and an end date. Offers are the strongest post type for bringing people through the door, which is exactly why you should not post one every week. A customer who sees a discount every Monday learns to wait for Monday and never pays full price. One offer a month, tied to something real, is the right rhythm.

The event post is for something that happens on a specific date: a grand opening, a training, a product launch, a Christmas party at your restaurant, a live sale. The date and time show prominently, so customers can plan around them. Event posts suit the calendar we actually live by: Christmas, Easter, Sallah, Independence Day, Valentine's Day, your business anniversary.

The product post showcases one specific product with a photo and a price in naira — a phone model, a cake design. Customers comparing prices need the price there. A product post without a price is a shop window with the price tags turned around.

## How to word an offer so people act on it

An offer is a promise, and promises need to be exact. The offers that fail in Nigeria almost always fail in the same four ways: the benefit is vague, the deadline is missing, the claiming step is complicated, or the conditions are hidden until the customer reaches the till.

Start with one clear benefit, stated in one sentence. "20% off all Ankara dresses this weekend" is clear. "Big discount on many items" is not, because the customer cannot picture what she gets. If the offer applies only to certain items, say so in the post. If there is a minimum spend, say so. Hidden conditions create arguments at the till and bad reviews afterwards.

Then set a real deadline, and mean it. "Valid until Sunday 14 December" pushes a customer to act now. "Limited time only" pushes nobody. Match the length to the goal: a weekend flash sale runs two or three days, a festive promotion runs two weeks, a clearance runs until stock finishes — and if you say "until stock finishes", remove the post when the stock is gone. If you extend an offer every week, customers learn your deadlines are fiction.

For walk-ins, one instruction is enough: "Mention this Google offer at the till." For WhatsApp orders, a short code works: "Send DEC20 to claim it." And use the terms field for the rules in one plain sentence: "One discount per customer. Valid at our Lekki branch only."

Finally, honour the offer exactly as written. Nothing damages trust faster than a customer arriving for an advertised discount and being told it has finished, it does not apply to her, or the price has changed. If stock runs out, update or remove the post the same day. Your Google listing is a public promise, and customers in Lagos have long memories for broken ones.

## Four posts you can adapt today

Here are four posts for real kinds of Nigerian businesses, with real prices. Copy the shape, change the details, and publish.

The first is an offer post for a hair salon in Lekki Phase 1. The photo is a stylist finishing a set of knotless braids on a real customer, in daylight, inside the salon. The text reads: "Knotless braids, ₦25,000 this weekend only — was ₦30,000. Valid Friday to Sunday at our Admiralty Way salon. Mention this Google offer at the till. One discount per customer, walk-ins welcome, call 0803 456 7890 to book your chair."

The second is an update post for a phone accessories shop in Computer Village, Ikeja. The photo is the new Oraimo power bank on the counter, still in its box, next to the shop's signboard. The text reads: "New stock: Oraimo 20,000mAh power bank, ₦18,500. Charges a phone four times — we tested it ourselves yesterday. Available now at Shop 14, Otigba Street, Computer Village. Send us a WhatsApp message to reserve yours before they finish."

The third is an event post for a restaurant in Surulere planning a Christmas lunch. The photo is last year's Christmas table, full plates, a family eating. The text reads: "Christmas Day lunch at our Surulere restaurant — buffet, live music, and a free drink for every child. Sunday 25 December, 12 noon to 5pm. ₦15,000 per adult, ₦7,500 per child. Book your table before 20 December on WhatsApp — last year we were full by the 22nd."

The fourth is a product post for a bakery in Abuja. The photo is a three-layer birthday cake, chocolate with red velvet filling, on a clean table in daylight. The text reads: "Birthday cakes from ₦35,000 — three layers, your flavour, your colours. Order 48 hours ahead and collect at our Wuse 2 shop, or we deliver across Abuja for ₦3,000. Send us a WhatsApp message with your date and we will confirm within an hour."

Notice what none of these posts do. None of them shout in capital letters. None of them say "affordable prices" instead of a price. None of them say "DM for price". And none of them hide the location. "At our Surulere branch", "Shop 14, Otigba Street", "Admiralty Way" — customers search with location in mind, and a post without a place is a post without a destination.

## Events, promos and holidays: the calendar that does the planning for you

The hardest part of posting weekly is deciding what to post about. The Nigerian calendar solves most of that problem if you plan a month ahead. Christmas and New Year, Easter, Sallah, Independence Day on 1 October, Valentine's Day, Mother's Day, Children's Day, Black Friday, your business anniversary — each one is a natural reason for an event post or an offer, and customers expect businesses to mark them.

Plan it in one sitting at the start of the month. Pick the two dates that matter for your business, decide the offer or the event for each, take the photos in advance, and write the posts before the week gets busy. A restaurant plans its Christmas lunch in November, not on 23 December. And use the right type for the job: an event post carries a date, an offer post carries a deadline.

## The posts that waste effort

Some posts look like work and achieve nothing. The copied-and-pasted Instagram post, full of hashtags and "link in bio": hashtags do nothing on Google, and "link in bio" points nowhere. Write for the searcher — what you offer, what it costs, where you are, how to reach you.

The post with a photo that does not match the words: new shoes with a photo of an old shopfront, a food discount with a blurry photo of a kitchen. Take a fresh photo for each important post.

The offer too complicated to understand in five seconds: "Buy two get one free on selected items excluding new arrivals, only on Tuesdays, for the first ten customers who spend above ₦50,000" is a puzzle, not a promotion. If you cannot explain the offer in one sentence, simplify it until you can.

The post with no price and no date: "new stock available, quality products, affordable prices" gives the reader nothing to act on. Every post needs at least one of the two — a price or a date — and the best posts have both.

And the motivational quote, the viral joke, the forwarded prayer. They get no calls and teach Google nothing about what you sell. Every post should help a customer decide to visit, call, or order. If it does neither, do not post it.

## A routine that fits a busy owner

You do not need a marketing team. You need a phone and a reminder. On Monday morning, spend ten minutes: pick one topic from your week, take one good photo, write the post, publish it. That single post keeps your listing fresh for the week.

On Wednesday or Thursday, add a second post only if you have something real to say. Do not force it. A quiet week with one good post is better than a week with three weak ones.

Once a month, plan one offer, tied to something real. Announce it a few days early with an update post, publish the offer itself, and after it ends, post a short thank-you to the customers who took part. Then check the numbers once a month: which posts got calls, which got views. If posts with prices get more calls, post more prices.

Mrs Okafor's restaurant posted its first update on a Monday: today's lunch menu, with prices, a photo of the actual rice, and "call to order". By Friday she had posted a weekend offer. By the end of the month her listing had four fresh posts, and the office workers searching at lunchtime were tapping her name instead of the restaurant two doors down. Her rice had not changed. Her prices had not changed. Her listing finally looked like a business that was open, busy, and worth visiting today.
