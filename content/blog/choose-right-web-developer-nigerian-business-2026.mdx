Every Nigerian business owner who has paid for a website has one of two stories: the developer who "delivered on time and the site still works," or the developer who disappeared after collecting 50% — leaving behind a template, a WhatsApp number that goes quiet, and a very expensive lesson.

The difference between those two stories is rarely talent. It is *vetting*. In 2026, hiring a web developer in Nigeria is easy; choosing the right one is a skill you can learn today. This guide gives you the exact framework — what to check, what to ask, and how to compare quotes — so your website becomes an asset, not an expensive regret.

---

## Step 1: Decide what kind of developer you actually need

The biggest mistake Nigerian business owners make is hiring a "developer" without knowing which type of developer their project demands. Not all web builders are the same.

**The three types you'll meet:**

1. **Freelancers.** Individuals who handle design-to-code alone. Best for landing pages, portfolios, simple business sites — but watch for single points of failure if they vanish.
2. **Small agencies.** Teams of designers, developers, and sometimes marketers. Best for complete sites plus ongoing support and marketing thinking.
3. **No-code specialists.** Builders using tools like Webflow and WordPress. Best for sites you'll need to update frequently without heavy coding.

Before you call anyone, write down your real need — a landing page, a full business site, e-commerce, or a booking/payment web app. Your need decides the developer type and the appropriate price.

---

## Step 2: Screen candidates with the 5-point checklist

When you shortlist developers or agencies, run them through this checklist. Do this *before* discussing price.

**1. Real portfolio or proof of work.**

Ask for links to live websites they built — not screenshots, live links you can open on your phone. Then test them on mobile: load speed, readability, and whether the WhatsApp button actually works.

**2. Tech stack awareness.**

You don't need to become technical, but a developer who builds modern, maintainable sites is a better long-term bet than one using fifteen-year-old methods. Ask simply: "What technology do you use to build business websites, and why?" A confident answer beats a vague "everything, sir."

**3. Communication quality.**

Send one question and time the reply. Fast, clear, professional responses predict how they'll handle a crisis. A developer who takes four days to answer your first message will take four days when your site is down.

**4. Local context.** Do they understand Nigerian customers? Do their sample sites have WhatsApp buttons, naira-friendly pricing, and mobile-first design?

**5. Maintenance offer.** Every website needs care. Does the developer offer a maintenance plan, or will the site be abandoned after handover? A good developer plans for month six, not just launch day.

---

## Step 3: Ask the questions that reveal the truth

Skip the soft questions; go straight to the ones that separate professionals from briefcase developers:

1. *"Can you show me 3 live websites you built for businesses like mine?"* (Live, not screenshots.)
2. *"How do you ensure my site loads fast on Nigerian networks?"*
3. *"Will my site work properly on mobile?"* (The answer should be an automatic "yes," not a "we'll try.")
4. *"What happens after you deliver — support and maintenance?"*
5. *"Who owns the website — domain, files, and content?"* (The answer should be you, in writing.)
6. *"How do you handle revisions during the project?"* (Clear scope beats "unlimited forever.")
7. *"Can you explain the payment schedule?"* (Deposit + milestones with final delivery; be wary of 100% upfront.)

Take notes. A developer who dodges these questions now will dodge your calls later.

---

## Step 4: Compare quotes the smart way — not just by price

Nigerian customers love a bargain, but a website is not a market. Any proper quote should include: design, development and page structure, mobile responsiveness, basic SEO setup, WhatsApp/contact integration, a handover so you can manage content, an agreed revision cycle, and at least a short period of after-launch support.

**Red flags in quotations:**

- "₦30,000 full website, no questions." Extremely low prices usually mean template-dumping.
- "100% upfront before we start." Respectable developers protect themselves with deposits, not full payment.
- Vague scope: "$5000 for everything" with no breakdown of what "everything" means.

Compare three quotes on scope, not just naira. The cheapest site that costs you three rebuilds is the most expensive choice you'll make.

---

## Step 5: Test the developer with a small trial task

Before committing to a full project, give the developer a small, paid trial — a single landing page or a minor redesign of one existing page. A tiny cost, an enormous information gain. Watch whether they deliver on the agreed date, whether quality matches their portfolio, and whether they communicate without hand-holding.

One small project tells you in days what a month-long contract will reveal in pain. In 2026, with AI tools in every freelancer's hands, the trial task matters even more — it reveals who delivers polished, personalised work and who just pastes AI output.

---

## Step 6: Choose based on the future, not just the present

Your website isn't a one-time purchase — it's the foundation your business will grow on. When choosing, think two years ahead:

- Will this developer add e-commerce, booking, or a blog later?
- Is the site built so *you* can update content yourself?
- Does the developer understand how the site supports your marketing (SEO, Google Business, WhatsApp)?

The businesses that thrive online in Nigeria aren't the flashiest — they're the ones who built on a foundation that could grow with them.

---

## Step 7: Protect yourself in the agreement

Even after you choose, protect the relationship:

- Put the scope, timeline, payment milestones, and ownership in writing — a simple contract or detailed message exchange works.
- Agree in advance what happens to your files and domain if the relationship ends.
- Keep copies of everything — passwords, hosting accounts, and your site's files.
- Plan for maintenance from day one so your site never becomes obsolete.

A written agreement isn't suspicion; it's professionalism. And it's the tool that turns a "developer-client" relationship into a partnership.
