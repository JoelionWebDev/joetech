Walk through any busy market in Lagos, Abuja, Port Harcourt, Ibadan or Onitsha and you will see it: women buying creams, soaps, perfumes, hair products and makeup, sometimes every week. Beauty is not a luxury purchase for most Nigerian customers — it is a regular part of life. That steady, repeat demand is why a cosmetics shop is one of the most popular retail businesses in the country, and why it can work even for a first-time business owner who starts small and learns the trade properly.

But a cosmetics shop is not simply "buy products and sell them." The difference between a shop that survives and one that closes within a year usually comes down to three things: selling genuine products, pricing honestly, and knowing exactly which customers you serve. Sell a fake cream that damages somebody's skin and word will travel faster than any advert. Price too high and customers will walk to the next shop. Stock the wrong products and your money will sit on shelves gathering dust.

This guide walks you through the business step by step: understanding the market, choosing your niche and location, working out startup costs, finding genuine suppliers, setting prices, arranging your shop, and marketing in a way that brings customers back again and again.

## 1. Understand the Cosmetics Market in Nigeria

The Nigerian beauty market covers a wide range of products: skincare (creams, lotions, soaps, serums and sunscreens), hair care (relaxers, shampoos, conditioners, hair oils, wigs and attachments), makeup (foundations, lipsticks, powders, mascaras and eyeshadows), perfumes and body sprays, and personal care items such as deodorants, toothpaste and sanitary products. Most successful small shops do not stock everything at first. They stock the fast-moving items their particular customers buy weekly, then expand.

Your customers are mostly women aged 18 to 55, but do not ignore men: male grooming products, perfumes, deodorants and beard care are a growing segment, and men often buy without haggling as much. Teenagers and students buy affordable makeup and hair products; working-class women buy mid-range skincare and perfumes; wealthier customers buy premium and imported brands. Salons, makeup artists and small resellers are also important customers — they buy in larger quantities and return regularly if your prices are fair.

Demand is steady all year, with peaks around December, wedding season, Valentine's Day and festive periods, when people buy gifts and prepare for events. New trends arrive constantly through Instagram, TikTok and Nollywood: a product can become popular overnight because an influencer used it, and just as quickly fade. That is why you should never tie up all your money in one trending product. Stock the proven sellers first and test new trends in small quantities.

The biggest risk in this business is fake products. Nigeria's markets contain many counterfeits of popular creams, perfumes and makeup, and some fake skincare products contain harmful substances. Selling them can damage your customers' skin, destroy your reputation and attract trouble from regulators. Your entire business depends on customers trusting that what you sell is genuine. Protect that trust from day one, even if genuine stock costs more and earns a smaller margin at first.

## 2. Choose Your Niche, Location and Shop Format

Before you buy anything, decide who you are serving. A clear niche makes buying, pricing and marketing far easier. Here are the niches that work well for beginners.

**General cosmetics shop.** A mix of skincare, makeup, hair products and perfumes for everyday customers. This is the most common model and works best in a busy market or residential area with heavy foot traffic. The range is wide, so you need good knowledge of many products.

**Skincare-focused shop.** Creams, soaps, serums, sunscreens and body lotions, including products for different skin types and tones. This niche attracts loyal customers who return for the same product every month, and it rewards a shop owner who can advise customers properly. It also carries the highest risk if you stock harmful or fake lightening products — avoid anything without proper labelling and NAFDAC registration.

**Makeup and beauty supply.** Foundations, lipsticks, lashes, brushes and palettes aimed at makeup artists, students and young women. This works well near salons, fashion areas and universities, and it benefits greatly from Instagram and TikTok marketing, including short videos showing products in use.

**Hair products shop.** Relaxers, shampoos, hair oils, wigs, attachments and accessories. Hair products sell fast and customers are loyal to brands, but wigs and human hair are expensive stock, so start with the affordable fast-movers and add premium hair only when you understand the demand.

**Wholesale and salon supply.** Selling in bulk to salons, makeup artists and smaller retailers. Margins per item are thinner, but volumes are larger and customers are regular. This suits owners with supplier contacts and enough capital to buy in cartons.

Location matters as much as products. The best locations are busy markets, streets near salons and boutiques, areas close to universities or offices, and residential neighbourhoods with good foot traffic. A shop inside a market benefits from existing crowds but faces fierce competition from neighbouring stalls. A shop on a main road costs more in rent but is easier to find and can build a distinct brand. A small shop in your neighbourhood can work if the area has enough residents and few competitors, but you will need stronger WhatsApp and social media marketing to pull customers in.

Visit your chosen area at different times of day. Count the foot traffic, note the existing cosmetics shops, and observe what they stock and how they price. If three shops on one street already sell the same products, you need a clear difference — better prices, genuine products, a wider range, or friendlier service — not just another identical shop. Also check rent, local government levies, and whether the shop has good light, ventilation and secure storage. Cosmetics suffer in heat and sunlight, so a hot, stuffy shop can damage your stock.

You can also start without a shop: selling from home on WhatsApp and Instagram, or from a small kiosk or a shared space. This keeps costs low while you learn which products sell. Many successful shop owners started this way and moved into a proper shop only after building a customer base. The trade-off is trust — customers prefer to see and smell a product before buying, so a physical presence, even a small one, helps a great deal.

## 3. Work Out Your Startup Costs and Business Plan

Write a simple one- or two-page plan: what you will sell, who your customers are, where your shop will be, how much it will cost, and how you will find customers. The plan forces you to face the numbers before spending.

Startup costs vary widely by location and scale. In a market stall or small shop, a lean start might look like this. Shop rent and deposit: in many Nigerian cities, a small shop costs ₦300,000 to ₦1,500,000 per year depending on location, with landlords often asking for one or two years upfront, plus an agency fee. Shelves, a counter, a glass display and a signboard: ₦150,000 to ₦400,000. Initial stock: ₦500,000 to ₦2 million for a modest range of fast-moving products, or ₦3 million to ₦5 million for a well-stocked shop. Business registration, branding and packaging: ₦50,000 to ₦150,000. A working float for restocking and daily expenses: at least ₦100,000 to ₦200,000.

Altogether, a small cosmetics shop can be started for roughly ₦1 million to ₦3 million in a modest location, while a well-stocked shop in a prime area may need ₦5 million to ₦10 million. If your budget is smaller, start from home or a shared space with ₦300,000 to ₦500,000 of fast-moving stock, prove the demand, then move into a shop. Do not spend all your money on stock and leave nothing for rent, transport and restocking — the shop needs cash to keep shelves full after the first sales.

Plan your money in three buckets: setup (rent, shelves, registration), stock, and working capital for at least two to three months. Cosmetics is a cash-flow business: you sell a cream today, use the money to restock tomorrow, and the profit accumulates slowly. Keep a separate business account or a dedicated wallet, record every sale and every purchase, and at the end of each week check which products sold and which did not. Products that do not sell in a month are tying up your money — discount them, bundle them, or stop restocking them.

## 4. Find Genuine Suppliers and Buy Your First Stock

Suppliers make or break this business. The main sources are major markets — Idumota and Balogun in Lagos, Onitsha Main Market, Ariaria in Aba, and the big markets in Kano and Abuja — as well as authorised distributors and direct agents of known brands. Distributors usually cost slightly more per item but offer genuine products, receipts and sometimes credit. Market traders may be cheaper, but you must know how to spot fakes and you must build a relationship with a trader you trust.

When choosing a supplier, visit in person first. Buy a small quantity, check the products carefully, and sell them before committing to a large order. Check the packaging, spelling, batch numbers, expiry dates and NAFDAC numbers where applicable. Compare the price with two or three other suppliers — a price far below the market rate is a warning sign, not a bargain. Ask where the supplier gets the product and whether they can supply the same brand consistently. A supplier who disappears after one delivery leaves you with customers you cannot serve.

For your first stock, focus on fast-moving products with proven demand: popular body creams and lotions, bathing soaps, shampoos and conditioners, hair oils, deodorants and body sprays, lipsticks and foundations in shades that suit Nigerian skin tones, and perfumes at different price points. Buy small quantities across many products rather than a large quantity of one product, so you learn what your particular customers prefer. Buy a few premium items too, for customers who want them, but do not let expensive stock dominate your shelves at the start.

Handle stock properly. Store creams and makeup away from direct sunlight and heat, keep the shop clean and dust-free, and arrange products by category so customers can find things easily. Check expiry dates every month and move near-expiry products to the front, or discount them before they expire. Never sell an expired product — it can harm a customer and end your business. Keep a stock book or spreadsheet recording what you bought, at what price, from whom, and when it sold. This record is what lets you reorder the right products at the right time.

## 5. Price Your Products for Profit and Trust

Pricing in cosmetics is delicate. Customers compare prices constantly, and a difference of ₦500 can send them to the next shop. Yet pricing too low leaves no profit and makes customers suspicious that the product is fake. Your price must cover the cost of the product, transport, rent, packaging and your time, plus a reasonable profit.

A common approach is a markup of 20 to 40 percent on the cost price for most fast-moving items, with higher margins on premium and imported products and thinner margins on very competitive items such as popular soaps and body sprays. For example, a cream that costs you ₦5,000 might sell for ₦6,500 to ₦7,500; a perfume that costs ₦12,000 might sell for ₦15,000 to ₦18,000. These are planning guides, not rules — check the going price in your area for each product and position yourself fairly. Some shops charge slightly less on popular items to attract customers, then earn more on less common products.

Be transparent about prices. Display prices clearly on shelves or in your WhatsApp catalogue so customers do not have to haggle over every item. Many Nigerian customers will still negotiate, especially for bulk purchases, so decide your lowest acceptable price in advance and do not go below it in the heat of a sale. Offer fair discounts for bulk buyers such as salons and makeup artists — for example, 5 to 10 percent off when they buy a carton or a set quantity — and keep a simple price list you can update as supplier prices change.

Accept different payment methods: cash, bank transfer and POS. Many customers now prefer transfer, so display your account details clearly and confirm each payment before handing over the product. Give a receipt for larger purchases. Keep your pricing consistent on WhatsApp, Instagram and in the shop — nothing frustrates a customer more than seeing one price online and another in the shop.

## 6. Arrange the Shop and Attract Customers

A cosmetics shop sells with its eyes first. Arrange products neatly by category — skincare together, hair care together, makeup together — with popular items at eye level and prices visible. Keep the glass display clean, the shelves dusted, and the shop smelling pleasant. Good lighting matters: customers need to see true colours, especially for foundations and lipsticks. If you can, keep a small mirror and testers for customers to try shades, and always have tissue and wipes available. A tidy, bright shop feels trustworthy; a dusty, crowded one makes customers doubt the products inside.

Register the business with the CAC and open a separate bank account, so you can issue receipts and be taken seriously by suppliers and corporate buyers. A Google Business Profile and a WhatsApp Business account with a catalogue are your most important marketing tools. Post new arrivals, restocks and short videos of products on WhatsApp status and Instagram regularly, especially in the evenings when people browse. Show real products, real prices and real results — not borrowed photos of items you do not stock.

Build loyalty deliberately. Remember regular customers' names and their usual products. Offer a small gift or discount after several purchases. Create a simple WhatsApp broadcast list — with customers' permission — for new arrivals and price updates, but do not spam it daily. Partner with nearby salons, makeup artists and boutiques: they can recommend your shop, and you can recommend them. Sell honestly: if a product does not suit a customer's skin type, say so and suggest a better option. That honesty loses one sale and wins a customer for years.

## 7. Run the Shop Day to Day and Grow

Open on time, every day, including Saturdays, when many customers shop. Greet people warmly, let them browse without pressure, and answer questions about products honestly — including how to use them and what skin types they suit. Learn your products: read labels, watch brand tutorials, and know which cream is for dry skin, which shampoo is for relaxed hair, and which foundation shades match different skin tones. Knowledge is what turns a shopkeeper into a trusted adviser.

Track your numbers weekly: total sales, best-selling products, products that are not moving, and cash in hand. Reorder fast-movers before they run out — an empty shelf loses a sale and sends a regular customer elsewhere. Avoid overstocking slow items, and be careful with credit: many small shops fail because they gave goods on credit and never got paid. If you give credit at all, limit it to known, trusted customers, record it in writing, and set a clear repayment date.

Grow step by step. Add a new product line only when customers keep asking for it. Consider delivery within your area for regular customers, a small online store or Instagram shop, and eventually a second location or a wholesale arm supplying salons. Watch out for the common mistakes: buying fake stock to chase a low price, copying every trend, overstocking slow products, and mixing business money with personal spending. Start with proven products, sell only what is genuine, price fairly, treat customers well, and your cosmetics shop can grow from a small stall into a trusted beauty destination in your area.
