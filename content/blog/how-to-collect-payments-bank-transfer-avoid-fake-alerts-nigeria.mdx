Bank transfer is the most common way Nigerians pay for goods and services. A customer in Lekki orders shoes on WhatsApp, a client in Abuja pays for a cake, a shop in Ikeja settles a supplier invoice — and in each case the money moves by transfer. It is fast, it works across all banks, and almost every customer already knows how to do it.

It is also the payment method most abused by fraudsters. The fake alert scam has cost Nigerian small businesses millions of naira: a customer shows you a debit alert or a receipt on their phone, you release the goods, and later you discover no money ever entered your account. By then the customer is gone, your goods are gone, and there is little you can do.

This guide shows you how to collect payments by bank transfer safely, how to spot a fake alert in seconds, and how to set up payment systems that protect your business without driving honest customers away.

## How the Fake Alert Scam Works

To stop a scam, you must understand how it works. The fake alert scam comes in several forms, and new ones appear every year.

The most common form is the fake SMS alert. The fraudster sends you a text message that looks exactly like a bank alert: "Your account has been credited with ₦25,000." The message may even come from a sender name that looks like your bank. But it is not from your bank. It is a message the fraudster typed and sent from a phone or an app, and no money has moved. If you check your real bank app or your account balance, the money is not there.

A second form is the fake receipt. The customer shows you a screenshot of a successful transfer on their phone, or sends it to you on WhatsApp. The screenshot looks real — it has the bank logo, the amount, and a transaction reference — but it was created with an editing app or a fake alert generator. Some fraudsters even use a real transfer screen, then cancel the transaction before it completes.

A third form is the delayed or reversed transfer. The customer genuinely starts a transfer, shows you the "processing" screen, and asks you to release the goods quickly because they are in a hurry. Later, the transfer fails, is reversed, or was sent to a wrong account on purpose. You have already handed over the goods.

A fourth form targets sellers who are in a rush. The fraudster orders during a busy period — a Saturday market rush, a festive season, a big delivery day — when you are distracted and your staff are under pressure. They count on you glancing at a phone screen instead of checking your own bank app. Busy sellers are the easiest victims, so your payment checks must be simple enough to follow even when you are rushed.

Knowing these tricks is half the defence. The other half is a system that makes it impossible for a fake alert to get past you, no matter how convincing it looks.

## The One Rule That Stops Almost Every Fake Alert

There is one rule that, if you follow it every single time, will stop almost every fake alert scam: never release goods or start work until you have confirmed the money in your own bank account, using your own bank app or your own USSD balance check.

Not the customer's phone. Not a screenshot. Not a text message. Your own bank app, opened by you, showing your own balance or your own transaction history. That is the only confirmation that matters.

This sounds simple, but it is hard to follow under pressure. A customer is standing in your shop, they are in a hurry, they show you an alert, and other customers are waiting. It feels rude to make them wait while you open your app. Do it anyway. A genuine customer will understand and wait the thirty seconds it takes. A fraudster will become angry, try to rush you, or suddenly remember an urgent appointment. That reaction is itself a warning sign.

Make this rule visible. Put a small notice in your shop and on your WhatsApp Business profile: "Goods are released only after payment is confirmed in our account." When the rule is public, customers expect it, and you do not have to argue about it each time. Train your staff on the same rule, and make it clear that no member of staff may release goods on the strength of a customer's phone screen alone.

If your bank app is slow or your network is poor, keep a second way to confirm: your bank's USSD code for a balance check, or SMS alerts from your own bank that you have set up yourself. The key point is that the confirmation comes from your bank to you, not from the customer to you.

## How to Check a Payment Properly in Under a Minute

A proper payment check takes less than a minute once it becomes a habit. Build it into your sales routine so it happens automatically.

Step one: ask the customer for the exact amount, the sender's name, and the bank they transferred from. Write it down or type it into your notes. This makes it harder for a fraudster to change their story, and it gives you something to compare against your bank record.

Step two: open your own bank app and check your recent transactions, not just your balance. A balance can be confusing if you had other money in the account. Your transaction history shows the sender's name, the amount, and the time. Match all three against what the customer told you. If the name does not match, or the amount is different, do not release the goods.

Step three: if the amount is large — say ₦50,000 or more — wait for the money to show as available, not just pending. Some transfers show as pending for a few minutes, especially between different banks or at weekends. For a large order, it is worth waiting five minutes. Tell the customer honestly: "I can see it is pending, give me five minutes for it to clear, then I will pack your order." Honest customers accept this.

Step four: for orders you deliver, confirm payment before you hand the goods to the dispatch rider, not after. Once the rider leaves, you have lost control. For online orders, confirm payment before you start making a custom product, such as a cake or a tailored dress. A deposit that never arrives is the most common loss for makers of custom goods.

## Set Up a Business Account and Separate Your Money

Many small business owners still receive business payments into a personal account. That is risky for several reasons, and a business account is one of the best protections you can give yourself.

A business account, registered with your CAC business name, looks more professional and builds trust. Customers are more comfortable paying "Ada's Fashion Hub" than paying a personal name they do not recognise. It also makes it easier to track your business income separately from your personal spending, which matters when you want a loan, when you file taxes, or when you simply want to know whether your business is making a profit.

Open a dedicated account for the business and use it only for business payments. Do not mix it with your salary, your personal transfers, or money from family. When your business money is separate, a fake alert is easier to spot, because every credit in that account should match a real order in your log.

You can also open accounts with two different banks. Use one as your main receiving account and keep the other as a backup. If one bank's app is down or its network is slow, you can ask the customer to transfer to the second account. Having a backup also helps when a customer claims they transferred to the wrong account — you can check both quickly.

Tell your customers your account details in a fixed format, saved as a note on your phone, so you always send the same details: account name, account number, and bank. This avoids errors, and it avoids the situation where a staff member gives out the wrong number. Never change your account details by voice call alone; send them in writing on WhatsApp, so there is a record.

## Use Payment Links and POS Options to Reduce Risk

Bank transfer is not your only option, and adding safer payment options reduces your exposure to fake alerts.

Payment links from Paystack or Flutterwave are a strong choice for online sellers. You create a link for a product or an amount, send it to the customer, and they pay with their card, transfer, or bank app through the payment platform. You receive a real confirmation from Paystack or Flutterwave, not from the customer, and the money lands in your account with a clear record. The platform charges a small fee, usually around 1.5 percent, but that fee buys you protection and a proper receipt for every sale.

A POS terminal is useful if you have a physical shop. When a customer pays by card on your own terminal, the money goes directly into your account and you get a printed receipt. Never let a customer pay on their own POS device and show you the screen — that is the same trap as a fake alert. Your terminal, your receipt, your confirmation.

For large orders, ask for a deposit before you start work, and the balance before delivery. A typical split is 50 percent to start and 50 percent on delivery, or 60 percent upfront for custom work where you must buy materials. State the split clearly when you quote the price, so the customer is not surprised. A customer who refuses to pay any deposit for a custom order is a warning sign.

Opay and Palmpay are popular with many Nigerian customers, especially for smaller amounts, and they can be legitimate payment options. The same rule applies: confirm the money in your own Opay or Palmpay app before you release goods. Do not accept a screenshot from the customer's app as proof.

## Warning Signs That a Payment May Be Fake

Fraudsters give themselves away if you know what to watch for. These warning signs do not prove a payment is fake on their own, but two or three together should make you check very carefully.

The customer is in an unusual hurry. They say they are late for a flight, their driver is waiting outside, or they must leave in two minutes. Genuine customers are sometimes in a hurry, but fraudsters use urgency as a weapon, because a rushed seller skips checks. Slow down deliberately when someone rushes you.

The customer is buying a high-value item quickly with no questions. A genuine buyer of a ₦150,000 phone or a ₦80,000 dress usually asks questions, checks the quality, and negotiates. A fraudster wants the transaction over fast, before you have time to think.

The alert arrives before you expect it, or the amount is slightly different from what you agreed. If you agreed ₦25,000 and the alert says ₦25,500, or the alert arrives the moment the customer taps their phone without any delay, be suspicious. Real transfers take a few seconds at least, and the amount should match exactly.

The customer refuses to wait while you check your app. They become angry, they say they have done this many times, or they offer to leave the goods and come back later. A genuine customer who has truly paid has nothing to lose by waiting thirty seconds.

The sender's name does not match the customer's name, and they have no good explanation. Sometimes a genuine customer pays from a spouse's or a friend's account, and that is fine — but they should tell you in advance, and the name should still appear in your transaction history. If the name is completely different and the customer cannot explain why, wait until you can verify the payment another way.



## What to Do If You Have Already Been Scammed

If you discover that a payment was fake after you have released the goods, act quickly. Speed matters, because the fraudster will disappear fast.

First, gather your evidence. Screenshot the fake alert or receipt the customer sent you, save the WhatsApp chat, note the customer's phone number, name, and any address or delivery details you have, and record the date, time, and amount. Do not delete anything, even if you feel embarrassed. This evidence is what the bank and the police will ask for.

Second, report the matter to your bank immediately. Visit your branch or call your bank's customer care line and explain that you received a fake credit alert. If the fraudster used a real account, the bank can place a hold or start a trace. If the money was transferred to a real account and then withdrawn, recovery is hard, but a quick report gives you the best chance.

Third, report to the police and to the Economic and Financial Crimes Commission if the amount is large. Bring your evidence and a written statement. Many sellers do not report small losses because they feel it will lead nowhere, but reports help the authorities track repeat offenders, and a police report is often needed if you later need to make an insurance claim or a bank dispute.

Fourth, warn other sellers. Share the fraudster's phone number and method — without sharing anything you cannot prove — in your traders' association group or your market group on WhatsApp. Fraudsters move from shop to shop, and a warning can save the next seller.

Finally, review what went wrong and fix it. Was it a busy day? Did a staff member release the goods? Did you skip the check because the customer was a regular? Change the system, not just your intentions. Put the confirmation rule in writing, train your staff again, and consider moving more sales to payment links, where the platform confirms the payment for you.


## Frequently Asked Questions

### How can I tell a fake bank alert from a real one?

You cannot reliably tell by looking at the customer's phone. Fake alerts are designed to look identical to real ones. The only reliable check is your own bank app or your own balance check. Open your app, check your transaction history, and match the sender's name, amount, and time.

### Should I accept payment by transfer for large orders?

Yes, but take extra care. For orders above ₦50,000, wait until the money shows as available in your account, not just pending, before you release the goods. For very large orders, consider a payment link or a direct bank deposit at the branch, where the teller can confirm it.

### What should I do if a customer's transfer is delayed?

Ask the customer to wait a few minutes while the transfer clears, and check your app again. Transfers between different banks can take a few minutes, especially at weekends or during bank maintenance. If the money has still not arrived after 30 minutes, ask the customer to contact their bank and share the transaction reference, so you can follow up with your own bank.

### Can a customer reverse a transfer after I have delivered?

A completed transfer cannot be reversed by the customer alone, but a transfer that is still processing can fail, and a payment made by mistake can be recalled through the banks. This is why you should confirm the money is available in your account, not just that a transfer was started, before you release goods.

### Is it safe to share my account number with customers?

Yes. Your account number and account name are meant to be shared so customers can pay you. Never share your PIN, your bank app password, your OTP, or your BVN with anyone, including someone who claims to be from your bank. Your bank will never ask for those details.

## What to Do Next

1. Write down your payment rule today — goods are released only after you confirm the money in your own bank app — and share it with your staff and pin it on your WhatsApp Business profile.
2. Open a dedicated business account if you do not have one, set up your own bank SMS alerts, and create a simple payment log for every sale.
3. Set up a payment link with Paystack or Flutterwave for your online orders, so the platform confirms each payment for you and you have a clear record of every sale.
