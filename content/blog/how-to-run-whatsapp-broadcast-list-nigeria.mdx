Funke sells Ankara and ready-to-wear from a small shop on Herbert Macaulay Way in Yaba. One Saturday in October, she sent a broadcast about new lace fabrics to 180 contacts. By Monday, three people had replied, one had ordered, and eleven had blocked her. "I have 180 people on my list," she said. "Why did only three reply?"

I asked her one question: how many of those 180 people had actually saved her number? She did not know. We checked together. WhatsApp only delivers a broadcast to people who have your number saved in their phone. Her real list was not 180. It was closer to 40 — old customers, old classmates, and people she had chatted with once about a price and never heard from again.

That is the whole game with broadcast lists in Nigeria. Anybody can tap "New broadcast" and add 256 names. The hard part is getting real customers to save your number, and then sorting those people so you stop sending the same message to a woman who bought from you last week and a stranger who asked for a price in March and vanished.

## The save is the bottleneck

WhatsApp's rule is simple and it is not negotiable: a broadcast message only reaches contacts who have saved your number. No save, no delivery. No delivery, no sale. It does not matter how good your photo is or how fair your price is.

Most sellers treat this rule as a small technical detail. It is not. It is the business. A list of 2,000 unsaved numbers is worth less than a list of 60 saved ones, because the 60 will see your message and the 2,000 will not.

## Give them a reason, at the exact moment they care

Nobody saves a number because a seller said "please save my number." People save numbers because the number is about to be useful to them. Your job is to catch the moment when the customer is already thinking about the next time she will need you, and attach the save to that moment.

The strongest moment is right after a purchase. The customer has just paid. She is happy. She is already wondering when the next style will arrive. That is when you say it, in one sentence, with the benefit attached: "Save my number as Funke Fabrics so I can send you the new lace when it lands on Thursday, before it finishes." The customer hears a benefit — first look at new stock — and the save takes three seconds.

The second strongest moment is right after delivery. Chidi, who sells phone accessories at Computer Village, sends a short message the evening a customer's order arrives: "Your power bank don reach? Save my number as Chidi Accessories — I will send you the new Oraimo stock on Friday before I post it anywhere." Customers who have just received what they paid for are in a generous mood. Many of them save the number on the spot.

The third moment is when a customer asks a question but does not buy — the price of a wig, delivery to Ajah, whether size 42 is available. Before the chat ends, give her a reason to stay connected: "Save my number as Adaeze Hair — I will message you when the bone-straight wigs come back, so you do not miss them." No pressure. Just a door she can walk back through.

Notice what all three moments have in common. The ask is short. It names your business, so the contact is easy to find later. And it promises something concrete — new stock on Thursday, first look on Friday, a message when the wig returns. "Save my number for updates" promises nothing. Nobody saves a number for "updates."

## Your first 30 days: from 20 to 200

A new list feels empty, and that emptiness makes sellers do desperate things — buying phone numbers, scraping groups, adding every contact on their phone. Do not. A list built on strangers will get your number reported, and a reported number gets restricted. Build it the slow way, for 30 days, and the list will be yours.

Start with what you already have. Open your WhatsApp chats and write down every person who bought from you in the last three months, every person who asked a serious question, and every person who collected an order from your shop. That is your seed list — usually 20 to 40 names. Message each of them personally, not as a broadcast, and ask them to save your number, with the benefit attached. Half of them will do it. You now have a real list of 15 to 20 people who will actually receive your messages.

In the second week, make the ask part of every sale. Every customer who pays, collects, or receives a delivery hears the one-sentence ask before they leave. Serve ten customers a day, six save your number, and you have added 30 names in a week. Put the same ask on your shop wall, on a card inside every delivery bag, and in your Instagram bio. A customer who sees the number three times is far more likely to save it than one who hears it once.

In the third week, ask your best customers for one introduction each. Not a blast to their whole contact list — one person. "If you have a sister or a friend who buys lace, share my number with her." A referral from a happy customer arrives with trust already attached, and the new person saves your number because her friend told her to.

By the fourth week, count what you have. Ten customers a day for 30 days, half saving your number, puts you at 150 names before referrals. Add the referrals and the people who found you through your bio and your shop sign, and 200 is not a fantasy. It is arithmetic.

## Where new names come from without buying numbers

Buying a list of phone numbers is the fastest way to kill a broadcast list. Those people never asked to hear from you, they never saved your number, so your messages never reach them anyway — and the ones who do see your message report it as spam. Enough reports and your number gets restricted, and then even your real customers stop receiving your messages. There is no shortcut here. There are only honest sources, and there are more of them than most sellers use.

Your delivery bag is one. A small card inside every order — "Save our number for new stock alerts" — costs almost nothing and reaches every buyer. Your shop sign is another: a line under your business name, "WhatsApp: 0803 456 7890", turns every passer-by into a possible save. Your status is a third. Once a week, post: "New lace lands Friday. My broadcast list sees it first. Save my number and send 'ADD ME'." People who reply with "ADD ME" have asked to join. That is permission, and permission is the whole difference between a customer and a complaint. And your existing customers are the best source of all. A woman who bought a dress from you and loved it will tell her sister, her colleague, her church member. Make it easy for her: "Share my number with anyone who asks where you got it."

## Splitting the list: buyers, enquirers, cold contacts

Once the list passes 50 names, one big list stops working. A buyer who paid ₦45,000 for a dress last week does not need the same message as a woman who asked for a price in February and never replied. Split the list into three buckets and the messages start landing.

The buyers bucket is people who have paid you. They already trust you, they know your prices, and they have seen your quality. They get first look at new stock, early access to offers, and a thank-you after delivery. They do not need convincing. They need a reason to come back. In WhatsApp Business, label them "Buyer" and add a note of what they bought — "Buyer, lace, ₦45,000, March." That note tells you what to send them next.

The enquirers bucket is people who asked a real question — about price, size, delivery, availability — but never bought. They are warm, not cold. They get the useful thing and the interesting thing: a photo of the new stock, a short answer to the question they asked, a reminder that the size they wanted is back. The goal is not to push them. The goal is to stay in front of them until the day they are ready. When they buy, move them to the buyers bucket the same day. A list that never moves people lies to you about who your customers are.

The cold contacts bucket is people who went quiet — they asked once, months ago, and never replied again, or bought once, a year ago, and disappeared. Do not delete them immediately, and do not keep messaging them like buyers. Send them one message, something genuinely useful, not a sale, and watch. If they reply, they move back to enquirers. If they stay silent for another 60 days, remove them. A list of 200 where 120 people open your messages will sell more than a list of 400 where 300 never see them.

WhatsApp Business makes the splitting easy. Labels cost nothing: "Buyer", "Enquirer", "Cold". Create three broadcast lists with matching names and send each one its own version of the message. A buyers' message can say "before it finishes" because buyers know your stock moves fast. An enquirers' message should say "the size you asked about is back" because that is personal to them. A cold contacts' message should be short and useful, with no pressure at all. Three versions of one idea takes ten extra minutes, and it is the difference between a message that feels written for you and a message that feels blasted at everyone.

## Keeping the list honest

A list rots if you never clean it. Once a month, spend twenty minutes on it. Check which numbers never receive your messages — WhatsApp shows a single grey tick, which usually means the person never saved your number. Move them out of your broadcast lists until they save you. Remove anyone who asked to stop, immediately and politely, with no argument and no "just one more message." And move people between buckets the moment their situation changes: an enquirer who buys becomes a buyer that day, a buyer who goes quiet for six months becomes cold.

The sellers with the strongest broadcast lists in Lagos are not the ones with the biggest lists. They are the ones who know every name on theirs — who bought what, who asked about what, who went quiet and why. Funke rebuilt her list the slow way after that Saturday in October. She started at 40 real saves, asked every customer for a month, and split the list into buyers and enquirers. By December her list was 210 names. Her next broadcast about Christmas lace got 47 replies and 19 orders. Same phone, same shop, same fabrics. The only thing that changed was the list — and the fact that the people on it had chosen to be there.
