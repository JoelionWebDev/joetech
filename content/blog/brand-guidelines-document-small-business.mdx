If you look at your business on WhatsApp, Instagram and your website, do they feel like the same business? For many small businesses, the answer is no. The logo is one colour here and another colour there. The tone is formal in one place and playful in another. Nothing is wrong with any single post or page on its own, but together they do not add up to a clear picture.

A brand guidelines document fixes that. It is a short reference that records how your brand should look and sound, so that anyone who works on your business, from a designer to a social media handler to a printer, makes the same choices. It is not a large corporate manual. For a small business, a few clear pages are enough, and the document pays for itself the first time it stops a costly reprint or a confusing post.

If you are still working out what your brand stands for, start with our guide to [building a brand identity](/blog/brand-identity-building). This post is about the next step: writing those decisions down in a document you can actually use.

## What a Brand Guidelines Document Actually Is

Think of it as the instruction sheet for your brand. It answers the questions people keep asking you: which logo should I use, which colours are ours, which font should this be in, and how should we speak to customers?

Without it, every new person guesses. One designer picks a bright blue because it looks nice. Another picks a darker blue because it looks serious. A staff member writes captions in slang while your website speaks formally. Customers may not be able to explain what feels off, but they notice the inconsistency, and inconsistency makes a business feel less reliable.

With it, decisions are quick. Nobody has to ask you about every small detail, and your business starts to look settled and professional, even if you are still small.

## The Sections Every Small Business Version Needs

You do not need dozens of pages. Cover these sections well, and you have a working document.

### 1. Logo rules

Show your main logo, and any approved variations, such as a version for dark backgrounds and a simple one-colour version. State clearly which version to use where, and what not to do: do not stretch the logo, do not change its colours, do not place it on a busy background where it cannot be read, and do not add effects like shadows unless they are part of the approved design. Include a note about clear space, meaning the logo should always have breathing room around it and should not be crowded by other text or images.

### 2. Colours

List your main brand colours and your supporting colours, with their exact colour codes so a designer or printer can match them. Name each colour in plain language too, for example "deep blue" or "warm gold", so anyone can recognise it. Explain where each colour belongs: one colour for headings, one for buttons or highlights, and neutral colours for backgrounds and body text. When colours are written down, your website, flyers and social posts stop drifting into different shades.

### 3. Fonts

Name one font for headings and one for body text, and say what to use if those fonts are not available. Keep it to two fonts at most. Too many fonts make a design look scattered, while two consistent fonts make everything feel connected, from your website to a price list. If your brand uses a font that is hard to find, note a common alternative that looks close enough for everyday documents.

### 4. Tone of voice

Write down how your business speaks. Are you friendly and direct, or formal and reserved? Give a few example sentences in your brand voice, and a few examples of what you would never say. This section matters more than most people expect, because customers meet your tone in captions, replies, emails and website copy. A short list of do and do not examples helps anyone writing for you to sound like you, not like themselves.

### 5. Photos and imagery

State what kind of images fit your brand. Do you prefer bright, real photos of your work and your team, or clean product shots on plain backgrounds? Note anything to avoid, such as dark or blurry images, or images that have nothing to do with your business. This section saves a lot of back and forth when someone is choosing pictures for a post or a page.

### 6. Templates and everyday items

Include the everyday pieces people actually need: a social media post layout, a flyer layout, a business card, and a simple letter or invoice header. You do not need to design all of these at once. Start with the ones you use most, and add the rest over time. Templates are where a guidelines document earns its keep, because they turn good intentions into consistent output without extra effort each time.

## How to Use It Day to Day

A guidelines document only works if people can find it and use it. Keep it in one place that everyone who works with you can reach, such as a shared folder, and send it to any new designer, printer or assistant before they start work. When you brief someone, point to the relevant section instead of explaining everything from scratch.

Use it yourself as a check before publishing. Before a post or flyer goes out, ask: is this our logo, our colours, our fonts and our tone? That thirty second check catches most problems.

Review the document from time to time. Businesses change. You may add a service, change a colour, or shift your tone as you grow. When that happens, update the document and let everyone know.

## Mistakes That Make the Document Useless

The most common mistake is making it too long. A document nobody reads helps nobody. Keep it short, visual and practical, with examples rather than long explanations.

Another mistake is writing rules without examples. Saying "use our colours correctly" means little on its own. Showing the correct use beside the incorrect use makes the rule obvious at a glance.

A third mistake is keeping the document to yourself. If only you have it, it cannot guide anyone else. Share it with the people who create things for your business, and treat it as a working tool, not a private file.

Finally, do not confuse a guidelines document with a logo alone. A logo is one piece. The document covers how the whole brand behaves: colours, fonts, tone, images and layouts together.

## A Simple Checklist to Build Yours

- Write down your main logo and its approved variations, with clear do and do not rules.
- List your brand colours with their exact codes and where each one is used.
- Choose one heading font and one body font, plus a fallback for each.
- Describe your tone of voice with example sentences.
- Note the kind of photos and images that fit your brand.
- Create templates for the items you use most, such as social posts and flyers.
- Store the document where your team and suppliers can reach it, and update it when your brand changes.

Work through that list once, and you will have a document that most small businesses never get around to making, even though it is one of the cheapest ways to look more professional.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I really need one if my business is small?**

Yes, and arguably more so. A small business has fewer chances to make an impression, so each one needs to count. A guidelines document helps a small team look consistent without hiring a full marketing department.

**How long should it be?**

For most small businesses, a few pages are enough. Cover logo, colours, fonts, tone, images and templates clearly, with examples. If it grows beyond what anyone will read, shorten it.

**Who should write it?**

Ideally the person or agency that designed your brand, because they already made these decisions. If your brand grew without a designer, you can still write it yourself by recording the choices you already make most often, then tightening them into rules.

**How often should I update it?**

Review it whenever your business changes in a visible way, such as a new service, a new colour, or a new way of speaking to customers. A quick review once or twice a year is enough for most small businesses.
