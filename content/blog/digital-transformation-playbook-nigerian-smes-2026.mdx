Dunni bakes cakes from her kitchen in Lekki. For two years, orders came through WhatsApp, prices lived in her head, and every December she worked until 2 a.m. because she could not tell which orders were confirmed, which were paid, and which were just people asking questions. In January, she decided to change that with ninety days and a plan she could follow one week at a time.

This is that plan, written the way Dunni followed it. Three phases of about a month each, and each phase ends with a test. If the phase passes its test, you spend money on the next one. If it fails, you stay put and fix it. That single rule is what separates a plan from a shopping spree.

The costs below are honest ranges for a small Nigerian business in 2026. They assume you do the work you can do yourself and hire out only the jobs that genuinely need a professional.

## Phase One: Get Your House in Order (Weeks 1–4, ₦0–₦40,000)

Nothing in Phase One is visible to your customers. That is the point. You are fixing the inside of the business before you invite more people in.

Week one is mapping. Sit down with a notebook and write out, step by step, how an order becomes money today. A customer messages. You reply. You quote a price. They ask questions. They pay, or they don't. You bake. They collect or a rider delivers. Write every step, including the ones that go wrong — the customer who asks the price three times, the one who pays half and disappears, the order you forgot because it was buried in a chat. Dunni's map took one evening and filled four pages. It showed her that she spent about two hours a day just answering the same five questions.

Week two is the price list and the answers. Write down your real prices, every product, every size, delivery fees included, and put them in one document. Then write short, friendly answers to the five questions you answer most, and save them as quick replies in WhatsApp Business. This is do-it-yourself work, and it costs nothing. Dunni's quick replies cut her daily messaging time from two hours to about forty minutes in the first week.

Week three is records. Open a Google Sheet and make three tabs: orders, money in, money out. Every order gets one row: customer name, what they ordered, price, deposit, balance, date needed, status. Every evening, spend ten minutes updating it. This is the least glamorous week of the plan and the most important. A business that cannot see its own numbers cannot make a single good decision, and no tool you buy later will fix numbers that were never recorded. Cost: nothing. A Google account is free, and Sheets works on a phone.

Week four is the tidy-up: a proper business email at your own domain, a Google Business Profile so customers can find you on Maps, and a backup. The domain and email cost roughly ₦15,000 to ₦40,000 for the year. The backup costs nothing — turn on automatic WhatsApp and photo backup and check that it actually runs.

The test at the end of Phase One is simple and strict. For seven days straight, every order must be in the sheet, every price must come from the price list, and every repeat question must be answered with a quick reply. If you manage that for a full week, Phase One passes. If orders are still living only in your head or in scattered chats, Phase One is not done, and you do not spend a kobo on Phase Two. Dunni failed this test in her first attempt — by Wednesday she was back to quoting prices from memory. She repeated week three, and the second time it stuck.

Do-it-yourself in this phase: everything. There is nothing here that needs a professional, and hiring one now would be paying someone to organise a mess you have not looked at yet.

## Phase Two: Let Customers Find You, Order, and Pay (Weeks 5–8, ₦150,000–₦400,000)

Phase Two is the one customers see, and it is where most of the money goes. It is also the phase where businesses waste the most, so the order of work matters.

Week five is the website, and this is the first job to hire out. A simple, fast website — home, menu or catalogue, prices, order form, contact — from a competent developer costs roughly ₦150,000 to ₦350,000 in Lagos. What you are paying for is not a pretty page. You are paying for a page that loads fast on cheap data, shows your prices clearly, and sends the customer straight to WhatsApp or a payment link. Before you hire anyone, write one page of notes: what you sell, your prices, your delivery areas, and three photos of your best work. If a developer quotes you ₦1.5 million for a first website, walk away. That is a phase-three budget for a phase-two job.

Week six is payments. Set up a payment link with Paystack or Flutterwave so a customer can pay a deposit without messaging you first, and test it yourself with a small payment of ₦500. Integration on a simple website costs roughly ₦30,000 to ₦80,000 if your developer handles it, and the platforms take about 1.5% plus ₦100 per transaction. Keep bank transfer as an option, because many customers still prefer it, but the link is what lets a customer pay at 11 p.m. while you sleep.

Week seven is the catalogue and the Google Business Profile. Photograph your ten best products in daylight, write one honest line about each, and put them on the website and in your WhatsApp catalogue. Then finish your Google Business Profile: correct address, correct hours, ten photos, and ask your five happiest customers for a review. This is do-it-yourself work, and it is the cheapest marketing in the plan.

Week eight is the dress rehearsal. For one full week, run every order through the new path: customer finds you, sees the price, pays the deposit through the link or transfer, and the order lands in your sheet. Write down everything that breaks — the link that confuses people, the photo that will not load, the delivery fee nobody can find. Fix those before you announce anything.

The test at the end of Phase Two is numbers, not feelings. Ten orders must have come through the website or the payment link, at least five of them from customers you had never served before, and every one of them must be in the sheet with a confirmed deposit. If the website exists but nobody uses it, the phase has not passed — you have a poster, not a channel.

Hire out in this phase: the website build and the payment integration. Do yourself: the words, the photos, the prices, the Google profile, and the testing. Nobody can write your prices for you.

## Phase Three: Automate the Repetition and Learn From the Numbers (Weeks 9–12, ₦30,000–₦110,000)

Phase Three is where the business starts to feel different, because the repetitive work begins to disappear. It is also the phase most businesses should skip if Phase Two has not passed its test.

Week nine is connections. Use a free tool like Zapier or Make to link the pieces: when a payment comes in, a row appears in your sheet. When an order is marked complete, the customer gets a thank-you message automatically. The free tiers cover a small business, and paid plans start around ₦30,000 a month only when your volume grows. If the word automation sounds grand, ignore it — this is just stopping yourself from typing the same information twice.

Week ten is the weekly content habit. One hour, once a week, to prepare the next seven days of posts: two product photos, one behind-the-scenes video, one customer review, one price or offer post. Write them in one sitting, schedule them, and stop thinking about social media for the rest of the week. If writing is not your strength, this is a fair job to hire out — a freelance content writer in Lagos charges roughly ₦20,000 to ₦50,000 a month for this — but keep the photos and the voice yours.

Week eleven is the review. Open your sheet and answer four questions with real numbers: which product made the most money, which one took the most time for the least profit, where did new customers come from, and what did you spend on tools? Dunni discovered that her smallest cakes, the ones she thought were her bread and butter, barely broke even after delivery costs, while her celebration cakes carried the business. She raised the price of the small ones by ₦2,000 and stopped offering free delivery on them. That one decision, made from her own numbers, was worth more than any tool she had bought.

Week twelve is the decision week. Look at the whole ninety days and decide what continues, what stops, and what comes next. Keep the subscriptions that earn their keep and cancel the rest.

The test at the end of Phase Three is that the business runs a normal week without you carrying it in your head. Your helper can take an order, confirm a payment and update the sheet without calling you. Your content is scheduled. Your numbers are current. If you go away for a weekend and the business still takes orders correctly, Phase Three has passed.

Hire out in this phase: content writing, only if you want to, and only after you have done it yourself for a month so you know what good looks like. Do yourself: the connections, the weekly review, and the decisions. No consultant can tell you which of your products makes money. Your sheet can.

## What the Ninety Days Actually Cost

Add it up and the full plan runs roughly ₦180,000 to ₦550,000 for a typical small business, spread across three months rather than spent in one panicked week. Phase One is nearly free. Phase Two carries the website and the payment setup, which is most of the money. Phase Three is small monthly tools and, if you choose it, a content writer.

That range is deliberate. The plan is built so that you can stop after any phase and keep what you gained. A business that finishes Phase One has clean records and fast replies, and that alone is worth more than a website built on chaos.

The other number that matters is time. Budget about five hours a week of your own time in Phase One, three hours a week in Phase Two once the developer is building, and two hours a week in Phase Three.

Dunni's ninety days ended in April. Her December that year looked nothing like the one before it. Orders came through the website, deposits arrived before she started baking, and at 10 p.m. on the busiest Saturday, her sheet told her exactly what was due, what was paid, and what could wait until morning. She did not buy a transformation. She followed a plan, one week at a time, and refused to pay for the next step until the last one had proved itself.

That refusal is the whole secret. Start with week one, and do not spend on week five until week four passes its test.
