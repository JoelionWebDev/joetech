Your website might be quietly losing you customers — not because your products are bad, but because your site is slow, confusing on a phone, missing key information, or invisible in search. Most business owners never find out — they just notice enquiries are low.

You can find most website problems yourself in about 30 minutes, with no technical skills and no paid tools.

## Before You Start

Start on your phone, because that is how most Nigerians will visit you. Write down everything you notice, even small things: a phone number that is hard to find, a button that does not work, a blurry photo. At the end, sort your notes into "fix now" and "fix later." You are aiming for an honest picture of what your website does well and where it lets you down.

## Minutes 1–5: The First Impression Test

Open your website on your phone, on a regular mobile network — not your office Wi-Fi. This is the experience most of your customers get.

**How fast does it load?** Count the seconds. If your homepage takes more than a few seconds to appear, many visitors will leave before they see anything. **Can you tell what the business does in five seconds?** A visitor should understand your business almost immediately. If your homepage opens with a vague slogan and no clear statement of what you sell, that is a problem. Your headline should say what you do and who you serve, in plain words.

**Does the site look right on a small screen?** Check whether text is cut off, buttons are too small to tap, images overlap, or you have to scroll sideways. If any of that happens, your site has a mobile problem — and since most Nigerians browse on phones, a mobile problem is a business problem. Also check that your phone number or WhatsApp button is easy to find.

## Minutes 6–12: Check Speed and Technical Health

Now move to your computer for the technical checks.

**Test your speed for free.** Google's PageSpeed Insights is free: type your website address in, and it gives you a score for mobile and desktop, plus a list of specific problems. Focus on the top three recommendations and write them down.

**Check that your site is secure.** Look at the address bar. It should show a padlock icon and an address starting with "https". If it shows "Not Secure", your site has a security problem. Visitors notice this, browsers warn about it, and Google ranks secure sites higher. Fixing it usually means installing an SSL certificate, which most hosts offer for free.

**Check for broken links, and test your contact form.** Click through your main menu and your most important pages. A broken "Contact Us" link or a dead product page is a lost customer. Check that your social media icons lead to your real profiles, then fill in your contact form and submit it using your own email address. Many businesses have a form that has been silently broken for months.

## Minutes 13–19: Review Your Content

A website is not just design and speed. The words on it decide whether a visitor trusts you enough to buy.

**Is your content up to date?** Check for old prices, discontinued products, expired promotions, or a copyright notice from three years ago. Outdated content tells visitors the business may not be active. If you have a blog, check when the last post was published — a blog that stopped two years ago can make the whole business look abandoned.

**Does each page answer a real question?** Your services page should explain clearly what you offer, what it costs (or how to get a quote), and how to order. Your About page should say who you are and why a customer should trust you. If a page is full of vague claims and no useful detail, rewrite it in plain language.

**Are your photos real and good quality?** Blurry photos, or stock images that clearly have nothing to do with your business, hurt trust. Customers want to see your actual products, your actual shop or office, and your actual team. And check that your business details match across your website, Google Business Profile, and social media — inconsistent details confuse customers and search engines.

## Minutes 20–25: Check Search Visibility

Your website can be beautiful and still invisible. Search for your business name on Google. Does your website appear at the top? If you cannot find yourself by your own name, something is wrong.

Next, search for what you sell, plus your location — for example, "fashion designer in Lekki". Do you appear on the first page? If not, note which competitors do, and look at what their websites do differently.

Also check your page titles. Each page should have a clear title describing what it is about — "Affordable Catering Services in Lagos" rather than just "Home". If every page has the same generic title, search engines struggle to understand what your site offers. If you have not claimed your Google Business Profile, do so — it is free.

If your audit turns up serious problems with your website's design or structure, compare notes with our guide on [5 signs your website needs a redesign](/blog/5-signs-your-website-needs-redesign). Some issues are quick fixes; others are signs that the site itself needs rebuilding.

## Minutes 26–30: Check Trust Signals

In the final five minutes, look at your website the way a cautious first-time buyer would. Can a visitor verify you are real? A physical address, a real phone number, photos of your team or premises, and genuine customer reviews all build trust. A website with no address, no phone number, and no reviews feels risky, and careful Nigerian customers will simply buy from someone else.

Are your prices or pricing process clear? A customer should understand how pricing works and how to get a quote. Do you have a privacy policy? If your website collects customer details through a form, it should have one — under Nigeria's data protection law, it is a legal requirement. And is there a clear next step on every page? Every page should tell the visitor what to do next: call, chat on WhatsApp, request a quote, or place an order. A page with no call to action is a dead end.

## Decide What to Fix First

Now sort your notes into three groups. **Fix today:** the urgent problems — a broken contact form, a site that is not secure, a wrong phone number, or a homepage that does not say what you do. These cost you customers every day they remain broken. **Fix this week:** slow images, outdated prices, a missing privacy policy, or an incomplete Google Business Profile. **Fix this month:** new photos, rewritten service pages, or a design refresh.

Pick the three most important items and do them first. Then repeat the audit in three months.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How often should I audit my website?

At least twice a year, and every time you change something important — a new service, a new price list, or a redesign. A quick monthly check of your contact form, your homepage speed, and your Google Business Profile is also worth doing.

### Do I need paid tools to audit my website?

No. Google PageSpeed Insights, Google Search, and your own phone and eyes cover most of what a small business needs. Paid tools add detail, but they are not necessary for finding the problems that cost you the most customers.

### My website scored badly on speed. What is the most likely cause?

In most cases, oversized images. Photos straight from a phone camera can be several megabytes each, and a page full of them loads slowly on a mobile network. Compressing your images is usually the single biggest speed win.

### What if my audit shows my website needs a complete rebuild?

Not every bad audit means a rebuild. Many problems — slow images, broken links, missing information, no HTTPS — can be fixed on your existing site. Consider a rebuild when the design is badly outdated, the site does not work on mobile at all, or its platform can no longer be updated securely.

## Make the Audit a Habit

A website is not a one-time project. Thirty minutes, twice a year, is enough to catch the problems that cost you customers.
