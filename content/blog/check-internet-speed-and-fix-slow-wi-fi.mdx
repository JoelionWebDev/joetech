Nothing is more frustrating than Wi-Fi that works fine in the morning and crawls in the evening — right when you are trying to send a customer a video, join a Zoom meeting, or upload a file. In Nigeria, where many homes and small businesses depend on a single router or a mobile hotspot, slow internet is a daily complaint. The good news is that most slow Wi-Fi problems have simple causes, and many of them you can fix yourself in a few minutes, without buying anything new. This guide shows you how to test your speed properly, understand what the numbers mean, and fix the most common causes of slow Wi-Fi.

## What Internet Speed Actually Means

Internet speed is measured in megabits per second, written as Mbps. When a speed test shows a result, it usually gives you three numbers: download speed, upload speed and ping. Download speed is how fast data comes down to your device — it controls how quickly web pages load, videos stream and files download. Upload speed is how fast data goes up from your device — it controls how quickly you can send files, upload videos, or appear clearly on a video call. Ping, measured in milliseconds (ms), is how long it takes a tiny signal to travel to a server and back. Low ping means a fast response; high ping means delay, which you notice as lag in video calls and online games.

For most everyday use, download speed matters most. Browsing, watching videos and scrolling social media are mostly downloading. Upload speed matters when you send large files, back up photos, or run video calls where other people need to see and hear you clearly.

It also helps to know the difference between your internet speed and your Wi-Fi speed. Your internet speed is what your provider delivers to your router. Your Wi-Fi speed is what your phone or laptop actually gets from the router over the air. If your provider delivers 20 Mbps but your phone only gets 5 Mbps in the next room, the problem is your Wi-Fi, not your internet plan. Testing in the right way tells you which one is at fault.

## How to Test Your Internet Speed Properly

Testing your speed is free and takes less than a minute. The most widely used tool is Speedtest by Ookla, available at speedtest.net and as a free app on Android and iPhone. Google also offers a speed test — just search "internet speed test" and tap the "Run speed test" button. Another reliable option is fast.com, run by Netflix, which measures download speed with one tap. All of these work in Nigeria on mobile data, fibre, and fixed wireless connections.

Before you run the test, a few details matter. First, close apps that are using the internet — video streaming, downloads, cloud backups and app updates will all drag your result down. Second, run the test close to the router, so you are measuring your internet connection rather than a weak Wi-Fi signal through walls. Third, run the test two or three times and take the average, because a single test can be affected by a brief network hiccup. Fourth, test at different times of day. Many Nigerian networks slow down in the evening when everyone in the area is online, and knowing this helps you tell a network problem from a problem in your own home.

If you want to test the speed your provider delivers — rather than your Wi-Fi — connect a laptop to the router with a cable and run the test that way. If the cabled speed is good but the Wi-Fi speed is poor, your Wi-Fi setup is the problem, and the fixes in this guide will help. If the cabled speed is also poor, the problem is with your provider or your plan, and you should contact them.

## What Speed Do You Actually Need?

You do not need the fastest plan on the market to work well online. What you need depends on what you do. Basic browsing, email and WhatsApp work fine on a few Mbps. Streaming video in standard quality needs around 3 to 5 Mbps, and high-definition video needs around 5 to 10 Mbps per stream. A video call on Zoom or Google Meet works well on around 3 to 5 Mbps in each direction, though a group call with many people needs more. Uploading large videos or backing up photos to the cloud is where upload speed matters — a slow upload speed can make a single video take a very long time.

The bigger factor for most homes and shops is how many people and devices share the connection. A 10 Mbps connection might feel fast for one person browsing, but add three phones streaming videos, a laptop on a Zoom call, and a smart TV, and everything slows down. As a rough guide, a small household with two or three people doing everyday browsing and video calls is usually comfortable on 10 to 20 Mbps. A busy shop or office with many devices, video calls and uploads will want 20 Mbps or more. If your speed test consistently shows far less than what you pay for, that is worth a call to your provider.

## Why Your Wi-Fi Is Slow: The Common Causes

Slow Wi-Fi usually comes down to a handful of causes. The most common is distance and obstacles. Wi-Fi signals weaken as they pass through walls, especially concrete walls, which are common in Nigerian buildings. A router in one corner of a flat may barely reach the far bedroom. Metal objects, mirrors and even large water tanks can also block or reflect the signal.

The second common cause is too many devices. Every phone, laptop, TV and smart device connected to your Wi-Fi takes a share of the connection. A device quietly downloading updates in the background can eat a surprising amount of speed. Visitors who connect to your Wi-Fi and start streaming videos add to the load too.

The third cause is interference. Wi-Fi routers broadcast on radio channels, and if your neighbours' routers are using the same channel, the signals interfere with each other. This is common in flats and estates where many routers sit close together. Most modern routers can choose a less crowded channel automatically, but older ones may need a manual change.

The fourth cause is the router itself. A router that has been running for weeks without a restart can slow down. An old router may not support the faster Wi-Fi standards that newer phones use. And a router placed on the floor, inside a cabinet, or behind a television will perform far worse than one placed in the open.

The fifth cause is your internet plan or provider. Network congestion in the evening, a plan that is too slow for your household, or a fault on the provider's side can all leave you with slow speeds no matter what you do at home. Finally, your own device can be the problem: an old phone with a weak Wi-Fi chip, a phone with a damaged antenna, or a laptop full of background apps will feel slow even on a good connection.

## Fix 1: Restart Your Router and Modem

The oldest trick in the book works because it actually helps. Routers are small computers, and like any computer they benefit from a restart. Over time, their memory fills up and small software glitches build up. Unplug the router and modem from power, wait about 30 seconds, then plug them back in. Wait a couple of minutes for the lights to settle and the connection to come back, then test your speed again.

Make this a habit: restart your router once a week, or at least once every two weeks. If you find yourself restarting it every day to keep it working, that is a sign the router is failing or too old, and it may be time to replace it.

## Fix 2: Move Your Router to a Better Spot

Where your router sits matters more than most people realise. The best place is high up, in a central spot, out in the open — on top of a shelf or a table, not on the floor and not inside a cupboard or behind the TV. Wi-Fi signals spread outwards and slightly downwards, so height helps. Keep the router away from thick concrete walls, metal cabinets and large mirrors where possible, and away from other electronics like microwaves and cordless phones, which can interfere with the signal.

If your home or shop is large, one router may simply not cover it. In that case, a Wi-Fi extender or a mesh Wi-Fi system can help. An extender picks up the signal and repeats it further into the house, while a mesh system uses two or three small units placed around the building to give even coverage. Mesh systems cost more but work better, especially in larger buildings with thick walls.

## Fix 3: Use the Right Wi-Fi Band

Most modern routers broadcast two Wi-Fi networks: 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz. The 2.4 GHz band reaches further and passes through walls better, but it is slower and more crowded. The 5 GHz band is much faster, but it does not travel as far and struggles with walls. If you are in the same room as the router, connect to the 5 GHz network for the best speed. If you are two rooms away, the 2.4 GHz network may actually work better because the 5 GHz signal is too weak by then.

Many routers combine both bands under one network name and choose for you, which is fine. But if your router shows two separate network names — often with "5G" at the end of one — try each one in the room where you work and see which performs better. Run a speed test on each to compare.

## Fix 4: Change the Wi-Fi Channel

If you live in a flat or an estate with many routers nearby, your Wi-Fi channel may be crowded. Routers let you change the channel in their settings page, which you open by typing the router's address into a browser — usually something like 192.168.1.1 or 192.168.0.1, printed on a sticker on the router. Inside the settings, look for the wireless section and the channel option. On the 2.4 GHz band, channels 1, 6 and 11 do not overlap with each other, so pick whichever of those is least used around you. If this sounds technical, ask someone comfortable with routers to help, or leave the router on its automatic setting — many modern routers pick a good channel on their own.

## Fix 5: Reduce the Load on Your Network

Take stock of what is connected. Open your router's settings page and look at the list of connected devices — you may be surprised. Old phones, a smart TV you forgot about, or a neighbour who knows your password could all be using your connection. Change your Wi-Fi password if you suspect someone else is using your network.

On your own devices, check for background activity. Automatic app updates, cloud photo backups and software updates can quietly consume a lot of speed. Set app updates and large backups to run at night, and pause them when you need speed for a video call or an upload. Streaming video in the highest quality also uses far more data than standard quality — dropping a stream from 4K to HD, or HD to standard, frees up speed for everything else.

## Fix 6: Update Your Router's Software and Check Your Devices

Router makers release software updates that fix bugs and improve performance. Check your router's settings page for a firmware update option and install it if one is available.

Also look at your devices. A very old phone may not support newer Wi-Fi standards and will always be slow on a modern network. If one device is slow while others are fast on the same Wi-Fi, the problem is that device, not the network — try forgetting the network and reconnecting, restarting the device, or updating its software.

## When the Problem Is Your Provider

If you have tried the fixes above and your speed is still far below what you pay for, the problem may be on your provider's side. Test with a cable connected directly to the router: if the wired speed is also poor, your Wi-Fi is not the problem. Test at different times of day to see whether evenings are much worse — that points to network congestion. Check whether your data allowance is exhausted, because some plans slow down sharply once you pass a limit.

When you contact your provider, have your numbers ready: your speed test results, the time of the tests, and whether you tested by cable or Wi-Fi. That makes it much easier for their support team to help you. If your provider consistently delivers far less than promised and support does not resolve it, it may be worth comparing other providers in your area — ask neighbours what they use and how it performs before you switch.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a good internet speed in Nigeria?

It depends on what you do. For basic browsing and WhatsApp, a few Mbps is enough. For video calls and HD streaming, around 10 Mbps feels comfortable for one or two people. A household or small shop with several people streaming, calling and uploading at the same time will usually want 20 Mbps or more. What matters most is that the speed you get is close to the speed you pay for.

### Why is my Wi-Fi slow only in the evening?

Evening is peak time — more people in your area are online, streaming and downloading, which congests the network. It can also be peak time inside your own home, with more family members using the connection. Test your speed in the morning and in the evening to compare; a big evening drop points to congestion rather than a fault in your house.

### Why is my Wi-Fi slow in one room but fast in another?

Wi-Fi signals weaken with distance and through walls, especially concrete walls. The room furthest from the router, or separated by several walls, will always be slower. Moving the router to a more central spot, or adding a Wi-Fi extender or mesh unit, usually fixes it.

### Should I use 2.4 GHz or 5 GHz Wi-Fi?

Use 5 GHz when you are near the router — it is faster. Use 2.4 GHz when you are further away or behind walls — it reaches further. If your router shows both networks, test each one where you normally work and use whichever gives the better speed.

### Will restarting my router really help?

Yes, often. Routers run for weeks without a break, and restarting clears temporary glitches and frees up memory. Unplug it for 30 seconds, plug it back in, wait for the connection to return, and test again. If you need to restart it daily, the router may be failing and worth replacing.

### How can I tell if someone else is using my Wi-Fi?

Open your router's settings page and check the list of connected devices. If you see devices you do not recognise, change your Wi-Fi password to a strong one and reconnect only your own devices. A sudden drop in speed with no change in your own usage is another clue.


### When should I upgrade my internet plan?

Consider upgrading when your speed tests consistently show that your connection cannot keep up with your household or shop — video calls freeze, uploads take very long, or several people cannot use the internet at the same time. Before upgrading, fix your Wi-Fi setup first, so you know the problem is the plan and not your router.

## What to Do Next

Start by testing your speed properly: stand near your router, close apps that use the internet, and run a speed test two or three times. Note the download speed, upload speed and ping. Then walk to the room where you normally work and test again. If the speed drops sharply with distance, move your router to a more central, higher spot and test again. Restart the router, check which devices are connected, and switch to the 5 GHz network if you are close to the router.
