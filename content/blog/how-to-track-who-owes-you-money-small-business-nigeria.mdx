Ask a Nigerian small business owner how much customers owe them, and the answer is often a shrug. "Some people owe me," they say. "I think it is plenty." They know the money is out there — in the hands of neighbours, regular customers, colleagues, and friends of friends — but they cannot say exactly who owes what, or how much, or since when.

This is one of the quietest ways small businesses in Nigeria lose money. Not theft. Not bad sales. Just credit given with a good heart and a bad memory. A customer takes goods worth ₦45,000 and promises to pay at month end. Month end comes, the customer is quiet, the seller is busy, and the debt slowly fades from memory. Multiply that by ten customers, or twenty, and a business can have hundreds of thousands of naira sitting in other people's pockets while the owner struggles to restock.

Selling on credit is part of doing business in Nigeria. Customers expect it, relationships depend on it, and refusing it completely can cost you sales. But credit without records is not generosity — it is a leak. This guide shows you how to keep track of who owes you money with simple systems that work for a one-person business, a market stall, or a growing shop — no accounting degree required.

## Why Credit Gets Out of Hand in Nigerian Businesses

Credit in Nigeria is rarely a formal arrangement. There is usually no signed agreement, no interest, and no fixed date. A customer says "I will pay you on Friday" or "when salary comes" or "next week", and the seller says yes because the customer is a regular, or a neighbour, or someone they do not want to offend.

That informality is the problem. Without a written record, both sides remember the debt differently. The seller remembers ₦50,000. The customer remembers ₦30,000, because they paid ₦20,000 one evening and the seller forgot. Or the customer remembers paying in full, and the seller remembers a balance. These disagreements damage relationships that took years to build — and they are entirely avoidable.

There is also the problem of volume. When you are selling to dozens of people a week, you cannot carry every debt in your head. Memory is not a record-keeping system. The seller who says "I remember everything" is usually the seller who has forgotten the most.

And then there is the emotional side. Chasing money you are owed is uncomfortable, especially when the person is a friend, a church member, or a family member. Many sellers avoid the conversation entirely — they stop asking, and the debt becomes a permanent gift they never meant to give. A proper record does not make the conversation easy, but it makes it factual: this is the date, this is the amount, this is what has been paid. Facts are much easier to discuss than feelings.

The goal of this guide is not to stop you selling on credit. It is to make sure that every naira you lend comes back to you — or at least, that you always know exactly where it is.

## Step 1: Decide Your Credit Rules Before You Need Them

A record-keeping system works best when it sits on top of clear rules. Before you worry about notebooks or apps, decide — and write down — your credit policy. It does not need to be a formal document. A few sentences in your notebook are enough.

Start with these questions:

**Who gets credit?** Not everyone should. A reasonable rule is that only customers you know well — regulars who have bought from you before and paid on time — get credit. A brand-new customer who walks in off the street should pay in full. That is not rude; it is sensible, and most people understand it.

**How much credit?** Set a maximum amount per customer, and a maximum total amount you are willing to have outstanding at any time. For a small business, a sensible ceiling is an amount you could afford to lose without the business collapsing — because some debts will never come back, and your business must survive that.

**For how long?** Set a clear repayment period — seven days, fourteen days, or by month end. "Pay me when you can" is not a repayment period; it is an invitation to forget. A specific date gives both sides something to work towards.

**What happens if they do not pay?** Decide in advance, while you are calm. Perhaps no new credit until the old debt is cleared. Perhaps no more goods at all. Perhaps a smaller credit limit next time. Whatever you decide, apply it consistently — including to friends and family, who are often the worst offenders precisely because they know you will not enforce the rules.

Once your rules exist, tell your customers. You do not need a big announcement. Just mention it naturally when credit comes up: "I can give you one week to pay the balance, and we write it down so we both remember." Customers respect a business with clear rules far more than they respect one with none.

## Step 2: Record Every Credit Sale the Same Day

This is the single most important habit in this entire guide: write down every credit sale on the day it happens. Not tomorrow. Not "when I have time". The same day, before you close the shop or put your phone down for the night.

Every record needs the same five pieces of information:

1. **The customer's full name** — not just "Aunty Blessing" or "that man from the estate". Full name, and ideally a phone number.
2. **The date** the goods or service were given.
3. **What was taken** — the items, the quantity, and the total value.
4. **What was paid** — the deposit or part-payment, if any.
5. **The balance owed, and the date it is due.**

That is all. Five facts, written in the same format every time, and you will never again have to guess who owes you what.

A simple entry looks like this: "12 Nov 2026 — Mrs Adaeze Okafor, 0803 555 0142 — 2 lace wrappers, ₦60,000 — paid ₦30,000 — balance ₦30,000 — due 26 Nov 2026."

Write it the moment the customer leaves, or before you hand over the goods if you can. The longer the gap between the sale and the record, the more likely you are to forget a detail — and a record with the wrong amount is almost as bad as no record at all.

## Step 3: Choose Your Record-Keeping Tool (Start Simple)

You do not need expensive software to track debts. You need a system you will actually use every day. Here are the options, from simplest to most advanced — pick the one that fits your business today, and upgrade later if you need to.

### Option 1: The notebook

A dedicated hardcover notebook, used for credit sales only — not mixed in with your general notes, your personal jottings, or your prayer points. One page per customer, or one entry per line in a ruled table, whichever you prefer.

The notebook has real advantages: it never runs out of battery, it works when the network is down, and it is always in your shop. Its weakness is that it can be lost, damaged, or ruined by water — so keep it in a safe place, and consider photographing the pages at the end of each week as a backup.

Use a pen, not a pencil, so entries cannot be quietly altered. When a customer pays part of a debt, write the payment under the original entry with the date, and calculate the new balance. When a debt is fully paid, draw a line through the entry and write "PAID" with the date. That single visual habit — seeing debts turn into paid entries — is surprisingly motivating.

### Option 2: The spreadsheet

Google Sheets (free, on any phone) or Microsoft Excel. One sheet, with columns for date, customer name, phone number, items, total amount, amount paid, balance, and due date. Sort by due date to see who should pay next, and filter by customer name to see one person's full history.

A spreadsheet is better than a notebook when your debts grow beyond a handful, because you can search instantly and the totals add themselves up. Keep a backup — Google Sheets saves automatically to the cloud, which also protects you if your phone is lost or stolen.

### Option 3: A business app

Several free and low-cost apps are built for exactly this problem, including apps designed for Nigerian traders that let you record sales on credit, send payment reminders by WhatsApp or SMS, and see your total outstanding debts at a glance. Some popular options among Nigerian small businesses include bookkeeping apps that work offline and sync when you have data — useful when you are in a market with weak network.

If you choose an app, pick one that lets you export your data, so your records are never trapped inside a single app. And remember: an app you do not open every day is worse than a notebook you do. Choose the tool you will actually use, not the one that looks most impressive.

### What about WhatsApp?

Many sellers keep their credit records in WhatsApp — a chat with the customer, or a message to themselves. WhatsApp is useful as a backup and as a way to confirm a debt with the customer ("Here is the balance, please confirm"), but it is a poor primary record. Chats get buried, phones get lost, and searching through months of chats for one amount is slow and painful. Use WhatsApp to confirm and remind — keep the real record in your notebook, spreadsheet, or app.

## Step 4: Confirm Every Debt With the Customer

A record that only you have seen is only half a record. The other half is making sure the customer agrees with it.

After every credit sale, send the customer a short message confirming the details: "Thank you for your purchase today. Your balance is ₦30,000, due on 26 November. My account details are..." This takes thirty seconds and prevents almost every future disagreement.

Why does this matter so much? Because memories differ, and because a customer who has seen the amount in writing is far more likely to pay on time. It also protects you: if a customer later claims they paid in full, you have the message — and their reply — to refer to.

When a customer makes a part-payment, confirm that too, with the new balance: "Received ₦15,000 today. Thank you. Your remaining balance is now ₦15,000." Send it by WhatsApp or SMS so there is a written trail. If the customer pays cash, write a simple receipt — even a handwritten one in your notebook, signed by both of you — so the payment is not forgotten by either side.

This habit also has a subtle psychological effect: it signals that you are keeping records, and that you take your business seriously. Customers treat debts more seriously when they know the seller is tracking them. The sellers who get paid fastest are rarely the loudest — they are the most organised.

## Step 5: Follow Up on Time, Politely, and Consistently

This is where most Nigerian sellers fail — not in recording the debt, but in asking for it. They wait. They hope the customer will remember. They do not want to be seen as troublesome. And the debt grows old, and then it dies.

Build follow-up into your weekly routine. Once a week — pick a day, perhaps Monday morning or Saturday afternoon — open your record and look at every debt that is due this week or already overdue. Then message each person.

A good reminder is short, friendly, and specific:

"Good morning Mrs Adaeze, I hope you are well. Just a gentle reminder that your balance of ₦30,000 for the lace wrappers is due on 26 November. You can send it to the usual account whenever you are ready. Thank you!"

Notice what this message does: it greets the person warmly, states the exact amount, states the exact date, and gives them a clear way to pay. It does not accuse, it does not beg, and it does not apologise for asking for your own money.

If the due date passes without payment, follow up again — once a few days after the date, and once a week after that. Keep the tone friendly but firmer each time. If the customer replies with a genuine difficulty — salary delayed, an emergency at home — agree on a new date together and record it. Flexibility is fine, but the new date must be written down, just like the old one was.

After two reminders with no response and no payment, it is time for a phone call, not another message. A call is harder to ignore, and it tells you something a message cannot: whether the customer is avoiding you, or simply busy. If the customer still does not respond, stop giving them credit — and consider whether you should continue selling to them at all.

## What to Do Next

1. **Start your record today.** Get a dedicated notebook, open a Google Sheet, or download a bookkeeping app — whichever you will actually use. Write down every debt you can remember right now, with names, amounts, and dates. It will not be perfect, but it is far better than what you have today, which is memory.

2. **Write down your credit rules.** Who gets credit, how much, for how long, and what happens if they do not pay. Keep the rules somewhere you can see them, and start applying them to every new credit sale from today.

3. **Send your first reminders this week.** Pick the five oldest or largest debts in your new record and send each customer a short, polite message confirming the amount and the due date. You may be surprised how many pay quickly once they are reminded — most people are not dishonest, they are just busy and forgetful.

Keeping track of who owes you money will not make you popular with every customer. But it will make your business stronger, your cash flow healthier, and your relationships cleaner — because nothing damages a friendship faster than a forgotten debt and a disagreement about the amount. Write it down, confirm it, follow up, and get paid. Your business deserves nothing less.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I stop selling on credit completely?

Not necessarily. Credit is a normal part of doing business in Nigeria and refusing it entirely can cost you loyal customers. The answer is not to stop giving credit, but to give it carefully — only to trusted customers, with clear limits, a written record, and a fixed repayment date.

### What is the simplest way to track who owes me money?

A dedicated notebook used only for credit sales is the simplest system that works: one entry per sale with the customer's name, phone number, date, items, amount paid, balance owed, and due date. If you prefer your phone, a Google Sheet with the same columns works just as well and backs up automatically.

### How do I ask a friend or family member to pay what they owe?

Keep it factual and friendly. Send a message stating the amount, the date, and the due date — no accusations, no guilt. Treating friends and family exactly like other customers, with the same records and the same reminders, actually protects the relationship, because it removes arguments about what was agreed.

### What should I do if a customer refuses to pay?

First, confirm the debt in writing and give them a final date. If they still do not pay, stop giving them credit and consider whether to keep selling to them at all. For large amounts, you may need to involve a mediator both sides respect, such as a community or family elder. Going to court is usually slow and expensive for small amounts, so prevention — deposits, limits, and records — matters far more than recovery.

### How long should I give a customer to pay?

For most small businesses, seven to fourteen days is reasonable, or by an agreed month-end date for salaried customers. Whatever you choose, agree on a specific date at the time of the sale and write it down. "Pay me when you can" is not a repayment date and almost always leads to forgotten debts.
