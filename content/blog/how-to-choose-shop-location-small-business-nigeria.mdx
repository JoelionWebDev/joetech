A woman in Surulere sells beautiful Ankara fabrics. Her materials are good, her prices are fair, and her Instagram page is active. Yet her shop is quiet most days. Three streets away, another fabric seller with similar products is always busy. The difference is not the fabric. It is the location. The busy shop sits on a road where people walk past all day, close to a bus stop and a market. The quiet shop sits inside a compound, down a side street, where nobody passes unless they already know it is there.

Location is one of the biggest decisions a Nigerian small business owner will ever make. A good location brings customers to you every day without advertising. A bad location forces you to spend money on adverts, delivery, and discounts just to get people through the door. Worse, a bad location often comes with a long lease, so you are stuck paying rent for a place that does not bring sales.

This guide will help you choose wisely. It is written for real Nigerian conditions: Lagos traffic, Abuja estates, Onitsha markets, Port Harcourt streets, Ibadan neighbourhoods, and small towns where everyone knows everyone. Follow the steps, do the maths before you pay, and test the place before you commit.

## Why Location Matters More Than Decoration

Many new business owners spend their money in the wrong order. They pay for a big signboard, paint the shop, buy shelves, and stock goods — and only afterwards realise that few people pass the shop. Decoration does not bring customers. People do.

A good location does three things for you. First, it gives you free visibility. Every person who walks past your shop is a free advert. If one thousand people pass daily and only one in a hundred enters, that is ten customers a day without spending a naira on promotion.

Second, it builds trust. In Nigeria, customers trust a shop they can see and visit. A shop on a known road feels real. A shop hidden in a residential street feels risky, especially for new customers who found you online and want to confirm you exist before they pay.

Third, it reduces your delivery and marketing costs. When customers can walk in, you spend less on dispatch riders, less on sponsored posts, and less on discounts to attract people. Over a year, that saving is often bigger than the difference in rent between a good street and a bad one.



## Step 1: Know Exactly Who Your Customer Is

Before you look at any shop, write down who you are selling to. Be specific. "Everybody" is not a customer. "Working women aged 25 to 45 in Lekki who buy ready-to-wear clothes" is a customer. "Parents in Ikeja who need school supplies" is a customer. "Young men in Yaba who buy phone accessories" is a customer.

Your customer determines your location. If you sell baby items, you need to be near homes, schools, hospitals, and churches where mothers pass daily. If you sell building materials, you need to be near construction sites and estates under development, not in a fashion district. If you sell food, you need to be near offices, schools, or markets where hungry people pass at lunch time.

Ask yourself these questions and write the answers down:

Who is my main customer, and how old are they? Where do they live and work? Where do they already shop for what I sell? Do they come by car, by bus, by keke, or on foot? Do they shop in the morning, at lunch, or in the evening? How much can they afford to pay?

Now visit the places where those customers already go. If you sell hair products, spend a morning in a busy market in Lagos Island or in Bodija Market in Ibadan. Watch who buys, what they buy, and how much they spend. If you sell office lunch packs, stand near an office complex in Ikeja GRA or Wuse in Abuja at noon and count the people buying food. This observation is free market research, and it is more reliable than guessing.



## Step 2: Study Foot Traffic Like a Scientist

Foot traffic means the number of people who pass a location on foot in a day. For most retail shops in Nigeria, foot traffic is the single most important factor. Do not guess it. Count it.

Visit the shop you are considering at different times: morning, afternoon, and evening, on a weekday and on a Saturday. Sit in a nearby spot, or stand across the road, and count how many people pass in thirty minutes. Multiply to estimate the full day. Do this for at least two or three days. A street that is busy on Saturday morning may be empty on Tuesday afternoon.

While you count, watch carefully. Are the people passing your target customers, or are they just passing through? A road full of school children is good for snacks and stationery, but not for luxury perfumes. A road full of office workers is good for lunch and dry cleaning, but not for building materials. Numbers alone are not enough. You need the right people.

Finally, check the evening. Many Nigerian shops make their best sales between 4pm and 8pm, when workers are returning home. If a street is dark, unsafe, or empty after 6pm, you will lose those evening sales. Visit at night before you sign anything.

## Step 3: Do the Rent Maths Before You Fall in Love

Rent is usually the biggest fixed cost for a Nigerian shop owner. Many businesses fail not because sales are low, but because rent is too high for the sales the location can produce. Do the maths before you pay.

First, find out the total cost, not just the rent. In Nigeria, shop rent often comes with extra charges: agent fee, agreement fee, caution fee, service charge, and sometimes a full year or two years paid upfront. Ask for every charge in writing. A shop advertised at 800,000 naira a year can easily become 1,200,000 naira after agent and agreement fees.

Second, work out what the rent means per day and per month. If the rent is 1,200,000 naira a year, that is 100,000 naira a month, or about 3,300 naira a day. Your shop must make enough profit, not just sales, to cover that every single day, plus your other costs.

Third, set a rent limit based on your expected profit. A useful guide for retail is that rent should not be more than 10 to 15 percent of your monthly sales. If you expect to sell 1,000,000 naira worth of goods in a month, and your profit margin is 30 percent, your monthly profit is 300,000 naira. Rent of 100,000 naira would take one third of your profit. That is too high for most small businesses. Rent of 50,000 naira would be more manageable.



Fourth, ask about rent increases. Many Nigerian landlords increase rent after the first year. Ask the landlord and the neighbours: "How much was rent here two years ago? How often does it increase?" Get the rent terms and the renewal terms in your agreement. A cheap first year that doubles in year two is a trap.

Fifth, never pay for a shop you have not seen inside, in daylight, with the power on. Check the ceiling for leaks, the walls for damp, the floor for cracks, and the toilet if there is one. A shop that floods or has no ventilation will cost you in damaged goods and unhappy staff.

## Step 4: Study the Competition Around You

Competition is not always bad. In Nigeria, clusters of similar shops often attract more customers, not fewer. Computer Village in Ikeja works because buyers know they will find many phone sellers in one place. Balogun Market works because fabric buyers know they can compare prices in one area. Being near competitors can bring you customers who came for them and discovered you.

But there is a limit. If a street already has ten shops selling exactly what you sell, and they are all established with loyal customers, you will struggle to stand out unless you offer something different: better quality, lower prices, better service, or a product they do not carry.

Walk the street and list every competitor within a five-minute walk. Note what they sell, their prices, their opening hours, and how busy they are. Then ask yourself: what can I offer that they do not? Perhaps you open earlier, close later, deliver to homes, accept transfers easily, stock a wider range of sizes, or offer a product they have stopped carrying.

Also look for complementary businesses, not just competitors. A hair salon next to a cosmetics shop is a good combination. A food shop next to a school is a good combination. A phone repair shop next to a phone accessories shop is a good combination. Customers who come for one business often buy from the next. If you can find a spot next to a busy business that serves the same customers but sells something different, that is often better than being alone.



One warning: be careful of locations where a big supermarket or a new mall is about to open nearby. It can bring more people to the area, which is good, but it can also take your customers if it sells what you sell at lower prices. Ask around about any planned developments before you sign.

## Step 5: Check Access, Parking, Security, and Power

In Nigeria, a shop can fail because customers cannot reach it easily, cannot park, feel unsafe, or cannot get light. These practical factors matter as much as foot traffic.

**Access.** Can cars stop in front of your shop, or is it on a road where stopping is not allowed? Can a keke or okada drop a customer at your door? Is the entrance easy to find, or is it behind another building? Can a heavily pregnant woman, an elderly person, or a person carrying a baby enter easily? If customers have to climb a long staircase or cross a busy road without a crossing, many will not bother.

**Parking.** In Lagos and Abuja, parking decides whether car owners will stop. If there is no parking space, a customer driving past will keep going to a competitor who has parking. Look for a shop with at least a small space in front, or a nearby open space where cars can park without blocking the road. Watch at peak time: if cars are double-parked and traffic wardens are active, customers will avoid the area.

**Security.** Visit the area at night and ask the neighbours about security. Is there a vigilante group or a police post nearby? Have there been robberies on the street? Is the shop in a gated complex with a gate man, or open to the road? Check the doors, the windows, and the burglary-proof bars. Budget for good locks, and ask whether the landlord provides a security guard or whether shop owners contribute to one. A shop that is broken into once can wipe out months of profit.

**Power and water.** Ask how many hours of electricity the area gets daily. Ask the neighbours what they spend on generator fuel. A shop that needs light for a fridge, a freezer, a salon dryer, or a computer cannot survive on two hours of power a day without a big generator cost. Check whether there is a borehole or regular water supply if your business needs water. Check the toilet and drainage. These are not small details. They are running costs that affect your profit every month.



## Step 6: Watch Out for Hidden Costs and Local Rules

Every Nigerian location comes with costs that agents rarely mention. Find them before you pay.

**Local government levies and market charges.** Some areas charge daily or monthly levies for shops, signage, waste disposal, and local government permits. Ask the existing shop owners what they pay each month to the local government, the market association, and any other group. Add those to your rent maths.

**Demolition and road construction.** This is a serious risk in Nigeria. Ask whether the road is marked for expansion, whether the building has any government notice, and whether the area has had demolitions before. Talk to older shop owners and check with the local government if you are unsure. A shop demolished after six months is a total loss.



Get everything in writing. A written tenancy agreement should state the rent, the duration, the renewal terms, what happens if you want to leave early, who pays for repairs, and what the extra charges are. If the landlord will not give you a written agreement, walk away. No matter how good the location looks, a verbal agreement in Nigeria is a risk you do not need.

## Step 7: Test the Location and Negotiate Before You Pay

The smartest way to choose a location is to test it cheaply before you sign a long lease. If the area has a market day or allows temporary stalls, rent a small space for a weekend and sell there. Count your sales, talk to customers, and ask where they came from. Real sales data beats guesswork. You can also run a simple poll on your WhatsApp Status or Instagram Stories, asking your existing customers whether they would visit a shop in that area. If testing is not possible, negotiate a shorter first lease, such as six months instead of two years, with an option to renew. A landlord who believes in the location will often agree.

Many Nigerian small business owners accept the first price an agent gives them, but agents and landlords expect negotiation. Find out the real price by asking two or three agents about similar shops on the same street and asking neighbours what they pay. Negotiate the total package, not just the rent: can the agent fee be reduced, can you pay six months now and six months later, or can the landlord give you one month free to set up? Avoid the common mistakes: do not choose the cheapest shop just because it is cheap, because it is often cheap because nobody goes there, and do not choose the most expensive shop for prestige if the rent will eat your profit. Do not copy a competitor blindly, and do not forget the rainy season — a street that is busy in December can be dead in July if the road floods. Never pay in cash without a receipt, and never sign an agreement you have not read fully.

## Your Location Checklist

Before you pay for any shop in Nigeria, tick every box on this list.

You know who your customer is and where they already shop. You have counted foot traffic at different times and on different days. You have done the rent maths and rent is not more than 15 percent of expected monthly sales. You know all the extra charges, including agent, agreement, and local levies. You have checked access, parking, security, power, water, and network. You have talked to neighbouring shop owners and the previous tenant. You have checked for flooding, demolition risk, and signage rules. You have a written agreement with clear rent, duration, and renewal terms. You have tested the area if possible, or negotiated a shorter first lease.

If you cannot tick most of these boxes, keep looking. The right shop is worth waiting for. A wrong shop is expensive to leave and painful to stay in.

## Conclusion: Choose for Your Customers, Not for Yourself

Choosing a shop location in Nigeria is not about finding the finest building or the busiest road. It is about finding the place where your customers already are, where they can reach you easily, where they feel safe, and where the rent leaves you with profit after all your costs.

Take your time. Walk the streets. Count the people. Talk to the neighbours. Do the maths. Test the area. Negotiate firmly. The few weeks you spend choosing well will save you months of struggle and thousands of naira in wasted rent.

Your products deserve a location that gives them a fair chance. Give them that chance, and your shop will have the best foundation any small business can have: customers who find you easily, trust you quickly, and come back again and again.
