Ada sells skincare from a small shop on Admiralty Way in Lekki Phase 1. Her brand is called Glow by Ada. She has 640 people on her email list, a MailerLite account she pays nothing for, and a problem every small business owner in Lagos knows too well: she opens her laptop on a Tuesday morning, stares at the blank email editor, and closes it again.

It is not that she has nothing to say. Her customers ask her questions every day. Which cream works for oily skin in this Lagos heat? When is the vitamin C serum coming back? Is the ₦18,500 glow set worth it? She answers all of it on WhatsApp, one customer at a time, and never writes any of it down as an email.

So her list sits there. Six hundred and forty people who once cared enough to hand over an email address, hearing nothing for weeks, then getting three emails in one week when a new product lands. That is not a strategy. That is panic with a send button.

A plan fixes this. Not a thick document. A plan that fits on one page: a money goal for the quarter, four groups of people to talk to differently, and a calendar that says exactly what goes out and when. Here is how Ada built hers for the first quarter of 2027.

## Start With a Number, Not a Vibe

Most email plans start with "grow the list" or "send more emails." Those are chores, not goals. A goal needs a naira figure attached to it, because a naira figure tells you whether the plan worked.

Ada sat down with her notebook in the last week of November and did simple sums. In October, her shop made ₦1,850,000 in total sales. About ₦210,000 of that came from customers who clicked a link in an email. That was with her sending emails whenever she remembered, which was about five times in the month. She decided her first-quarter goal would be ₦600,000 in email-driven sales across January, February and March 2027 — roughly ₦200,000 a month, which meant doing deliberately what she had been doing by accident. Underneath it she wrote two supporting numbers: grow the list from 640 to 900 by the end of March, and keep her open rate above 25%.

## Split the List Before You Write a Word

Ada's second move was to stop treating 640 people as one crowd. She exported her list into a spreadsheet one evening and sorted it by what people had actually done, not what she hoped they would do. Four groups fell out, and nearly every small business list splits the same way.

The first group is the new subscribers, 148 people who joined the list but have never bought anything. They signed up for the 10% discount or downloaded her free "Skincare in Harmattan" guide. They are curious but uncommitted. They need to meet Ada before they meet her prices.

The second group is the first-time buyers, 211 people who bought once and never came back. This is the biggest group on most small business lists, and the most ignored. They liked the product enough to pay once. Something — forgetfulness, a cheaper option, a delivery that took two days too long — stopped them from returning.

The third group is the repeat buyers, 187 people who have ordered twice or more. One of them, Mrs. Eze, has ordered the ₦12,000 shea body butter every six weeks since March. They do not need convincing. They need to feel noticed, and to hear about things before everyone else does.

The fourth group is the quiet ones, 94 people who have not opened an email or bought anything in over three months. Ada's instinct was to delete them. Her better instinct was to give them one honest chance to stay, and then let them go.

She tagged all four groups in MailerLite in one evening, so in January she was never writing to "everyone." She was writing to 148 curious people, or 187 regulars, or 94 quiet ones.

## Decide What Kind of Email Goes Out, and How Often

Ada sells to people who check email on their phones, mostly in the morning before work or in the evening after traffic. She chose two sends a week: Tuesday morning and Friday morning. Two is enough to stay present and few enough that a one-woman business can keep it up for thirteen weeks without burning out.

She also chose four kinds of email, and gave each kind a job.

Story emails carry the teaching. How to layer serums in harmattan. Why a ₦9,500 cleanser can outperform a ₦25,000 one. A customer story, like Blessing from Ikoyi, whose dark spots faded after six weeks on the glow set. Story emails build the trust that makes the selling emails work. They go to the whole list, or to the new subscribers and first-time buyers who still need convincing.

Sales emails carry the offer. A new product, a price change, a bundle. They are short, specific, and they name the price. Ada sends at most one of these a week, because a list that only hears "buy now" stops opening.

Restock emails carry news. The vitamin C serum is back after three weeks out of stock. The ₦18,500 glow set has twelve units left. These go to the people who asked about the product, or to repeat buyers first, because they are the ones who will act fastest.

Holiday emails carry the occasion. Valentine's Day in February. International Women's Day on 8 March. Ada plans these a month ahead, because holiday emails written the night before read like they were written the night before.

The rule she taped to her laptop: three story emails for every two sales emails, roughly. Trust first, offer second. When she broke the rule in the past and sent four promotions in a row, her unsubscribes tripled.

## The Worked Calendar: Ada's January 2027

Here is the part most guides skip. A real calendar, with real dates, for a real business. January 2027, two sends a week, eight emails. Ada writes all eight in the last week of December, in one sitting, with her phone on silent. She schedules them and spends January answering customers instead of staring at blank editors.

**Tuesday 5 January — story email, to new subscribers and first-time buyers.** Subject: "Your skin survived December. Now what?" The email is about post-holiday skin: too much makeup, too little sleep, harmattan dust. It teaches a simple three-step reset with products she already sells, and ends with the 10% first-order code for new subscribers who have never used it.

**Friday 8 January — restock email, to repeat buyers first.** Subject: "The vitamin C serum is back (12 bottles only)." The serum has been out of stock since mid-December. Mrs. Eze and eleven other regulars asked about it. This email goes to repeat buyers on Friday morning and to the rest of the list on Saturday, so the regulars genuinely get it first. Twelve bottles, ₦14,000 each, first come first served.

**Tuesday 12 January — story email, to the whole list.** Subject: "Blessing's dark spots took 6 weeks. Here is what she used." A customer story with a photo Blessing agreed to share. It names the products, the routine, and the price of the full set, ₦32,000.

**Friday 15 January — sales email, to first-time buyers.** Subject: "You bought the cleanser. The set is ₦4,000 off this weekend." This is a bundle offer for people who bought one item and never tried the rest. The discount runs Friday to Sunday only.

**Tuesday 19 January — story email, to new subscribers.** Subject: "Oily skin in Lagos heat? Read this before you buy anything." A teaching email aimed at the people who joined for the harmattan guide. It explains why heavy creams fail in humidity and which lighter products work. It links to three products and nothing else. No discount. New subscribers need to learn before they are asked to spend.

**Friday 22 January — restock email, to the whole list.** Subject: "New: the ₦9,500 cleanser your skin will actually finish." A new product launch, kept simple. What it is, who it is for, what it costs, and a photo of Ada holding it in the shop.

**Tuesday 26 January — story email, to the whole list.** Subject: "3 mistakes that waste your skincare money." Short, blunt, useful. Buying products that fight each other. Skipping sunscreen. Storing serums in a hot bathroom. Each mistake links to the right product.

**Friday 29 January — sales email, to repeat buyers and first-time buyers.** Subject: "February preview: regulars shop first." A quiet announcement that the Valentine's glow bundle lands on 1 February, and that repeat buyers can order it from 29 January at ₦22,000 instead of ₦26,000.

Eight emails. Four stories, two sales, two restocks. Every one has a named audience, a named job, and a subject line written before the month starts. Ada's January takes one writing afternoon and then runs itself.

## February and March, in Short

February is the Valentine's month. The glow bundle launches 1 February, a gift guide goes out on Tuesday 9 February, and a last-order date email goes out on Friday 12 February, because deliveries in Lagos slow down badly around Valentine's weekend and Ada would rather warn people than apologise to them.

March is Women's Month. The International Women's Day email goes out on 8 March, built around three of her customers who run their own businesses, with a 15% discount code for the week. The last week of March is the quarter review: she counts the money, checks the open rates, and asks which of the four groups grew and which shrank. If the first-time buyers did not come back, April's plan tilts toward them.

## What to Do When the Plan Meets Real Life

A plan is not a prison. In the second week of January, Ada's supplier in Onitsha delays a delivery by four days. The restock email scheduled for 22 January has nothing to restock. She swaps it for a story email she already wrote in December as a spare, about how to tell a real product from a fake one in Lagos markets. She keeps a spare email in her drafts at all times, for exactly this reason. One spare, written when she has energy, saves a missed week when she does not.

And when March ends, she does the review properly. Not a feeling, a count. How much money did email bring in? Which group bought most? Which email got the most orders? She writes the answers on one page and uses them to plan April. A quarterly plan is a loop, not a one-off. Each quarter gets easier because the last quarter left notes.

## Your Turn, in One Afternoon

You do not need Ada's exact business. You need her afternoon. Pick your number for the quarter. Sort your list into the four groups — new, bought once, bought twice or more, gone quiet — even if your list has sixty people and not six hundred. Choose two days a week and four kinds of email. Then sit down and write the first month before the month starts, subjects and all.
