Every day, Nigerian small businesses lose money to online scams. It does not matter whether you sell clothes on Instagram, run a catering business from your kitchen, or manage a shop in Lekki — fraudsters are looking for you. They study how businesses like yours work, then they use that knowledge to trick you into sending money, goods, or sensitive information.

The painful part is that most of these scams are avoidable. The fraudster rarely hacks into your account with clever technology. Instead, they rely on urgency, trust and confusion. A message that looks like it came from your bank. A customer who sends a fake payment alert. A "supplier" who offers goods at a price that seems too good to be true. Once you know how these tricks work, you can spot them in seconds.

## The Most Common Scams Targeting Nigerian Businesses

The first step to protection is knowing what you are up against. These are the scams Nigerian business owners report most often.

**Fake payment alerts.** A customer sends you a screenshot of a bank transfer, or a text message that looks like a debit alert from your bank. The money never arrives, but the screenshot looks convincing. You release the goods, and the "customer" disappears. Some fraudsters even use apps that generate fake alert messages that look identical to real bank notifications.

**Phishing messages.** You receive an SMS, email or WhatsApp message claiming to be from your bank, a delivery company, or a platform you use. It warns that your account will be blocked unless you click a link and "verify" your details. The link opens a fake website that looks like the real one, and anything you type there — your password, your PIN, your card details — goes straight to the fraudster.

**Business impersonation.** A scammer copies your business name, logo and photos, then creates a fake Instagram page or WhatsApp account pretending to be you. They collect payments from your customers for goods that will never be delivered. Your real customers lose money, and your reputation takes the hit.

**Fake suppliers and bulk deals.** Someone contacts you offering goods at a price far below market value — maybe imported phones, bales of clothes, or bags of rice. They ask for a deposit or full payment upfront, then vanish. They may even send you a fake company registration document or a borrowed office address to look legitimate.

**The overpayment trick.** A "customer" sends you more than the price of your goods, then asks you to refund the difference quickly. The original payment was made with a stolen card or a fake transfer, so it gets reversed — but the refund you sent is real money, gone for good.

## How to Verify Payments Before Releasing Goods

Fake payment alerts are the single biggest cause of losses for Nigerian online sellers. Build a habit that makes this scam impossible.

First, never release goods based on a screenshot or a text message alone. Screenshots can be faked in seconds, and SMS alerts can be forged. The only proof of payment that matters is your own bank balance. Open your bank app yourself — do not use a link someone sent you — and check that the money is actually in your account.

Third, be patient with transfers. Bank transfers in Nigeria are usually instant, but network delays happen. If a customer is rushing you — "I need it now, my driver is waiting" — that urgency is itself a warning sign. A genuine customer will wait the few minutes it takes for you to confirm the payment in your own app.

Fourth, for large orders, use a dedicated business account that you check before every dispatch, and agree your payment terms in writing before you start work — see our guide on [how to send a quotation that wins the job](/blog/how-to-send-quotation-that-wins-the-job).

## Spotting Phishing Messages Before You Click

Phishing works because the messages look urgent and official. Slow down and check these things before you click any link or reply to any message.

**Look at the link.** Before clicking, look carefully at the web address. Fraudsters use addresses that look almost right — a misspelt bank name, an extra word, or a strange ending. If you need to log in to your bank, type the address yourself or open the official app. Never log in through a link in a message.

**Never share your PIN, OTP or password.** No bank, no delivery company and no government agency will ever ask you for your PIN, your one-time password, or your full card details. Anyone who asks for these is a fraudster, even if they claim to be helping you. This includes people who call you claiming to be bank staff.

## Protecting Your Business Accounts

Your Instagram page, your WhatsApp Business account and your email are the front door of your business. If a fraudster gets in, they can message your customers, change your prices and collect payments in your name. Lock that door properly.

Turn on two-step verification everywhere it is available — WhatsApp, Instagram, Facebook and your email. This means that even if someone gets your password, they still cannot get in without a second code. On WhatsApp, go to Settings, then Account, then Two-step verification, and set a six-digit PIN you will remember.

Use a different, strong password for each important account. A password that you reuse across Instagram, email and your bank is a single key that opens everything. Use a phrase you can remember with numbers and symbols mixed in, and never share your passwords with staff who do not need them. If an employee leaves, change the passwords they knew.

Be careful about who has access to your business accounts. If a staff member or a social media manager helps you, give them only the access they need, and review that access regularly. Remove old accounts and devices you no longer use — check the list of logged-in devices in your WhatsApp and Instagram settings at least once a month.

## Dealing with Impersonators and Fake Pages

If someone copies your business online, act fast. Report the fake account on the platform — Instagram, Facebook and WhatsApp all have a "report" option for impersonation. Ask your real customers and followers to report it too; platforms act faster when several people report the same account.

Make your real pages easy to verify. Use the same business name, logo and phone number everywhere. Add your real WhatsApp number to your Instagram bio and your website, so customers can always confirm they are talking to the real you.

## What to Do If You Have Been Scammed

If a scam has already happened, do not hide it out of shame. Acting quickly can limit the damage and may help others avoid the same trap.

First, contact your bank immediately and report the transaction. If the money went to another Nigerian bank, your bank can raise a complaint and request a recall. This does not always recover the money, but it is your best chance, and it is strongest in the first hours.

Second, report the fraudster. Keep screenshots of the conversation, the phone number, the account details and any payment evidence. Report the number and account to your bank, to the platform where you met them, and to the Economic and Financial Crimes Commission (EFCC) through their official channels. Your report helps build a record that can protect the next business owner.

Third, warn your customers and your network. A short post explaining what happened — without sharing sensitive details — helps your customers stay alert and shows that you are honest and transparent.

Finally, review what went wrong and fix it. Was it a payment you did not verify, a link you clicked in a hurry, or a password that was too simple? Change the process so it cannot happen the same way twice.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How can I tell if a payment alert is real or fake?

The only reliable check is your own bank app or your account balance. Open your banking app yourself and confirm the money is in your account. Never trust a screenshot, an SMS, or a forwarded message as proof of payment, and never release goods until you have confirmed the balance yourself.

### What should I do if I clicked a suspicious link?

Do not enter any details on the page. Close it immediately. If you already entered your password, change it right away from the official app or website and turn on two-step verification. If you entered bank or card details, call your bank at once and ask them to watch or block the account.

### Can a scammer really copy my whole Instagram business page?

Yes, and it happens often. They copy your photos, your bio and your business name, then message your followers pretending to be you. Protect yourself by turning on two-factor authentication, watermarking your product photos, and reporting fake pages quickly — and ask your followers to report them too.

### Is it safe to accept bank transfers from new customers?

Yes, as long as you verify the payment in your own bank app before releasing goods or starting work. Transfers are safe when confirmed properly. The risk is not the transfer itself — it is trusting a screenshot or a message instead of checking your real balance.

## Conclusion

Online scams are a cost of doing business online in Nigeria, but they do not have to be a cost your business pays. Verify every payment in your own bank app, never click links in urgent messages, never share your PIN or OTP, lock your accounts with two-step verification, and act fast if something goes wrong. Teach these habits to everyone who works with you.
