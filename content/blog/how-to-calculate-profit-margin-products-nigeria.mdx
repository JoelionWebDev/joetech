## Why Profit Margin Matters More Than Sales

Many Nigerian small business owners celebrate a busy day. The shop was full, orders kept coming in, and money entered the account. Yet at the end of the month, there is little left. Rent is due, suppliers need to be paid, and the owner wonders where all the money went.

Sales alone do not tell you whether your business is healthy. Profit margin does. It shows how much of each naira you earn is actually profit after you pay for the product and the costs of selling it. A trader who sells goods worth ₦2,000,000 in a month is not automatically doing better than a trader who sells goods worth ₦800,000. If the first trader spends almost everything on stock, transport, packaging and delivery, the second trader may keep more money.

This guide explains profit margin in plain language: the difference between gross and net margin, the exact formulas, worked naira examples, hidden costs to watch, common pricing mistakes, how to raise prices, and how to track margin per product.

Whether you sell clothes in Balogun Market, food in Lekki, shoes in Onitsha, cosmetics in Abuja, or handmade goods in Port Harcourt, the same maths applies.

## What Is Profit Margin?

Profit margin is the percentage of your selling price that remains as profit after costs are removed. It answers one simple question: for every ₦100 a customer pays you, how much do you keep?

There are two main types you need to know. Gross profit margin looks at the direct cost of the product itself. Net profit margin looks at all the costs of running the business. Both matter, and confusing them is one of the most common reasons small businesses underprice their products.

Gross margin tells you whether the product itself is profitable. Net margin tells you whether the whole business is profitable after every other expense. A product can have a good gross margin and still lose money once delivery, packaging, data, bank charges and market levies are added. You need accurate numbers, a calculator, and the discipline to include every cost.

## Gross Profit Margin Explained

Gross profit is your selling price minus the direct cost of getting the product ready to sell. That direct cost is often called the cost of goods sold. For a trader, it is mainly what you paid the supplier for the item. For a food vendor, it includes the raw ingredients for that dish. For a fashion seller, it includes fabric, sewing, and any direct production cost for that item.

The formula is:

Gross Profit = Selling Price − Cost of Goods Sold

Gross Profit Margin = (Gross Profit ÷ Selling Price) × 100

Let us work through a simple example. A trader in Lagos buys a pair of sneakers for ₦18,000 and sells it for ₦28,000. The gross profit is ₦28,000 minus ₦18,000, which is ₦10,000. The gross margin is ₦10,000 divided by ₦28,000, multiplied by 100. That gives about 35.7 percent. For every ₦100 the customer pays, ₦35.70 is gross profit before other business costs.

A higher gross margin gives you more room to cover transport, packaging, rent, and staff. A low gross margin means you must sell a very large quantity to make meaningful money.

## Net Profit Margin Explained

Net profit margin goes further. It subtracts every business expense, not just the cost of the product. That includes transport, delivery, packaging, airtime and data, POS charges, market levies, rent, salaries, electricity, generator fuel, and any other cost linked to selling that product.

The formula is:

Net Profit = Selling Price − All Costs

Net Profit Margin = (Net Profit ÷ Selling Price) × 100

Using the same sneakers example, suppose the trader pays ₦1,500 for transport from the wholesale market, ₦500 for packaging, ₦700 for delivery to the customer, and ₦300 in POS charges. The total extra cost is ₦3,000. The total cost is now ₦18,000 plus ₦3,000, which is ₦21,000. The net profit is ₦28,000 minus ₦21,000, which is ₦7,000. The net margin is ₦7,000 divided by ₦28,000, multiplied by 100. That is 25 percent.

Notice the difference. The gross margin looked like 35.7 percent, but the real margin after everyday costs is 25 percent. If the trader only looked at gross margin, they would think the business was doing better than it really was. That is why you must calculate both.

A healthy net margin depends on the product type. Fast-moving goods often work on thinner margins and rely on volume, while handmade or specialised products can support wider margins. What matters most is knowing your own number and watching whether it is rising or falling.

## Step-by-Step: How to Calculate Profit Margin on Any Product

Use these five steps for every product you sell. Do it once, write it down, and repeat it whenever your supplier price, transport fare, or packaging cost changes.

### Step 1: Write Down Your Selling Price

Start with the exact price the customer pays. If you offer a discount, use the discounted price, not the original price. If you sell in packs or bundles, calculate per pack or per bundle. Do not mix units.

### Step 2: List the Direct Cost of the Product

This is what you paid to get the product itself. For a reseller, it is the supplier price. For a food vendor, add up the ingredients for one portion. For a tailor, add fabric, lining, thread, buttons, zip, and any direct labour paid per piece.

### Step 3: Add Every Hidden Cost

This is the step most sellers skip. List transport, delivery, packaging, POS charges, data and airtime, market levies, and any commission paid to a rider or agent. Even small amounts matter: a ₦200 packaging cost on a ₦2,000 item is 10 percent of the price.

### Step 4: Calculate Gross Profit and Gross Margin

Subtract the direct product cost from the selling price to get gross profit. Divide gross profit by selling price and multiply by 100 to get gross margin as a percentage.

### Step 5: Calculate Net Profit and Net Margin

Add the direct cost and all the hidden costs together. Subtract that total from the selling price to get net profit. Divide net profit by selling price and multiply by 100 to get net margin. Write both percentages down. If the net margin is very low or negative, the product is not working at that price. You need to reduce a cost, change supplier, reduce packaging cost, or raise the price.

## Worked Example 1: Buying and Reselling Goods

A trader buys female handbags from a wholesaler and sells them online and from a small shop in Lagos. Here is the breakdown for one bag.

Selling price: ₦35,000. Wholesale cost: ₦20,000. Transport from the wholesale market: ₦2,000. Packaging: ₦800. Delivery to the customer within Lagos: ₦1,500. POS charge: ₦500. Data and airtime for customer chats: ₦400.

Gross profit is ₦35,000 minus ₦20,000, which is ₦15,000. Gross margin is ₦15,000 divided by ₦35,000, multiplied by 100. That is about 42.9 percent.

Extra costs add up to ₦5,200. Total cost is ₦20,000 plus ₦5,200, which is ₦25,200. Net profit is ₦35,000 minus ₦25,200, which is ₦9,800. Net margin is ₦9,800 divided by ₦35,000, multiplied by 100. That is 28 percent.

The trader keeps ₦9,800 on each bag after all listed costs. If the trader sells 20 bags in a month, the total net profit from bags is ₦196,000 before rent, salaries, and other fixed monthly costs. Margin per product and total monthly sales must be looked at together.

## Worked Example 2: A Food Vendor in Lagos

A food vendor in Lekki sells a plate of jollof rice with chicken. Food businesses have many small ingredient costs, so the vendor must add them carefully.

Selling price per plate: ₦4,500. Rice, pepper, tomatoes, oil, seasoning and chicken for one plate: ₦2,200. Takeaway pack and cutlery: ₦300. Gas and cooking cost per plate: ₦250. Delivery by rider: ₦600. POS charge: ₦100.

Gross profit is ₦4,500 minus ₦2,200, which is ₦2,300. Gross margin is ₦2,300 divided by ₦4,500, multiplied by 100. That is about 51.1 percent.

Total extra costs are ₦1,250. Total cost is ₦2,200 plus ₦1,250, which is ₦3,450. Net profit is ₦4,500 minus ₦3,450, which is ₦1,050. Net margin is ₦1,050 divided by ₦4,500, multiplied by 100. That is about 23.3 percent.

The vendor keeps ₦1,050 per plate. If the vendor sells 40 plates a day, that is ₦42,000 in daily net profit before rent and staff pay. If the vendor sells only 10 plates a day, the daily net profit is ₦10,500, which may not cover fixed costs. This is why food vendors must watch both margin per plate and the number of plates sold each day.

Small changes matter: if the ingredient cost rises from ₦2,200 to ₦2,600, net profit falls to ₦650 per plate and net margin drops to about 14.4 percent.

## Worked Example 3: Fashion Items in Abuja

A fashion seller in Abuja makes and sells a ready-to-wear Ankara dress.

Selling price: ₦45,000. Fabric: ₦12,000. Lining, thread, zip and buttons: ₦3,500. Tailor labour per dress: ₦8,000. Steaming and finishing: ₦1,000. Packaging: ₦1,200. Delivery within Abuja: ₦2,000. POS charge: ₦700.

Direct production cost is ₦24,500. Gross profit is ₦45,000 minus ₦24,500, which is ₦20,500. Gross margin is ₦20,500 divided by ₦45,000, multiplied by 100. That is about 45.6 percent.

Extra costs are ₦3,900. Total cost is ₦24,500 plus ₦3,900, which is ₦28,400. Net profit is ₦45,000 minus ₦28,400, which is ₦16,600. Net margin is ₦16,600 divided by ₦45,000, multiplied by 100. That is about 36.9 percent.

This is a strong margin, but if the seller gives a ₦5,000 discount without changing costs, net profit becomes ₦11,600 and net margin falls to 29 percent. Discounts are not free. Always recalculate margin after any discount.

## Hidden Costs That Quietly Eat Your Profit

Many Nigerian sellers lose money not because their product is bad, but because they forget small costs that repeat every day.

Transport and delivery come first. This includes trips to the wholesale market, moving stock to your shop, and sending orders to customers. If you use a dispatch rider, include the full rider fee. If you deliver yourself, include fuel and the value of your time on the road.

Packaging is next: bags, boxes, wraps, labels, and takeaway packs all cost money, and a ₦1,500 box changes the margin on a ₦10,000 item in a big way. Bank and payment charges also add up. If most customers pay by card or transfer, add the average POS or transfer charge per sale.

Data, airtime, and internet are business costs too. If you spend ₦15,000 a month on data and make 150 sales, that is about ₦100 per sale. Add a fair share to each product. Market levies, shop dues, and association fees work the same way: divide the monthly total by the number of items you sell to get a cost per item. The same method works for rent, electricity, and generator fuel. Also track breakage, spoilage, and returns, and adjust your pricing if those losses are growing.

## Pricing Mistakes That Kill Profit

The first mistake is copying a competitor's price without knowing their costs. A competitor may buy cheaper, have lower rent, or sell in larger quantities. Their price may work for them and fail for you. Price from your own costs, then check the market. Do not price from the market alone.

The second mistake is forgetting delivery. Many sellers advertise a price and then offer free delivery to attract buyers. Free delivery is not free for you. Either include delivery in the price, charge it separately, or set a minimum order for free delivery. A seller who absorbs ₦2,000 delivery on a ₦8,000 item has already given away 25 percent of the price.

The third mistake is giving discounts too easily. A 10 percent discount sounds small, but it can cut your net profit by a third or more. If your net margin is 20 percent and you give a 10 percent discount, you have given away half your margin. Offer discounts with a clear reason, such as a bulk order or a slow-moving item you need to clear. Always calculate the new margin before you agree.

The fourth mistake is pricing only to cover the product cost. If you buy an item for ₦5,000 and sell it for ₦6,000, you may think you made ₦1,000. After transport, packaging, data, and POS charges, you may have made nothing. Always price to cover total cost, not just supplier cost.

The fifth mistake is never reviewing prices. Supplier prices rise, fuel prices change, and packaging costs go up. A price that worked six months ago may now lose money. Review your top-selling products every month and adjust where costs have risen. The sixth mistake is selling too many low-margin products and ignoring high-margin ones. Track margin per product and focus your energy on the products that reward you.

## How to Raise Prices Without Losing Customers

Many sellers fear raising prices. They worry customers will leave and sales will fall. In practice, a careful price increase often works better than expected, especially when customers understand the value they receive.

Start by raising prices on your best products first, not everything at once. Choose one or two products where demand is strong and customers already love what you sell. A small increase is easier for customers to accept than a sudden large jump.

Explain the value, not just the price. If your food tastes better, your portion is generous, your fabric is original, your delivery is fast, or your after-sales care is strong, make that clear. Customers pay for the full experience, not only the item in the bag.

Improve something small at the same time, such as better packaging, faster replies on WhatsApp, or a neater presentation. Test the new price for two weeks and listen to feedback. If sales hold steady, the new price works. If sales fall sharply, review whether the increase was too large. State the new price clearly, keep your service excellent, and let the quality speak.

## Tracking Margin Per Product With a Simple Spreadsheet

You do not need expensive software to track profit margin. A simple spreadsheet on your phone or laptop is enough. Create one sheet with these columns: product name, selling price, supplier or production cost, transport, packaging, delivery, payment charges, other costs, total cost, gross profit, gross margin, net profit, and net margin.

Add one row per product. Fill in the numbers you already know. For costs that change, update the row when the price changes. Use simple formulas so the sheet calculates gross profit, net profit, and both margins automatically.

Review the sheet every week. Sort products by net margin. Highlight products with low or negative margins. Those are the products to fix first. You can raise the price, find a cheaper supplier, use simpler packaging, or stop selling the product if it never makes money.

Also track total profit per product, not just margin percentage: multiply net profit per unit by units sold. Keep a second sheet for monthly fixed costs such as rent, salaries, electricity, generator fuel, data, and market dues. At the end of the month, add total net profit from all products and subtract fixed costs. If you send invoices to customers, keep them consistent and professional. ## A Simple Monthly Checklist

Use this checklist at the end of every month. Recalculate margins for your top five products with current costs. Add up all discounts given and compare them to your total profit. Add up delivery and rider costs, and check slow-moving stock and fixed costs such as rent, fuel, data, and dues. Then write down one action for next month: raising one price, changing a supplier, reducing packaging cost, or stopping a product that loses money.

## Final Thoughts

Profit margin is a practical tool for every Nigerian small business owner. Calculate gross margin to see whether the product itself works, and net margin to see whether the business works after every cost. Include hidden costs, review prices regularly, and track each product separately.

Start today with your best-selling product. Write down its selling price, its direct cost, and every extra cost linked to selling it, then work out the gross and net margin. Good pricing is not about charging the highest possible price. It is about charging a fair price that covers your costs, rewards your work, and keeps customers happy.
