Tolu sells trainers from a shop in Onitsha. One afternoon a customer asked for a discount, Tolu agreed without thinking, and by evening he realised the sale had earned him almost nothing. He had not misjudged the customer. He had misjudged his own numbers — the same misjudgement that sits quietly in thousands of Nigerian shops, where the owner knows the buying price and the selling price and still cannot say, to the naira, what a sale actually earns.

The problem is rarely laziness. It is that three different ideas — gross margin, net margin and markup — sound like the same thing, and they are not. They use the same two numbers, cost and price, and combine them differently. Get the combination wrong and a price you believed carried 40 percent profit carries 28 percent. Do that across a hundred sales and the missing money is a month's rent.

This article works every formula on the same set of numbers, so you can see the differences with your own eyes instead of taking them on trust. One product, one seller, every formula, both ways.

## The Product We Will Follow

Tolu buys a pair of trainers from his supplier for ₦22,000 and sells it for ₦34,000. Keep those two numbers in mind. Everything below is built from them, plus the costs of actually getting the trainers to a customer: transport from the wholesale market ₦1,200, packaging ₦500, delivery by rider ₦1,500, POS charges ₦400, and data and airtime for the customer chats ₦200. Those extra costs add up to ₦3,800.

The trainers will carry us through gross margin, net margin, markup, and the price-setting formula. When the article switches to a second product, it will say so plainly.

## Gross Margin: What the Product Itself Earns

Gross profit is the selling price minus the direct cost of the product — what you paid the supplier, or what the materials and direct labour cost if you make it yourself. For Tolu's trainers, that is ₦34,000 minus ₦22,000, which is ₦12,000.

Gross margin turns that naira figure into a percentage of the selling price:

Gross profit margin = (Gross profit ÷ Selling price) × 100

For the trainers: ₦12,000 divided by ₦34,000, times 100, is 35.3 percent. Out of every ₦100 a customer pays for the trainers, ₦35.30 is gross profit before the costs of selling them.

Gross margin answers one question and one question only: does this product earn enough, before the everyday costs of running the business, to be worth selling? A gross margin of 35 percent gives Tolu room. A gross margin of 8 percent would not, because transport and delivery alone would swallow it. Gross margin is the first check, and plenty of sellers stop there. That is the first place the trouble starts, because gross margin is not what lands in your account.

## Net Margin: What the Business Actually Keeps

Net margin goes further. It subtracts every cost tied to the sale, not just the supplier price. Tolu's extra costs are ₦3,800, so his total cost is ₦22,000 plus ₦3,800, which is ₦25,800. His net profit is ₦34,000 minus ₦25,800, which is ₦8,200.

Net profit margin = (Net profit ÷ Selling price) × 100

For the trainers: ₦8,200 divided by ₦34,000, times 100, is 24.1 percent. Out of every ₦100 the customer pays, Tolu keeps ₦24.10 after every cost on the list.

Look at the two percentages side by side. Gross margin says 35.3 percent. Net margin says 24.1 percent. The gap between them — more than eleven points — is transport, packaging, delivery, POS charges and data. None of those are optional. None of them appear in the supplier's price. A seller who only watches gross margin walks around believing the business earns a third on every sale, while the real figure is under a quarter. Over a month of selling twenty pairs, that gap is ₦76,000 of profit that exists on paper and never reaches the account — ₦3,800 per pair, twenty times over.

Net margin is the number that decides whether the business survives. Gross margin decides whether the product is worth stocking. You need both, and you need to know which one you are quoting when you say "my margin is 35 percent."

## Markup vs Margin: The Same Numbers, Two Different Answers

Here is the mix-up that costs sellers real money. Markup and margin both describe the ₦12,000 gross profit on Tolu's trainers. Markup divides it by the cost. Margin divides it by the price.

Markup = (Profit ÷ Cost) × 100 = ₦12,000 ÷ ₦22,000 × 100 = 54.5 percent

Gross margin = (Profit ÷ Price) × 100 = ₦12,000 ÷ ₦34,000 × 100 = 35.3 percent

Same profit, same trainers, and the answers are nineteen points apart. Markup is always the bigger number, because the cost is always smaller than the price. Remember that, because the confusion runs in one direction: sellers add a markup and believe they have set a margin.

Tolu made exactly this mistake last year. He decided he wanted a 40 percent margin on a new line of trainers, so he added 40 percent to his cost. His cost was ₦22,000, so he priced them at ₦30,800 — ₦22,000 plus 40 percent of ₦22,000, which is ₦8,800. He told himself the margin was 40 percent. It was not. The margin was ₦8,800 divided by ₦30,800, times 100 — 28.6 percent. He had given away more than eleven points of margin with a single wrong step, and on twenty pairs that was ₦5,867 per pair he thought he was earning and was not, or ₦117,340 across the month.

The table below shows the same numbers worked both ways, so the pattern is visible at a glance.

| What you do | Calculation | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Add 40% markup to ₦22,000 cost | ₦22,000 × 1.40 | Price ₦30,800 |
| Margin you actually get at ₦30,800 | ₦8,800 ÷ ₦30,800 × 100 | 28.6% margin |
| Price you need for a true 40% margin | ₦22,000 ÷ (1 − 0.40) | Price ₦36,667 |
| Margin you actually get at ₦36,667 | ₦14,667 ÷ ₦36,667 × 100 | 40.0% margin |

That last row holds the formula for setting a price from a margin you actually want, instead of a markup you hope is the same thing:

Selling price = Total cost ÷ (1 − Target margin)

If Tolu wants a true 30 percent net margin on the trainers, and his total cost including the ₦3,800 of selling costs is ₦25,800, the price is ₦25,800 divided by 0.70, which is ₦36,857. He rounds it to ₦37,000. Check it: profit at ₦37,000 is ₦11,200, and ₦11,200 divided by ₦37,000 is 30.3 percent. The formula works because it divides by what is left after the margin, not by the cost. Use it whenever a customer, a supplier or a voice in your head says "just add 40 percent" — and ask whether that 40 percent is a markup or a margin, because the price differs by thousands.

## A Second Product, the Same Formulas

The formulas behave the same way on every product, but the numbers feel different when the product is made rather than bought. Take an Ankara dress made by a tailor in Abuja. The fabric costs ₦12,000, lining and notions ₦3,500, the tailor's labour ₦8,000, steaming and finishing ₦1,000. The direct cost is ₦24,500, and she sells the dress for ₦45,000. Gross profit is ₦20,500 and gross margin is 45.6 percent. Markup is 83.7 percent — ₦20,500 divided by ₦24,500 — which is why markup figures for handmade goods always look enormous. They are not wrong. They are measuring something else.

Add the selling costs — packaging ₦1,200, delivery ₦2,000, POS charges ₦700 — and the total cost becomes ₦28,400. Net profit is ₦16,600 and net margin is 36.9 percent. Now give a customer a ₦5,000 discount without changing anything else. The price becomes ₦40,000, net profit falls to ₦11,600, and net margin drops to 29 percent. A discount that sounded like a small kindness took nearly a third of the profit on that dress. Run the discount through the formula before you offer it, every time.

## The Mistakes That Hide Inside the Maths

Four mistakes turn good formulas into bad prices, and all four are visible in the examples above.

Forgetting delivery is the first. Tolu's rider charges ₦1,500 to deliver a pair of trainers inside Lagos and far more to Abuja. A seller who quotes ₦34,000 and then absorbs delivery has not sold trainers at a 24.1 percent net margin. He has sold them at a margin closer to 20 percent, and he will not notice until the month ends short. Decide in advance whether delivery is inside the price or added on top, and say so before the customer pays.

Counting revenue as profit is the second. Tolu once told a friend he had "made ₦680,000" in a month because he sold twenty pairs at ₦34,000. He had taken in ₦680,000. His total costs on those twenty pairs were ₦516,000, so his net profit before rent was ₦164,000. Revenue is what passes through your hands. Profit is what stays. The margin formula only works on profit, and a seller who confuses the two will spend money that was never theirs.

Pricing from competitors instead of costs is the third. The trader two stalls down sells similar trainers for ₦30,000. Tolu's total cost is ₦25,800, so at ₦30,000 his net margin would be 14 percent — thin, and one faulty pair away from nothing. The other trader might buy in bulk, might have no rent, might be clearing old stock. His price tells you nothing about your costs. Price from your own numbers first, then look at the market and decide whether your price can stand next to it. If it cannot, the answer is usually a cheaper supplier or a better product, not a price that loses money.

Forgetting the small costs is the fourth, and it is the quietest. Data at ₦200 a sale, a POS charge at ₦400, packaging at ₦500 — each one looks too small to matter, and together they are ₦3,800 on Tolu's trainers, more than a tenth of the price. A seller who forgets ₦500 of costs on every sale loses ₦50,000 after a hundred sales. The formula cannot protect you from costs you never wrote down.

## Doing It on One Product Tonight

Take your best-selling product and a calculator. Write down the selling price. Write down the direct cost. Subtract, divide by the price, and you have gross margin. Now list every other cost tied to selling it — transport, packaging, delivery, payment charges, data, levies — add them to the direct cost, subtract from the price, divide by the price, and you have net margin. Then divide the gross profit by the cost, and you have markup. Three percentages from the same product, and now you know which is which.

If the net margin is under 15 percent, the product is fragile: one discount or one cost rise breaks it. If it is over 40 percent, ask whether the price could come down a little and sell twice as fast. And if you have been adding a percentage to your cost and calling it your margin, redo the price with the formula above. The difference, on Tolu's trainers, was ₦5,867 a pair. Across a month of sales, that is not a rounding error. It is the business.
