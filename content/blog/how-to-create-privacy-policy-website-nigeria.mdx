Open almost any serious business website in Nigeria today and you will find a small link at the bottom of the page that says "Privacy Policy". Many small business owners skip it. They assume it is only for big companies, banks and tech startups. That assumption is wrong, and it can be expensive.

If your website collects a visitor's name, phone number, email address, home address, or payment details, you are collecting personal data. It does not matter whether you are a one-person fashion business in Lekki, a restaurant in Ikeja, a school in Abuja, or a real estate agency in Port Harcourt. Once you collect personal data, the law expects you to tell people what you collect, why you collect it, and what you do with it.

In Nigeria, that law is the Nigeria Data Protection Act, usually called the NDPA, signed into law in 2023 and enforced by the Nigeria Data Protection Commission, the NDPC. It applies to every business that processes personal data in Nigeria, including small businesses and sole traders.

The good news is that creating a privacy policy is not difficult. You do not need a lawyer to write a first version, and you do not need pages of complicated legal language. You need a clear, honest document written in plain English that tells your visitors the truth about their data. This guide will walk you through it step by step, explain what the NDPA requires, and give you a structure you can copy for your own website.

## What Is a Privacy Policy, Really?

A privacy policy is a public promise. It is the page on your website where you explain, in simple language, what personal information you collect from visitors, why you need it, how you use it, who you share it with, how long you keep it, and what rights visitors have over their own information.

Think of it as the data version of a shop signboard. When a customer walks into your shop, they can see what you sell and how you treat people. On a website, they cannot see any of that. The privacy policy is how you show them. It answers the question every careful visitor asks, even if only in their head: "If I give this business my number, what will they do with it?"

A good privacy policy builds trust. A missing one destroys it. Imagine a customer in Onitsha about to order goods worth ₦150,000 from your online store. They reach the checkout, they are asked for their name, phone number and delivery address, and then they look for some sign that this business is legitimate. No privacy policy, no terms, no contact address. Many will close the tab and buy from a competitor instead.

## What the NDPA Requires From Your Business

You do not need to memorise the whole Nigeria Data Protection Act, but you should understand its core ideas, because your privacy policy must reflect them.

**Lawful basis.** You must have a good reason to collect personal data. The most common reasons for a small business are that the customer asked you to do something, like deliver an order or send a quote, or that the customer gave you consent, like signing up for your newsletter or WhatsApp broadcast list. Collect data only when you have a reason.

**Purpose limitation.** Collect data only for the purpose you stated. If a customer gives you their phone number to deliver a cake, you cannot then add them to a daily marketing broadcast without their permission. The number was given for delivery, not for advertising.

**Data minimisation.** Collect only what you need. If you run a booking form for a hair appointment, you need a name and a phone number. You do not need a home address, date of birth and state of origin. Every extra field you add is extra responsibility.

**Accuracy.** Keep the information you hold correct and up to date. If a customer tells you their number has changed, update it.

**Storage limitation.** Do not keep personal data forever. When you no longer need it, delete it.

**Security.** Protect the data you hold. Use strong passwords, do not share your admin login, and be careful about who in your team can see customer details.

**Transparency.** Tell people what you are doing with their data. This is exactly what the privacy policy is for.

**Respect for rights.** Under the NDPA, people have the right to ask what data you hold about them, to ask you to correct it, to ask you to delete it, and to withdraw their consent for marketing. Your policy should explain how they can do that, usually by contacting you by email or WhatsApp.

None of this is unreasonable. It is simply treating people's information the way you would want your own information treated.

## Before You Write: List Everything Your Website Collects

Before you write a single sentence of your privacy policy, you need to know what your website actually collects. Many business owners are surprised when they make this list, because a website collects more than they think.

**Step 1: Walk through your website like a customer.** Open your homepage, your contact page, your checkout, your newsletter signup and your booking form. Write down every field a visitor is asked to fill in: name, email, phone number, address, and anything else.

**Step 2: List your contact channels.** If your site has a WhatsApp button, a contact form, or a live chat, note that messages sent through them contain personal data. If you use a form plugin, check where the submissions are stored and who receives them by email.

**Step 3: List your tools and services.** Your website almost certainly uses third-party services that collect data: Google Analytics, which tracks how visitors use your site; payment providers such as Paystack, Flutterwave, Opay or Moniepoint; email tools such as Mailchimp or Brevo; and social media embeds such as Instagram feeds or Facebook pixels.

**Step 4: Note what you collect offline that connects to the website.** If customers order on WhatsApp after seeing your website, or pay by transfer and send a screenshot, that data is part of the same picture.

**Step 5: Write down how long you keep each type of data.** Order records might be kept for one year for accounting. Marketing lists are kept until someone unsubscribes. Enquiry messages might be deleted after six months. You do not need exact dates for everything, but you need a sensible rule.

This audit usually takes less than an hour, and it is the most important part of the whole exercise. A privacy policy written without it is guesswork. A policy written after it is accurate.

## The Sections Every Nigerian Business Privacy Policy Needs

Now you can write the policy itself. Use plain English, short sentences and clear headings. Here are the sections to include, in order.

### 1. Introduction and who you are

Start with your business name, your website address, and a one-sentence statement of what the policy covers. Include your contact details so a visitor can reach you about their data. For example: "This Privacy Policy explains how Joetech (joetech360.ng) collects, uses and protects your personal information when you visit our website or contact us. If you have any questions, email us at info@joetech360.ng."

### 2. What information we collect

List everything, grouped clearly. A typical small business list looks like this: contact details you provide, such as your name, phone number, email address and delivery address; order and payment information, such as what you bought and how you paid; messages you send us through our contact form, WhatsApp or email; and technical information collected automatically, such as your device type, browser, and the pages you visit, collected through tools like Google Analytics.

### 3. How we use your information

Explain each purpose in plain language. For example: to process and deliver your order; to reply to your enquiries; to send you updates about your order; to send marketing messages only if you agreed to receive them; to improve our website and services; and to meet legal and tax obligations, such as keeping sales records. Be specific. Vague phrases like "to improve our services" mean little on their own. Pair them with real examples.

### 4. Who we share your information with

Be honest about the third parties involved. Most small businesses share data with a payment provider to process payments, a delivery company to deliver orders, an email service to send newsletters, and analytics tools to understand website traffic. Name them where you can. Also state clearly that you do not sell personal information to anyone. If you use WhatsApp Business, note that messages are handled through Meta's WhatsApp service. Customers appreciate this honesty.

### 5. How we protect your information

Describe, in general terms, the steps you take. Examples: our website uses HTTPS encryption; access to customer data is limited to staff who need it; we use strong passwords and two-factor authentication where available; payment details are processed by our payment provider and we do not store your card number. Do not reveal security details that could help an attacker, but give enough to show you take protection seriously.

### 6. How long we keep your information

State your retention rules simply. For example: "We keep order records for up to two years for accounting purposes. We keep marketing subscriber details until you unsubscribe. Enquiry messages are deleted after six months. When information is no longer needed, we delete it securely."

### 7. Your rights under the NDPA

List the rights your visitors have, and explain how to exercise them. Under the NDPA, a person can ask for a copy of the data you hold about them; ask you to correct inaccurate data; ask you to delete their data; withdraw consent for marketing at any time; and complain to the Nigeria Data Protection Commission if they believe their data has been misused. Give a clear contact method, such as an email address, and say you will respond within a reasonable time.

### 8. Cookies

If your website uses cookies, say so. Cookies are small files stored on a visitor's device. They remember preferences, keep items in a shopping cart, and help tools like Google Analytics understand traffic. Explain what cookies you use and that visitors can disable them in their browser settings, noting that some parts of the site may not work properly without them. If you use a cookie consent banner, mention it here.

### 9. Children's privacy

If your business serves children, for example a school, a creche or a children's clothing brand, state how you handle children's data. As a rule, do not knowingly collect personal data from children under 18 without a parent or guardian's consent. If you run a school website with an admission form, the form should be completed by a parent or guardian.

### 10. Changes to this policy

State that you may update the policy from time to time and that the date at the top will show the latest version. If you make a major change, for example starting to use a new marketing tool, let your subscribers know.

### 11. Contact us

End with your contact details again: business name, email, phone number and physical address if you have one. Make it easy for someone to reach you. A policy that hides the business behind a form with no email address looks suspicious.

## Writing Style: Plain English Wins

A privacy policy written in heavy legal language helps nobody. Your customers are not lawyers, and a document they cannot understand does not build trust. Write the way you speak to a customer in your shop. Short sentences. Everyday words. No jargon unless you explain it.

Compare these two versions. Legal version: "The Data Controller shall process the Data Subject's personal data in accordance with the provisions of the extant regulatory framework." Plain version: "We collect your name and phone number so we can deliver your order and contact you about it." Both say similar things. Only the second one will be read.

Avoid copying a privacy policy from a foreign website word for word. Templates from the United States or Europe often mention laws like the GDPR or the CCPA, which do not apply in Nigeria, and may describe practices your business does not follow. A copied policy that is wrong is worse than no policy at all, because it makes promises you are not keeping.

## How to Publish It on Your Website

Writing the policy is half the job. Publishing it properly is the other half.

**Step 1: Create a dedicated page.** The policy should live at an address like yourwebsite.com/privacy-policy. Do not hide it inside another page or a PDF that is hard to open on a phone.

**Step 2: Link it in the footer.** Add a "Privacy Policy" link to the footer of every page on your website. This is where visitors expect to find it, and it is also where regulators and ad platforms look.

**Step 3: Link it wherever you collect data.** Add the link near your contact form, your checkout, your newsletter signup and your booking form. A short line works well: "By submitting this form, you agree to our Privacy Policy." Make the words "Privacy Policy" a clickable link.

**Step 4: Add a cookie notice if you use analytics or ads.** A small banner that says your site uses cookies, with a button to accept, is now standard. Most website builders and WordPress plugins offer this for free.

**Step 5: Keep it updated.** Review your policy at least once a year, and update it whenever you add a new tool, a new payment method, or a new way of collecting data.

## Common Mistakes Nigerian Businesses Make

**Copying a foreign template without changing it.** A policy that mentions American laws and American addresses tells visitors you did not write it for your business.

**Writing a policy that does not match reality.** If your policy says you never share data with third parties, but your checkout sends customer details to Paystack and a dispatch rider, the policy is false. Accuracy matters more than length.

**Hiding the policy.** A policy buried three clicks deep or written in tiny grey text signals that you have something to hide. Put it in the footer and link it openly.

**Collecting data you do not need.** Long forms with ten fields when two would do. Every field is a liability. Remove what you do not use.

**Adding every WhatsApp number to a broadcast list.** This is one of the most common complaints in Nigeria. A customer who gave you their number to ask about a price did not agree to receive daily broadcast messages. Always ask for permission before adding someone to marketing lists, and always honour an opt-out immediately.

**Never updating the policy.** A policy dated 2021 on a website that added online payments in 2025 is out of date. Review it yearly.

## What Happens If You Ignore the NDPA?

The NDPC has the power to investigate complaints and to fine businesses that break the law. For small businesses, the realistic risk is not usually a huge fine on day one. It is the slow damage: lost customers who do not trust you, rejected ad accounts, payment providers asking questions, and a complaint from an unhappy customer that forces you to explain yourself without any policy to point to.

## Your Privacy Policy Checklist

- [ ] Audit everything your website collects, including tools like analytics and payment providers
- [ ] Write the policy in plain English with the eleven sections above
- [ ] Name the third parties you share data with, such as Paystack, Flutterwave and your delivery company
- [ ] Explain visitors' rights under the NDPA and how to contact you about their data
- [ ] State how long you keep each type of data
- [ ] Publish it at /privacy-policy and link it in your website footer
- [ ] Link it near every form, checkout and signup on your site
- [ ] Add a cookie notice if you use analytics or advertising tools
- [ ] Only add people to marketing lists with their permission, and honour opt-outs immediately
- [ ] Review and update the policy at least once a year

A privacy policy is not paperwork for its own sake. It is a promise to your customers that you will treat their information with respect. Write it honestly and publish it openly.
