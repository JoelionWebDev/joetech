# How to Do Simple Bookkeeping for Your Small Business in Nigeria

Ask a typical Nigerian small business owner how much profit they made last month and the most common answer is a shrug, followed by something like "business is moving" or "things are tight." They know roughly how much came in and roughly how much went out, but the exact numbers live only in their head. That is not bookkeeping. That is guessing, and guessing is expensive.

Bookkeeping is simply writing down the money that comes into your business and the money that goes out, in an organised way, every day. It is not accounting in the complicated sense. You do not need to learn debits and credits or hire an accountant to start.

This guide shows you exactly how to do it. By the end, you will know how to record sales, track expenses, separate your money from the business money, check whether you actually made a profit, and keep records that a bank, a tax officer, or an investor can understand.

## Why Bookkeeping Matters More Than You Think

The first reason is profit. Most small businesses in Nigeria do not know their real profit. They know their sales. A fashion vendor in Lekki might sell ₦800,000 worth of clothes in a month and feel successful. But if she spent ₦550,000 on stock, ₦60,000 on delivery, ₦40,000 on data and advertising, ₦30,000 on packaging, and ₦50,000 on shop expenses, her real profit is ₦70,000, not ₦800,000. Without records, she might spend as if she made ₦800,000 and end the month broke, wondering where the money went.

The second reason is pricing. You cannot price correctly if you do not know your costs. A caterer in Abuja who does not record the cost of ingredients, gas, transport and her own time will undercharge for jobs and work hard for almost nothing. Bookkeeping exposes the full cost of doing business, including the small costs that are easy to forget.

The third reason is tax. The Federal Inland Revenue Service and your state internal revenue service expect businesses to keep records. When tax officers visit or when you need a Tax Clearance Certificate, proper books make the process smooth. No records means estimates, arguments, and often a higher bill than you should pay.

The fourth reason is funding. Banks, microfinance institutions, cooperatives, and grant programmes all ask the same question: show us your records. A business that can show six months of sales and expenses has a story a lender can believe. A business with no records is a risk no lender wants to take. Your bookkeeping is the evidence that your business is real, stable, and growing.

The fifth reason is theft and leakage. When every sale and every expense is recorded, missing money becomes visible. A staff member who pockets cash or a supplier who overcharges shows up in the numbers when the numbers exist. Without records, nobody can prove anything, and the leak continues.

## The Golden Rule: Separate Your Money From the Business Money

Before you record a single transaction, do this one thing. Open a separate bank account for the business. If you already have one, use it properly. If you do not, open one this week. It can be a business account with a bank, or a dedicated personal account used only for business. The important thing is that business money and personal money never mix in the same account.

Every sale should go into the business account, whether by transfer, POS, or cash deposited at the end of the day. Every business expense should be paid from the business account. Your personal spending — food, rent for your house, school fees, family expenses — comes out of your personal account. If you need money from the business for personal use, transfer a fixed amount to yourself on a set date and record it as owner drawings. Never pay for personal things directly from the business account, and never pay for business things from your personal account without recording a transfer between the two.

This separation is the foundation of everything else in this guide. Without it, your records will always be muddled, your profit will always be wrong, and your tax filings will always be a headache. It costs nothing and changes everything.

If you run a business that still takes a lot of cash, set a daily routine: at the close of business each day, count the cash, record the day's sales, and deposit the cash into the business account the next morning. Do not carry days of cash around. Cash in a drawer is cash that can be spent without a record.

## What You Need to Start

You need three things, and you probably already have all of them. First, a recording tool. A hardcover notebook works perfectly. So does a free Google Sheet on your phone. Pick whichever you will actually use every day. Many business owners use both: a notebook at the shop for quick entries, and a Google Sheet updated each evening.

Second, a place to keep receipts and evidence. A simple envelope or a plastic file folder is enough. Every receipt, invoice, transfer confirmation, and POS slip goes in the folder, sorted by month. When you record an expense, note whether you have the receipt. If you bought something without a receipt — market purchases, transport, small cash items — write the details in your book immediately.

Third, twenty minutes a day. Bookkeeping fails when it becomes a weekend job or a month-end job. By then, you have forgotten half the transactions. Do it daily, ideally at the close of business when the day is still fresh. Twenty minutes a day is more than enough for most small businesses.

## The Five Books Every Small Business Needs

You do not need a full accounting system. Five simple records cover almost everything a Nigerian small business needs.

The first is the sales record. Every sale, every day, with the date, what was sold, the amount, and how it was paid — cash, transfer, or POS. If you sell on WhatsApp, your chat history is not a sales record. Transfer the details into your book or sheet at the end of the day. Record the full amount, not just the profit. If you sold a dress for ₦25,000, record ₦25,000, even though the dress cost you ₦15,000. The cost is recorded separately as an expense.

The second is the expense record. Every naira the business spends, with the date, what it was for, the category, and the amount. Use simple categories that fit your business: stock, transport, rent, salaries, data and airtime, advertising, fuel and power, packaging, repairs, and miscellaneous. At the end of the month, add up each category. You might discover that transport is your second biggest cost, or that advertising is eating more than it returns.

The third is the cash record. This tracks physical cash: how much cash came in, how much went out, and how much should be in the box or drawer right now. Count the cash at the end of each day and compare it with what your records say should be there. If the numbers do not match, find out why that same day, while the trail is still warm.

The fourth is the customer credit record, also called a debtors book. If you sell on credit — and many Nigerian businesses do, especially to regular customers — you must record who owes you, how much, and when they promised to pay. A simple page per customer, or a sheet with columns for customer name, date, amount owed, amount paid, and balance, will save you from endless arguments and forgotten debts. Review this book every week and follow up on overdue amounts politely but firmly. Unpaid credit is one of the quietest killers of small businesses.

The fifth is the supplier credit record, or creditors book. If a supplier gives you goods on credit, record what you owe, to whom, and when it is due. This keeps your relationships clean and stops a supplier from cutting you off because of a forgotten balance. It also helps you plan cash flow, because you know which payments are coming due in the next two weeks.

## Setting Up Your Google Sheet in Thirty Minutes

If you choose the digital route, here is a simple setup that takes about half an hour and costs nothing. Create a new Google Sheet and name it with your business name and the year, for example "Joetech Books 2026". Create five tabs: Sales, Expenses, Cash, Customer Credit, Supplier Credit.

The Sales tab needs columns for date, description or item, customer name, amount, payment method, and a notes column. At the bottom of the amount column, use the SUM formula to total the month. If you are not comfortable with formulas, ask a friend or search for a short tutorial on Google Sheets SUM. It is one formula, and once it is set up, it works forever.

The Expenses tab needs columns for date, description, category, amount, receipt (yes or no), and payment method. Use the SUM formula at the bottom too. At the end of the month, you can filter or sort by category to see how much each category cost you. This is where the insight lives.

The Cash tab is a simple running balance. Columns for date, description, cash in, cash out, and balance. Each day starts with the previous balance, adds the cash received, subtracts the cash spent, and gives the new balance. Count the physical cash and compare. Any difference is flagged and investigated the same day.

The Customer Credit tab has columns for customer name, date of sale, item, amount owed, amount paid, balance, and promised payment date. The Supplier Credit tab mirrors it: supplier name, date, goods received, amount owed, amount paid, balance, and due date.

Once the sheet is set up, protect it with a strong password on your Google account and turn on two-step verification. Your business records are sensitive. Do not share the sheet with staff unless they need it.

## Your Daily Routine: Twenty Minutes at Close of Business

A routine that works every day beats a perfect system used once a month. Here is a daily routine that takes about twenty minutes.

First, record all sales for the day. Go through your POS slips, transfer alerts, WhatsApp orders, and cash received. Enter each sale in the sales record. If you had fifty small sales in a provision shop, you do not need fifty lines. Record the total cash sales for the day as one line and the total transfer sales as another line, with a note of the number of transactions. The detail matters less than the daily total being correct.

Second, record all expenses for the day. Go through the receipts in your folder and the cash you spent. Enter each one with its category. Small cash expenses without receipts — transport, market purchases — get recorded from memory immediately, while the day is fresh. If you wait until tomorrow, you will forget.

Third, count the cash. Count what is in the till or cash box. Compare with what your cash record says should be there. If there is a difference, look for the cause now. A missing ₦2,000 is usually a forgotten expense or a sale recorded wrongly. Find it while you still can.

Fourth, file the receipts. Put today's receipts in the folder for the month. Throw away nothing until the month is closed and reviewed. A receipt you think is useless today might be the proof you need during a tax check next year.

## Your Weekly and Monthly Routine

Once a week, spend thirty minutes reviewing the week's numbers. Add up the week's sales and expenses. Check the customer credit book for anyone whose payment is due. Check the supplier credit book for anything due in the coming week. Look at the cash balance and decide whether you need to move money to the bank or take a top-up for petty cash. This weekly review is what turns daily recording into actual management. Without it, you are collecting numbers you never use.

Once a month, do a proper month-end close. This takes about an hour. Add up the month's total sales and total expenses. Subtract expenses from sales to get your gross profit before owner drawings and loan repayments. Compare this month with last month. Are sales rising or falling? Which expense category grew? Did any customer fail to pay? Write a short summary — five lines is enough — noting what went well and what needs attention next month.

Also at month end, set aside money for tax and for your own pay. A practical approach used by many Nigerian small business owners is to move a fixed percentage of monthly profit into a separate savings account for tax, and to pay yourself a fixed monthly salary by transfer.

## Common Bookkeeping Mistakes to Avoid

The most common mistake is recording sales but not expenses. Many owners track what came in and ignore what went out, then wonder why the account is empty. Expenses are half the picture. Record both, every day.

The second mistake is mixing personal and business money, which we covered above. It is worth repeating because it is the mistake that ruins more small business records than any other. One account for business, one for personal, and a recorded transfer between them when money moves.

The third mistake is recording from memory at the end of the week or month. Memory fades fast. A ₦3,500 market purchase on Tuesday is forgotten by Saturday. Record daily or accept that your books will be wrong.

The fourth mistake is not recording credit sales. If you give goods on credit and do not write it down, the customer may genuinely forget, and you have no record to remind them. Every credit sale is a loan from your business to your customer. Treat it with the same seriousness.

The fifth mistake is keeping records in your head or in scattered WhatsApp chats. Your phone's chat history is not a bookkeeping system. It cannot total your sales, it cannot show your expense categories, and it cannot be handed to an accountant or a bank. Move the numbers into a book or sheet every day.

The sixth mistake is never reviewing the numbers. Recording without reviewing is like taking your temperature and never looking at the thermometer. The weekly and monthly review is where bookkeeping pays off.

## When to Get an Accountant

You can do your own bookkeeping for a long time, and most small businesses should. But there are moments when professional help pays for itself. If your business is registered with the Corporate Affairs Commission and you need to file annual returns, an accountant can prepare them correctly. If your turnover grows past a few million naira a year, an accountant can advise on tax planning and on whether you should register for VAT. If you are applying for a significant loan, an accountant can prepare the financial statements a bank wants to see. And if you are ever audited or receive a tax query, an accountant speaks the language of the tax office.

Even when you hire an accountant, keep doing your daily recording yourself. The accountant works from your records. Clean daily records make their job cheaper and faster, and they make your business look professional. An accountant cannot fix a year of missing records without charging you for the reconstruction.

A practical middle ground is to do your own daily and monthly books and hire an accountant once a quarter or once a year to review them and prepare your tax filings. Many small accounting firms in Lagos and Abuja offer this kind of arrangement at a reasonable fee.

## Start Today, Not Next Month

Bookkeeping is one of those things that feels hard until you have done it for two weeks, and then it becomes a habit. The hardest part is the first day. So start today. Get a notebook or open a Google Sheet. Record today's sales and today's expenses. Count the cash tonight. Do the same tomorrow. After a week, you will have seven days of real numbers. After a month, you will know your profit. After six months, you will wonder how you ever ran a business without this.
