A graphic designer in Lagos finishes a logo for a client. The client loves it, uses it on his shop signboard and his Instagram page, and then stops replying to messages. Weeks pass. The designer has paid for data, spent hours on the work, and turned down other jobs to meet the deadline. Yet she has not been paid. This story is common in Nigeria. Many freelancers, tailors, builders, caterers, and small business owners do good work and then wait months for payment, or never get paid at all.

Late payment is not just annoying. It stops you from buying stock, paying rent, and taking new jobs. It causes stress and arguments. The good news is that most late payment can be prevented. It starts before the job begins, with clear terms, a deposit, and a written agreement. This guide shows you how to get paid on time, and what to do when a client is late.

## Why Clients Pay Late in Nigeria

Understanding why clients delay payment helps you prevent it. The first reason is simple: there was no clear agreement. The client did not know when payment was due, how much to pay, or what would happen if they paid late. When terms are vague, payment becomes vague too.

The second reason is cash flow. The client may be waiting for their own customers to pay them. A company that owes you money may itself be owed money. That does not make it right, but it explains why chasing politely and early works better than waiting silently.

The third reason is that some clients test new suppliers. They pay late to see if you will accept it. If you accept late payment without complaint the first time, they will do it again. If you follow up firmly and professionally, they learn that you expect payment on time.

The fourth reason is poor systems. In many Nigerian companies, payment needs approval from several people. Your invoice may sit on a desk while the manager is travelling or the accountant is busy. A polite reminder can move it from the bottom of the pile to the top.

The final reason is dishonesty. A small number of clients never intend to pay. They take the work and disappear. You cannot prevent every case, but you can reduce the risk by asking for a deposit, delivering work in stages, and keeping proof of everything.

## Step 1: Agree on Payment Terms Before You Start Work

Never start work without agreeing on payment terms in writing. A WhatsApp message is enough, as long as it is clear and the client confirms it. Before you begin, agree on these five things and send them to the client to confirm.

First, the total price. State the exact amount, not a range. "The total cost is 150,000 naira" is clear. "It will be around 100,000 to 150,000 naira" is not.

Second, the deposit. Ask for a deposit before you start. For most small jobs, 50 percent upfront is fair. For bigger jobs, 60 percent upfront is reasonable. A client who will not pay a deposit is a warning sign. If they cannot pay half now, how will they pay the full amount later?

Third, the payment schedule. State when the rest is due. "50 percent now, 50 percent on delivery" is simple and clear. For a long project, use stages: "30 percent to start, 40 percent after the first draft, 30 percent on completion." The client should know every payment date before work begins.

Fourth, the payment method. State your bank name, account number, and account name clearly. Offer transfer as the main method, and confirm whether you accept cash. Avoid confusion by giving one main account, not three different ones.

Fifth, the deadline for payment after delivery. For example: "Balance is due within 3 days of delivery." A clear deadline gives you a fair reason to follow up on day four.

Send these terms as a short message or a simple invoice before you start. Ask the client to reply, "I agree." That reply is your proof. Many disputes in Nigeria happen because terms were agreed by voice call and later forgotten. Put it in writing.

## Step 2: Always Ask for a Deposit

A deposit protects you in three ways. It shows the client is serious. It gives you money to buy materials and cover your costs while you work. And it reduces your loss if the client disappears, because you have already been paid for part of the work.

Many Nigerian business owners feel shy about asking for a deposit. They worry the client will go to a competitor who does not ask for one. In reality, serious clients expect to pay a deposit. It is normal in tailoring, catering, construction, design, and event planning. A client who refuses a deposit is often the client who will not pay the balance either.

Explain the deposit in a friendly, professional way. "To start your order, I need a 50 percent deposit of 75,000 naira. This covers the materials and secures your date. The balance of 75,000 naira is due on delivery." That is clear, polite, and fair.

Do not start work until the deposit arrives. If you start without it, you lose your bargaining power. The client has no reason to pay quickly, because the work is already being done. Wait for the deposit, even if it takes a day or two. A client who is serious will pay. A client who keeps promising "tomorrow" for a week is telling you something.

For regular, trusted customers, you can be more flexible. A customer who has paid you on time five times in a row has earned some trust. You can reduce the deposit or allow payment on delivery for small orders. But keep the deposit rule for new clients, large orders, and custom work that you cannot resell if the client changes their mind.

## Step 3: Send a Professional Invoice Every Time

An invoice is not just a piece of paper. It is a professional reminder that payment is due, and it gives the client's accountant something to process. Many Nigerian businesses lose money because they never send a proper invoice. They send a WhatsApp message saying "please pay," and the client forgets.

A good invoice includes your business name, your phone number, and your address. It includes the client's name, the date, and an invoice number. It lists each item or service, the quantity, the price, and the total. It shows the deposit already paid, the balance due, and the date it is due. It includes your bank details clearly.

You can create a simple invoice in a notebook, in Microsoft Word, or with a free invoice tool. Send the invoice as soon as the work is delivered, not a week later. The sooner you send it, the sooner the client can process it.

Send the invoice on WhatsApp and, if the client is a company, by email as well. Companies often need an email invoice for their records. Keep a copy for yourself, and note the date you sent it. If you need to follow up later, you can say, "I sent invoice number 12 on Monday, and the balance of 75,000 naira is due today." That is professional and hard to argue with.

## Step 4: Deliver Work in Stages for Big Jobs

For big jobs, do not deliver everything before you are paid in full. Deliver in stages, and tie each stage to a payment. This protects you and builds trust with the client, because they see progress before they pay the next stage.

A website designer can show a homepage design after the first payment, then build the full site after the second payment, then hand over full control after the final payment. A tailor can show the fabric and the first fitting after the deposit, then finish the outfit after the second payment. A builder can complete the foundation, then the walls, then the roof, with a payment at each stage.

Use watermarks or previews where possible. A designer can send a low-resolution preview with a watermark. A writer can send the first half of a document. A photographer can send small preview images. The client can see the quality of the work, but cannot use the final product until they pay. This is fair and professional.

Agree on these stages before you start, in writing. "Stage one: design draft, 30 percent. Stage two: full website, 40 percent. Stage three: final handover, 30 percent." When each stage is done, send the work and the invoice for that stage together. The client pays, and you move to the next stage.

## Step 5: Follow Up Politely, Early, and Regularly

Following up is not begging. It is part of running a professional business. Many clients pay late simply because they forgot, or because your invoice is buried under other work. A polite reminder helps them, and it helps you.

Follow up the day before payment is due. Send a friendly message: "Good morning, Mr. Ade. I hope you are well. This is a quick reminder that the balance of 75,000 naira for your logo is due tomorrow. My account details are on the invoice. Thank you." This is polite, professional, and gives the client time to arrange payment.

If payment is one day late, follow up again. Keep it short and friendly. "Good afternoon. I am checking on the balance for invoice 12. Please let me know when I can expect payment. Thank you." Do not wait a month before you follow up. The longer you wait, the harder it becomes to collect.

If payment is a week late, be firmer. Call the client, not just a message. A phone call is harder to ignore than a text. Stay calm and professional. "Good afternoon, I am calling about the balance of 75,000 naira, which was due last week. Can you tell me when it will be paid? I need to plan my expenses." Ask for a specific date, not a vague promise. "I will pay soon" is not a date. "I will pay on Friday" is a date. Write down the date they promise, and follow up on that day if payment does not come.

Keep a record of every follow-up: the date, what you said, and what the client promised. If the matter ever goes further, this record is your evidence. It also helps you stay organised when you have many clients.

## Step 6: Handle Difficult Clients Without Losing Your Temper

Some clients will test your patience. They will promise payment and disappear. They will complain about the work to avoid paying. They will ask for extra work for free. Stay professional, even when you are frustrated.

Do not deliver more work until the old balance is paid. If a client owes you for the last job and asks for a new job, say politely: "I would love to help with the new job. Let us first clear the balance of 75,000 naira from the last job, and then we can start the new one." This is fair, and it protects you from building up a bigger debt.

Do not reduce your price because a client is delaying payment. A discount for late payment rewards bad behaviour. If you want to offer a discount, offer it for early payment instead. "If you pay within 3 days, I will give you a 5 percent discount on your next order." That encourages prompt payment.

If a client complains about the quality to avoid payment, ask for specifics in writing. "Please tell me exactly what needs to be changed, and I will fix it." If the complaint is genuine, fix it quickly. If the client cannot name a specific problem, they are likely using the complaint as an excuse. Keep your messages polite and factual.

Know when to stop. If a client owes you a small amount and has ignored many messages and calls, it may be better to stop chasing and focus on new clients. Your time is valuable. Chasing 20,000 naira for three months can cost you more in lost work than the debt itself. Learn from it, tighten your deposit rule, and move on.

For larger amounts, consider a formal letter. A short letter on your business letterhead, stating the amount owed, the date it was due, and a deadline for payment, often works when messages do not. You can also ask a lawyer to write a demand letter for a serious debt. Many clients pay quickly when they receive a formal letter, because they realise you are serious.

## Step 7: Protect Yourself With Records and Proof

Good records are your best protection against non-payment. Keep proof of everything, from the first message to the final delivery.

Save all WhatsApp chats with clients. Do not delete them. Save the agreement, the deposit receipt, the invoice, and the proof of delivery. If you deliver a product, take a photo of the client receiving it, or get a signed delivery note. If you deliver a digital service, save the email or WhatsApp message where the client confirmed they received it and liked it.

Use a simple tracking sheet. For each client, write the name, the job, the total price, the deposit paid, the balance due, the due date, and the date you followed up. Update it every week. This sheet will show you at a glance who owes you money and how much. It also helps you plan your cash flow.

Do not mix business money and personal money. Use a separate bank account for your business, or at least keep clear records of what is business income. When a client pays, note it immediately. When you spend business money, note it too. Clear records make it easier to know whether you are making a profit, and easier to prove what a client owes you.

## A Real Example: A Caterer in Abuja Changes Her System

A caterer in Abuja cooks for weddings and corporate events. For years, she took bookings with a small deposit, or sometimes with no deposit at all, because she trusted her clients. Many clients paid late, and some never paid the balance. She often borrowed money to buy food for the next event.

She changed her system. Now she asks for 60 percent before the event, and the balance on the day of the event, before serving starts. She sends a written agreement on WhatsApp with the price, the menu, the number of guests, and the payment dates. She sends an invoice as soon as the client confirms. On the day of the event, she collects the balance before the food is served.

At first, she lost one or two clients who wanted to pay after the event. But her business became healthier. She no longer borrowed money to buy food. She paid her staff on time. Her regular clients respected the new system, because it was clear and professional. She still offers a small discount for clients who pay in full early, and that encourages prompt payment.

## Your Payment Checklist

Use this checklist before you start any new job.

You have agreed on the total price, the deposit, the payment dates, and the payment method in writing, and the client has confirmed it. You have received the deposit before starting work. You have sent a professional invoice with your bank details. You have delivered work in stages for big jobs. You have a follow-up plan: a reminder before the due date, a message on the due date, and a call if payment is late. You have kept records of every agreement, invoice, and delivery.

Getting paid on time is not about being harsh. It is about being clear, professional, and consistent. Clients respect a business that respects itself. Set your terms before you start, ask for a deposit, send an invoice, follow up politely, and keep good records. Your work deserves to be paid for, on time, every time. Start with your next client, and make on-time payment part of how you do business.
