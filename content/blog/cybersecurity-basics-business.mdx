Mrs Funke Adeyemi runs a fabric shop on Admiralty Way in Lekki. One Saturday afternoon, a customer bought ₦180,000 worth of lace, tapped his card on her POS machine, and the terminal printed a receipt that said approved. Funke wrapped the fabric, the man left smiling, and at the end of the day her account showed nothing. The receipt was real. The payment never was.

Funke is not a careless person. She is careful with her money. She just never had anyone explain, in plain language, what could go wrong and what to do about it. This article is that explanation, written for an owner who does not want to become an IT expert, just wants to stop losing money to tricks that are now everyday life in Nigerian business.

You do not need to fix everything in one day. You need one week, one small job each day, and rules your staff can remember without a training manual.

## Monday: Fix Your Passwords, Starting With Three Accounts

Most business owners in Nigeria use one password everywhere. It is usually something like a child's name plus a year of birth, and it opens the email, the Instagram page, the bank app and the POS dashboard. That is the single most dangerous habit in your business, and it costs nothing to fix.

Think of it like this. If one key opened your shop, your house, your car and your safe, losing that key would end you. A reused password is that key. When a website you signed up for years ago leaks its passwords — and this happens constantly — the first thing criminals do is try that same password on your email, your WhatsApp and your bank.

So on Monday, do three things. First, pick the three accounts that would hurt most if someone else got into them: your main email, your business WhatsApp, and your bank or payment app. Change each password to something long and different. A good password is not clever, it is long. Four random words you can remember work better than a short jumble of symbols.

Second, turn on the extra lock wherever it exists. On WhatsApp, open Settings, go to Account, then Two-step verification, and set a six-digit PIN. That PIN is what stops the most common attack on Nigerian businesses: someone registering your number on another phone and taking over your WhatsApp. It takes two minutes. On your email and your bank app, turn on two-factor authentication, the setting that asks for a code from your phone as well as your password.

Third, stop keeping passwords in your head and in a notebook by the till. Download a free password manager — Bitwarden is free and solid — and let it remember the rest. You remember one strong master password. It remembers everything else, and it will warn you when you reuse one.

A pharmacy owner in Ikeja named Chidi did this on a Monday morning and found he had been using the same password on eleven accounts, including his supplier portal. He changed them all before lunch. That is the scale of the problem, and the scale of the fix.

## Tuesday: Update Everything, Then Stop Ignoring the Pop-Ups

That little window that appears on your phone or laptop saying "Update available" is not an advert. Most of the time it is a repair for a hole that criminals already know about. When you tap "remind me later" for three months, you are leaving your front door open and telling yourself you will lock it eventually.

Tuesday's job is simple. Update your phone. Update your laptop. Update the POS app, the WhatsApp Business app and your bank app. Turn on automatic updates so you never have to think about it again. While you are at it, delete apps you do not recognise. A "free" app that promises cheap data or free DSTV is one of the commonest ways phones in Lagos pick up spyware. If you did not download it from the Play Store or App Store, and you cannot remember why it is there, remove it.

One more thing on Tuesday: check who is logged into your accounts. WhatsApp has a "linked devices" screen that shows every phone and computer connected to your number. Gmail has a security page that shows every device signed in. If you see a device you do not recognise, sign it out immediately and change that password.

## Wednesday: Back Up the Things That Would Close You Down

Ask yourself a hard question. If your phone was stolen on the way home tonight, what would you lose? For most small businesses the answer is terrifying: every customer contact, every order chat, every photo of your products, your price lists, your supplier numbers. Your whole business lives on one device that can be snatched on a bus in Oshodi.

Wednesday is backup day, and it costs nothing. Turn on WhatsApp chat backup to Google Drive or iCloud, and set it to run every night while you sleep. Turn on automatic photo backup so your product photos survive a stolen phone. If you keep customer records or orders in a spreadsheet, make sure that file lives in Google Drive or OneDrive, not only on the phone.

Then buy one external hard drive or a large flash drive — a 1TB drive costs around ₦45,000 to ₦60,000 in Computer Village — and once a month copy your most important files onto it and keep it somewhere that is not your shop. A fire, a flood or a burglary should not be able to take both copies at once.

Test the backup too. Backups you have never tried to restore are a promise, not a protection. Once, open the backup and check that a photo and a chat actually come back. Five minutes now beats discovering the backup was empty when you need it most.

## Thursday: Write Down the Rules Your Staff Must Follow

Most fraud inside a small business does not come from a hacker in another country. It comes from a rushed staff member, a confused new hire, or a rule nobody ever said out loud. Thursday is the day you write the rules down — on one sheet of paper, stuck on the wall by the till, in words a new employee can understand on their first day.

The first rule, and the one that matters most: nobody releases goods or starts work because a customer showed a screenshot. The money must be visible in your own bank app, on your own phone, before anything leaves the shop. Fake bank alerts are now so good that even careful people get fooled. A customer in a hurry, waving a phone with an alert that looks exactly like a GTBank message, is the oldest trick in Lagos markets. The rule protects your staff as much as it protects you, because it means they never have to make a judgement call under pressure. They just say, "Let me confirm in our account, one minute please." Honest customers wait. The ones who shout and rush you are the ones the rule exists for.

The second rule: only one or two named people check payments and approve dispatch. When everybody can approve, nobody is responsible, and a scammer only needs to find the most junior person on their first week. Name the people. Write their names on the sheet.

The third rule: staff never use their personal accounts for business money, never share the business phone's PIN or the POS password, and never click a link a "customer" sends asking them to confirm a delivery or update the POS app. Links in messages are how phones get taken over. If a message claims to be from your bank or your POS provider and asks you to tap a link, close it and call the number on the back of your card instead.

The fourth rule: the Wi-Fi password for the shop is not the same as the password for the POS machine or the business email, and the Wi-Fi password changes when a staff member leaves.

Sit your team down for fifteen minutes and walk through the sheet. Tell them the story of a real loss — Funke's ₦180,000, or one of your own — because stories stick where instructions slide off.

## Friday: Protect Your WhatsApp Like It Is Your Shop

For many Nigerian businesses, WhatsApp is the shop. It holds your customers, your orders, your reputation. And it is the account criminals want most, because once they have it they can message all your customers asking for money, and your customers will believe it is you.

You already set the two-step verification PIN on Monday. Friday is for the rest. Check your linked devices again and remove anything unfamiliar. Turn on the fingerprint or face lock on WhatsApp itself, so a stolen unlocked phone does not hand over your chats. Never share the six-digit code WhatsApp texts you — not with a customer, not with a "support agent" who calls you, not with anyone. WhatsApp will never call you to ask for it. Anyone asking for it is stealing your account, full stop.

Be suspicious of the message that says your sister, your pastor or your best customer needs ₦50,000 urgently and will pay you back tomorrow. When a contact you know suddenly starts talking about money in a strange way, call them on a normal phone call before you send anything.

The same caution applies to POS fraud at the counter. Watch the card, not the customer. If a card payment fails twice, do not keep trying it — ask for a transfer or another card. If a POS transaction is approved but no alert arrives on your phone and the money is not in your app, treat it as unpaid until you have checked with your provider. A receipt printed by a machine is not the same as money in your account.

## When Something Goes Wrong Anyway

Even with all of this, something may still go wrong. What you do in the first hour matters more than what you did before.

If your WhatsApp is taken over, message your customers from another number or your Instagram page immediately: "My WhatsApp was hacked, ignore any money requests from me." Then email WhatsApp support and start the recovery from a fresh install. If money left your account, call your bank at once and ask them to flag the transaction, then report to the police cybercrime unit with screenshots and the transaction details. Speed is everything. A report made the same day has a far better chance than one made a week later.

Then, once the dust settles, write down what happened and how it happened. Not to blame anyone, but so the same trick never works twice.

You do not need to be technical to be safe. You need a week of small jobs, a sheet of rules on the wall, and the habit of checking your own account before you believe anyone else's screen. Funke still runs her fabric shop on Admiralty Way. She changed her passwords, locked her WhatsApp with a PIN, wrote the rules down for her two staff, and she has not lost a kobo to fraud since. The tricks did not stop coming. They just stopped working.
