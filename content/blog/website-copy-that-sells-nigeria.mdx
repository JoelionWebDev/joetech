Open ten Nigerian business websites and you will see the same words again and again: "Welcome to our website. We are committed to excellence. We offer quality services." None of it is wrong, and none of it helps a visitor decide. The visitor already knows you think you are excellent. What they want to know is what you do, whether it is for them, and what to do next.

Website copy that sells is not clever copy. It is clear copy. It speaks the customer's language, answers their questions in order, and asks for one clear action. This guide shows how to write it, section by section, using examples any Nigerian service business can adapt.

## Start with the customer, not the company

The most common copy mistake is writing from the company's point of view. "We are a leading provider of innovative solutions" tells the visitor about you. "Affordable event decoration in Lekki, with setup and teardown included" tells the visitor about themselves and their problem. Guess which one keeps them reading.

A simple test helps. Read each sentence on your homepage and ask: is this about us, or about the customer? If most sentences start with "we", rewrite at least half of them to start with what the customer gets. Instead of "We offer a wide range of services", write "Choose from bridal styling, makeup, and gele tying, all in one studio visit." The second version lets the visitor picture themselves using the service.

This does not mean hiding who you are. Your story, your experience, and your values belong on the site, but they belong after the visitor understands what you sell. Lead with the customer's need, then earn their trust with your story.

## Write headlines that answer three questions

Your headline is the hardest-working sentence on your website. A visitor gives it a few seconds, and in that time it should answer three questions: What is this? Is it for me? Why should I care?

A useful headline formula is: what you do, plus who you serve, plus a benefit or a location. For example:

- "Home cleaning in Abuja, booked in minutes, done the same week"
- "Custom cakes in Surulere for birthdays, weddings, and corporate events"
- "Solar installation for homes and small businesses in Lagos"

Notice that none of these tries to be clever. They are specific, and specificity is persuasive. A vague headline forces the visitor to work out whether you are relevant. A specific headline does that work for them. Under the headline, add one short sentence that explains the main benefit or removes the main worry, such as "No hidden charges, and you approve the design before we print." That supporting line is often what turns a skimming visitor into a reading one.

If your business serves different kinds of customers, do not try to speak to all of them in one headline. Pick the customer you most want, and let your service pages speak to the others. A homepage that tries to say everything usually says nothing clearly. ## Describe services by outcome, not by process

Service descriptions are where copy usually becomes a wall of jargon. Businesses describe their methods, their equipment, and their internal steps, while customers are looking for outcomes. A customer does not buy a process. They buy a result.

Compare two descriptions for the same service. The process version says: "Our team utilises advanced techniques and state-of-the-art equipment to deliver comprehensive cleaning solutions." The outcome version says: "Deep cleaning for your home or office in Lagos, including kitchens, bathrooms, and floors, so the space feels fresh for guests or staff." The second version helps the customer imagine the result and decide whether it matches their need.

To write outcome-focused copy, answer three questions for each service: Who is it for? What exactly do they get? What changes for them afterwards? Keep each description to two or three short sentences. If a service needs more explanation, give it its own page and link to it, rather than stretching the homepage into an essay.

## Handle objections before the visitor leaves

Every customer has worries, and most will never tell you what they are. They simply leave. Good copy answers those worries in advance. Think about the questions customers ask you on WhatsApp or on the phone, and put the answers on the website.

Common worries for Nigerian customers include:

- How much will this cost, and are there hidden charges?
- How long will it take, and do I need to pay everything upfront?
- What happens if I am not happy with the result?
- Are you a real business, and where are you located?
- How do I reach you if something goes wrong?

You do not need to answer every worry on the homepage. A short line about your location and a visible phone number answers the trust question. A simple three-step process answers the "what happens next" question. A clear statement about deposits and revisions answers the money question. Each worry you answer is one less reason for a visitor to leave and check your competitor.

## End every section with one clear action

Copy that sells does not just inform. It guides. Every important section should end with, or point towards, one clear action: chat on WhatsApp, call, request a quote, or fill in a short form. The action should match what the visitor is ready to do at that point. A visitor who has just read your services is ready to ask a question, not necessarily to pay. "Chat with us on WhatsApp" fits that moment better than "Buy now".

Avoid stacking actions. If a section ends with five buttons — call, chat, subscribe, download, follow — the visitor often chooses none of them. Pick the action that leads most directly to a conversation, repeat it in a few places on the page, and use direct words. "Request a quote" is clear. "Get started" is vague. "See our services" is not an action at all.

## A quick copy editing checklist

Before you publish any page, read it aloud and check it against this list:

- Can a stranger understand what you sell within five seconds?
- Does the copy talk more about the customer than about the company?
- Is every service described by what the customer gets, not by your process?
- Are the main worries — price, timing, trust — answered somewhere on the page?
- Is there one clear next step, written in direct words?
- Have you removed jargon, empty phrases, and sentences that say nothing?

Reading aloud matters. Copy that sounds stiff or confusing when spoken will read the same way on screen. If you stumble over a sentence, your visitors will too.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How long should homepage copy be?**

As long as it takes to answer the visitor's questions, and no longer. Most service business homepages do well with a headline, a short supporting sentence, brief service descriptions, a simple process, and a call to action. Detail belongs on service pages, not crammed onto the homepage.

**Should I write the copy myself or hire someone?**

You can write it yourself if you follow the rules in this guide, because nobody knows your customers' questions better than you do. What helps is an outside eye: ask a friend or colleague to read it and tell you, honestly, whether they understand what you sell and what to do next.

**Is it wrong to mention my experience and awards?**

No, but put them in their place. Experience and proof build trust, and they work best after the visitor understands your offer. A short trust section near the top, or a fuller story on your about page, is more persuasive than leading with your own history.

**What is the biggest copy mistake Nigerian businesses make?**

Writing for themselves instead of their customers. Phrases like "excellence", "quality", and "solutions" feel safe, but they do not help a visitor decide. Specific words about what you do, who it is for, and what happens next will always outperform impressive-sounding generalities.

## Write for the customer in front of you

Good website copy is not about sounding impressive. It is about being understood. When a visitor lands on your homepage and immediately knows what you do, who it is for, and what to do next, you have done the hardest part of selling online. The design, the photos, and the features all support that understanding, but they cannot replace it.
