Every Nigerian online seller knows the message: "Please, my package has not arrived." Sometimes it is followed by "I have been waiting since Monday." Sometimes it is followed by anger. Sometimes the customer has already gone to social media to complain.

Delivery failures and returns are not a sign that your business is failing. They are a normal part of selling online in Nigeria, where addresses are hard to find, traffic is unpredictable, couriers are overstretched, and customers sometimes change their minds. The businesses that succeed are not the ones that never have a failed delivery. They are the ones that handle failures well — quickly, honestly, and in a way that leaves the customer willing to buy again.

## Why Deliveries Fail in Nigeria

Before you can fix delivery failures, you need to understand why they happen. Most failures fall into six categories.

**Wrong or incomplete addresses.** This is the biggest cause. A customer writes "No. 12, Allen Avenue, Ikeja" with no landmark, no house description, and a phone number that does not connect. The rider gets to Allen Avenue and cannot find the house. Or the customer gives a work address on a Saturday when the office is closed. Or the address is in a new estate that does not appear on maps yet. Nigerian addresses are often descriptive — "after the second junction, the yellow house by the church" — and a courier who does not know the area will struggle.

**Customer unavailable.** The rider arrives and the customer does not pick up the phone, is not at home, or asks the rider to wait for an hour. Riders have many deliveries to make and cannot wait. After two or three failed attempts, the package comes back to you.

**Courier problems.** The courier loses the package, delays it, damages it, or delivers it to the wrong person. Sometimes a package sits in a dispatch hub for days because it was not sorted. Sometimes a rider takes a package and disappears. These are courier failures, but the customer blames you, because you chose the courier.

**Payment problems.** For pay-on-delivery orders, the customer may not have the money when the rider arrives, or may refuse to pay because they found the item cheaper elsewhere. For prepaid orders, a failed transfer or a reversed payment can hold up dispatch.

**Weather and traffic.** Heavy rain floods roads in Lagos. A protest blocks a major road in Abuja. Fuel scarcity slows everything down. These are outside your control, but they still delay deliveries, and customers still expect to be told.

When you know which of these is causing most of your failures, you can fix the right thing. A business whose main problem is wrong addresses needs better address collection, not a new courier. A business whose main problem is courier delays needs a new courier, not a new returns policy.

## Step 1: Collect the Right Delivery Information Before You Dispatch

Most delivery failures are decided before the package leaves your shop. The information you collect at order time determines whether the delivery succeeds.

Always collect these five things: the customer's full name, a working phone number (and a second number if possible), the full delivery address including house number, street, area, and state, a landmark near the address, and any delivery instruction such as "call when you arrive" or "leave with the security guard."

Do not accept vague addresses. If a customer writes "Lekki" as their address, ask for the street, the estate, and the house number. If they write an address without a landmark, ask for one. This takes one extra message, and it saves a failed delivery that costs you far more.

Confirm the address before dispatch. Send the customer a message: "Please confirm your delivery address: [address]. Is this correct?" Customers often spot their own mistake when they see the address written back to them. This confirmation also protects you if the customer later claims the item went to the wrong address.

## Step 2: Pack Properly — It Prevents Returns

A large share of returns starts with bad packaging. An item that arrives broken, dirty, or crushed will be sent back, and the customer will be angry. Good packaging is cheaper than a return.

Use packaging that fits the item. A small item in a large box will move around and break. A fragile item wrapped in a single layer of nylon will not survive a bike ride over Lagos roads. Use bubble wrap, paper, or foam for fragile items. Seal boxes with tape, not with staples or hope. If you sell food, use containers that seal properly and will not leak. If you sell clothing, use a waterproof bag inside the outer packaging, because rain is a real risk.

Label the package clearly: the customer's name, phone number, and address, and your business name and phone number as the sender. Add "Fragile" where it applies. Include a packing slip or a small card inside the package with the item name, the quantity, and your contact details. If there is a problem, the customer knows exactly who to contact, and the courier knows exactly who the package belongs to.

## Step 3: Choose and Manage Your Delivery Partners

Your delivery partner is your business's reputation in the customer's hands. Choose carefully, and manage the relationship actively.

There are three main options for Nigerian small businesses. **Bike and van dispatch riders** — local riders in your city, often found through Instagram or WhatsApp — are fast and flexible for same-city deliveries, and they know the local area. Their weakness is reliability: a single rider can fall ill, run out of fuel, or simply not show up. **Interstate logistics companies** — such as GIG Logistics, DHL, Kwik, and similar operators — handle deliveries between cities and states, with tracking and more formal processes. They are slower and more expensive, but more reliable for long distances. **Bus park delivery** — sending a package on an interstate bus — is cheap and widely used, especially for deliveries to cities without formal courier coverage. Its weakness is that there is little tracking and little recourse if a package is lost.

## Step 4: Set Honest Delivery Promises

Overpromising on delivery time is one of the most expensive mistakes a Nigerian online business can make. It creates the failures you are trying to avoid.

Set your delivery times as ranges based on what actually happens, not what you hope will happen. If deliveries within Lagos usually take one to two days, say one to two days — not "same day" — unless you genuinely deliver same day almost every time. If interstate deliveries usually take three to five days, say three to five days. Customers prefer an honest three-day promise that is kept over a same-day promise that is broken.

Publish your delivery times and fees clearly on your website and in your WhatsApp catalogue. State the fee for each area: Lagos mainland, Lagos Island, Abuja, Port Harcourt, Ibadan, Onitsha, and anywhere else you serve. If the fee depends on the size or weight of the item, say so. A customer who sees the fee before ordering does not argue about it after ordering.

## Step 5: Communicate at Every Stage

Silence is what turns a small delay into an angry customer. A customer who hears nothing for three days assumes the worst. A customer who receives three short updates will wait patiently for five days.

Send these four messages for every order. **Order confirmation:** immediately after payment — "Thank you, we have received your order for [item]. Your delivery address is [address]. We will dispatch within [time]." **Dispatch notification:** when the package leaves — "Your order has been dispatched with [courier]. Your tracking number is [number]. Expected delivery: [date]." **Delivery day reminder:** on the morning of delivery — "Your package is out for delivery today. Please keep your phone nearby. The rider will call you." **Delivery confirmation:** after delivery — "Your order has been delivered. Thank you for shopping with us. Please let us know if there is any problem."

If there is a delay, tell the customer before they ask. "Hello, your delivery is delayed by one day because of heavy rain. It will arrive tomorrow. We are sorry for the inconvenience." That message takes 30 seconds to send and prevents hours of angry messages. Customers forgive delays. They do not forgive being ignored.

## Step 6: When a Delivery Fails — Act Fast

Even with good systems, deliveries will fail. What matters is what you do in the first few hours.

**First, find out what happened.** Contact the courier immediately and get the facts: where is the package, why was it not delivered, and when can it be redelivered? Do not guess, and do not repeat the courier's excuse to the customer without checking it. A rider who says "the customer was not available" may not have called at all.

**Second, contact the customer.** Do this before the customer contacts you, if you can. Explain what happened honestly, without blaming the customer or the courier in a way that sounds like an excuse. Offer a clear next step: a redelivery time, a new address, or a pickup option. For example: "Hello, we are sorry — the rider could not reach you today. We can redeliver tomorrow between 10 am and 2 pm, or you can pick up from our shop in Lekki. Which works better for you?"

**Fifth, follow up after the redelivery.** Confirm the customer received the item and is happy. That follow-up message is often what turns a frustrated customer into a loyal one. People remember how a problem was handled far longer than they remember the problem itself.

## Step 7: Write a Returns Policy That Is Fair and Affordable

Your returns policy is a promise. It must be fair enough that customers trust you, and affordable enough that it does not destroy your profit. Both matter.

Start with the non-negotiable part: if an item is faulty, damaged in transit, or not as described, the customer gets a replacement or a full refund. This is what the law and basic fairness require, and it is what builds trust. State it clearly on your website and in your order messages.

Then decide the rules for change-of-mind returns. Most Nigerian online businesses allow returns within seven days, provided the item is unused, in its original packaging, with tags attached. Fourteen days is more generous and can be a selling point if your margins allow it. Choose a window you can genuinely honour, and stick to it.

Decide who pays for return delivery. The fair standard is: if the fault is yours — wrong item, damaged item, or item not as described — you pay for the return and for sending the replacement. If the return is a change of mind — wrong size, or the customer simply does not like it — the customer pays for return delivery. Write this down so it is not negotiated fresh with every customer.

Publish the policy where customers can find it: on your website, in your WhatsApp catalogue, and in your order confirmation message. A policy that exists only in your head is not a policy.

## Step 8: Run a Simple Returns Process

A returns process should be easy for the customer and controlled for you. Here is a process that works for most small businesses.

**1. The customer contacts you.** They message you on WhatsApp or email, with their order details and the reason for the return. Reply quickly — within your working hours — and acknowledge the problem. Do not argue about whether the problem is real. Ask for a photo of the item if it is damaged or wrong, so you can see the issue.

**2. You approve or decline the return.** If the item is faulty, damaged, or wrong, approve it. If it is a change of mind within your window and in returnable condition, approve it. If it is outside your window, or the item has been used, or it is a non-returnable item, decline it politely and explain why, referring to the policy the customer agreed to. Offer an alternative where you can — an exchange for a different size, for example, even if a refund is not possible.

**5. You refund, replace, or exchange.** If it is a refund, send it to the original payment method and send the customer proof — a screenshot of the transfer or the payment processor's confirmation. If it is a replacement, dispatch it the same way as a new order, with the same tracking and communication. If it is an exchange, confirm the new size or item before dispatch.

**6. You record it.** Log the return: the order number, the item, the reason, the cost, and the outcome. This record is how you learn whether returns are caused by a particular product, a particular courier, or a particular problem in your packing.

## Step 9: Handle the Hard Cases

Some situations are harder than a simple return. Here is how to handle the most common ones.

**The item arrived damaged.** Apologise, ask for a photo, and offer a replacement or a refund immediately. Do not ask the customer to return the damaged item first if the damage is obvious from the photo — that adds delay and cost for a customer who has already been inconvenienced. Take the matter up with your courier separately, with the photo as evidence.

**The wrong item was sent.** Apologise, send the correct item as fast as possible, and arrange collection of the wrong item at your cost. This was your mistake, so you carry the cost. Check your packing process afterwards to find out how the mistake happened.

**The customer is abusive or threatening.** Stay calm and professional. Restate your policy once, offer the resolution your policy allows, and do not engage further if the customer continues to abuse you. You do not have to accept abuse to keep a customer. Keep records of the conversation in case the matter escalates.

## Step 10: Keep Records and Learn From Every Failure

If you do not record delivery failures and returns, you will keep paying for the same mistakes. Keep a simple log — a notebook or a spreadsheet — with these columns: date, order number, customer, item, courier, problem, cause, cost to you, and outcome.

Review the log once a month. The patterns will be obvious. Perhaps 40% of your failures are wrong addresses from customers in one area — that tells you to improve address collection. Perhaps one courier accounts for half your damaged packages — that tells you to change couriers. Perhaps one product accounts for most returns — that tells you the product description is wrong, or the product quality is poor, or the sizing is confusing.

Use the numbers to make decisions. Calculate your delivery failure rate: failed deliveries divided by total deliveries. Calculate your return rate: returns divided by total orders. Calculate the cost: delivery fees lost, redelivery fees, refund amounts, and replacement costs. For a healthy Nigerian online business, a return rate below 5% is good; above 10% means something is wrong — with your product descriptions, your packing, your courier, or your product quality.

## Your Checklist

- [ ] Order form collects full name, two phone numbers, full address, landmark, and delivery notes
- [ ] Addresses are confirmed with the customer before dispatch
- [ ] Delivery times and fees are published honestly for each area you serve
- [ ] Two delivery partners are available — a main one and a backup
- [ ] Customers receive order, dispatch, delivery-day, and delivery-confirmation messages
- [ ] Delays are communicated to the customer before they ask
- [ ] A written returns policy is published, covering faulty items, change of mind, and non-returnable items
- [ ] A simple returns process exists: contact, approval, return, inspection, refund or replacement
- [ ] Every failure and return is logged with cause and cost

Failed deliveries and returns will never disappear entirely from a Nigerian online business. Roads will flood, riders will get lost, customers will change their minds, and packages will occasionally go missing. What you can control is how ready you are: clear information at order time, honest promises, good packaging, reliable partners, fast communication, and a fair returns process. Get those right, and a failed delivery becomes a story of good service rather than a lost customer. In the end, customers do not judge you by whether a problem happened. They judge you by what you did about it.
