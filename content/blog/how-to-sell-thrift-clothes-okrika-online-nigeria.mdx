Okrika is one of the fastest-moving businesses in Nigeria. Every week, thousands of buyers in Lagos, Abuja, Port Harcourt, and Ibadan search online for quality second-hand clothes: vintage shirts, denim jackets, corporate gowns, children's wear, sneakers, and handbags. They want good quality at a fair price, and they want to buy from a seller they trust.

Selling Okrika online works because the demand is already there. You do not need to convince people that thrift clothes are worth buying. You need to convince them that your own stock is clean, well-graded, fairly priced, and honestly described. A seller who does that well builds repeat customers fast, because Okrika buyers come back every time they need new clothes.

But this business also has real risks. A bad bale can wipe out your capital. Poor photos hide the quality of good stock. Slow replies lose buyers to faster sellers. And a buyer who receives a stained or torn item will warn others about you. This guide explains how to sell thrift clothes online in Nigeria, from sourcing bales to grading, pricing, photographing, and delivering orders that keep customers coming back.

## Why Okrika Sells So Well Online

The biggest reason is price. A quality shirt that costs ₦25,000 new in a boutique might sell for ₦5,000 as Okrika, and a good denim jacket might go for ₦8,000 instead of ₦30,000. For students, young workers, and parents dressing growing children, that difference matters. Many buyers mix Okrika pieces with new clothes and nobody can tell the difference.

The second reason is variety. A single bale can contain dozens of styles, colours, and sizes that no boutique carries. Buyers who want a unique vintage shirt or a particular brand of jeans will search online for Okrika sellers rather than walk into a shop that stocks the same ten designs as everywhere else.

Finally, the business suits online selling because each piece is unique. A buyer who sees a specific jacket in your video knows there is only one of it. That urgency makes people buy quickly instead of thinking about it for a week.

## Sourcing Good Bales and Stock

Your profit is made when you buy, not when you sell. A good bale, bought at the right price from a reliable supplier, gives you pieces you can sell with confidence. A bad bale gives you stained, torn, or outdated clothes that nobody wants, and your money is gone.

Start by learning the market before you spend big. Visit the major Okrika markets: Yaba and Tejuosho in Lagos, and the big bale markets in Onitsha and Aba. Walk around, ask prices, and look at what different grades contain.

Understand grading. First grade bales contain cleaner, more fashionable pieces with fewer faults, and they cost more. Second grade bales are cheaper but contain more pieces that need washing, ironing, or minor repairs. As a beginner, first grade is usually safer, even though it costs more, because you will sell more of the bale and waste less. Avoid suppliers who promise "all first grade, no faults" at a very low price. That promise is rarely true.

Start with one bale in a category you understand. If you know women's gowns well, buy a bale of gowns. If you know sneakers, buy sneakers. Do not buy five different bales in your first month. Learn one category properly: what sells, what sizes move fastest, and what faults to check for.

Inspect before you pay when you can. Open the bale, check a sample of pieces for stains, tears, faded colours, and broken zips. Smell the clothes: heavily musty stock needs serious washing before it can be sold. If a supplier will not let you inspect, buy a small quantity first and test them with a small order before you trust them with a large one.

## Grading, Washing, and Preparing Your Stock

Once your bale arrives, sort it before you photograph anything. Lay the clothes out and grade each piece honestly: premium pieces with no faults, good pieces with tiny faults a buyer would accept, and pieces that are only fit for giveaway or rags. Sell only the first two grades. Giving away or discarding the bad pieces is better than selling them and getting a complaint that damages your name.

Wash everything you plan to sell. Buyers expect Okrika to be clean, and washing removes the strong bale smell that puts people off. Use a good detergent, wash in batches, and dry in the sun where possible. For delicate items, hand wash. For jeans and jackets, a normal wash is usually fine. Iron every piece before you photograph it. A wrinkled shirt photographs badly and looks cheap, while the same shirt ironed looks like a boutique item.

Check each piece carefully after washing. Look for stains that did not come out, loose buttons, weak seams, and fading. Fix small problems if you can. If a fault cannot be fixed, either sell the piece at a lower price with the fault clearly shown, or do not sell it at all.

Sort your stock by category and size. Keep men's shirts together, women's gowns together, children's wear together, and sneakers by size. This makes photographing faster and makes it easy to answer a buyer who asks "do you have this in size 42?" without searching through a pile for ten minutes.

## Pricing Your Okrika for Profit

Pricing Okrika is different from pricing new clothes, because every piece is unique and your cost per piece is not fixed. Start by working out your cost per piece: divide the price of the bale plus transport by the number of sellable pieces in it. If a bale costs ₦120,000 with transport, and you get 60 sellable pieces from it, your cost per piece is ₦2,000. That number is the foundation of every price you set.

Price each piece according to its quality, brand, and condition. A premium piece — a clean, fashionable, well-known brand in a popular size — can sell for three or four times your cost. A normal piece might sell for two times your cost. A piece with a small fault sells for less, with the fault clearly shown. Do not price everything the same. Flat pricing wastes money on your best pieces and leaves your weaker pieces unsold.

Study what other sellers charge for similar items. Check Instagram, TikTok, and WhatsApp groups for prices on the same type of clothes. If similar gowns sell for ₦7,000 to ₦10,000, pricing yours at ₦8,500 is reasonable. Pricing at ₦15,000 without a clear reason will leave you with unsold stock. Pricing at ₦3,000 might sell fast but leaves you no profit after delivery and packaging.

Offer bundles to move stock faster. A "3 shirts for ₦12,000" deal or a "buy two gowns, get one top free" offer helps you clear slower pieces while keeping your average price healthy. Bundles also increase the value of each order, which helps cover delivery costs.

Be ready to negotiate, but set a floor price. Many Okrika buyers will ask "last price?" Decide your lowest acceptable price for each category before you post, and do not go below it. Selling at a loss to please one buyer teaches your customers that your prices are not real, and they will keep pushing lower.

## Photographing and Listing Your Clothes

Your photos decide whether a buyer stops scrolling or moves on. Okrika needs better photos than new clothes, because the buyer is already worried about quality. Your job is to remove that worry with clear, honest pictures.

Photograph in daylight against a plain background. A white wall, a clean bedsheet, or a plain floor works well. Hang shirts and gowns on a hanger, lay jeans flat, and stand sneakers upright. Take at least three photos of each item: the front, the back, and a close-up of the fabric, label, or any detail that proves quality. If there is a small fault, photograph it clearly and mention it. Buyers respect that honesty, and it stops complaints later.

Show clothes on a person or a mannequin when you can. A gown looks far better worn than lying flat, and a jacket looks better on a shoulder than on a hanger. You do not need a model. You, a friend, or a simple mannequin will do. Make sure the lighting is bright and the background is tidy.

Film short videos of new arrivals. A thirty-second video flipping through a rail of new shirts, or showing five new gowns one after another, is one of the most effective ways to sell Okrika online. Post these videos on TikTok, Instagram Reels, and your WhatsApp status. New stock videos create excitement, and regular buyers will check your page daily to see what arrived.

Write clear descriptions for every item. State the type, the size, the colour, the condition, and the price. "Men's vintage shirt, size L, blue check, very good condition, ₦6,000" tells a buyer everything they need. "Fine shirt, DM for price" tells them nothing and sends them to a seller who is clearer.

## Selling on WhatsApp, Instagram, and TikTok

Most Okrika sellers in Nigeria take orders on WhatsApp Business, Instagram, and TikTok. Each platform plays a different role, and using all three well gives you more buyers than any one alone.

WhatsApp Business is where the sale happens. Set up a catalogue with your items, prices, and photos. Use your status daily to show new arrivals, sold items, and restocks. Create broadcast lists for your regular buyers, grouped by what they buy — men's wear, women's wear, children's wear — so you send each group only what they care about. Reply fast. In Okrika, the first seller to reply often gets the sale, because the buyer has already moved on to the next seller by the time a slow reply arrives.

Instagram is your shop window. Post your best pieces, styling ideas, and customer photos. Use a clear bio that says what you sell, your location, and how to order. Post consistently, three to five times a week, and use hashtags that buyers actually search for, like the type of clothing and your city. Share your customers' photos with permission. A buyer wearing your gown at a wedding is the best advert you can get.

TikTok is your discovery engine. Short videos of new stock, styling tips, and "what ₦10,000 buys in my Okrika shop" videos reach people who have never heard of you. Film in daylight, keep videos under a minute, and show the clothes clearly. You do not need dancing or comedy. A simple video of you holding up new arrivals and stating the prices can bring in dozens of messages.

Keep your prices and availability the same everywhere. When an item sells, mark it sold on every platform, or remove the post.

## Delivery and Payments

Delivery and payment rules must be clear before a buyer pays, because most Okrika disputes start here. State your rules in your bio, in your catalogue, and in your first message to every new buyer.

For Lagos orders, use dispatch riders. Agree rates with one or two reliable riders for different areas, and confirm the fee with the buyer before dispatch. Share the rider's number and expected arrival time as soon as the parcel leaves. For orders to Abuja, Port Harcourt, Ibadan, and other states, use a logistics company with tracking. Pack clothes in a clean poly bag inside a sturdy bag, and write the buyer's name and phone number clearly on the outside.

Take payment before dispatch. For Okrika, payment on delivery is risky, because buyers can reject the order when it arrives, and you lose both the delivery fee and the sale. Use Paystack or Flutterwave payment links, or a simple bank transfer, and confirm payment before you send the parcel. For new buyers who are nervous about paying first, your reviews, your clear photos, and your fast replies are what build the trust to pay.

Offer a clear return policy and stick to it. A fair rule is that a buyer can return an item within 24 hours if it is faulty or different from the photo, but not simply because they changed their mind. State this policy before payment.

Keep a simple record of every order: buyer name, item, size, amount paid, delivery address, and date sent. That record becomes your customer list. When new stock arrives in a category, message the buyers who bought that category before. They are your warmest audience.

## Common Mistakes

The first mistake is buying a bale without checking the supplier. A cheap bale from an unknown supplier is the fastest way to lose your capital. Start small, test the supplier with a small order, and only scale up when the quality proves itself.

The second mistake is selling unwashed, unironed clothes. Okrika already carries a smell and a wrinkled look straight from the bale. Sellers who skip washing and ironing wonder why their photos look dull and their buyers complain. Wash and iron every piece. It is the cheapest way to make your stock look premium.

The third mistake is hiding faults. A stain you do not mention becomes a complaint, a refund demand, and a bad review. A stain you show clearly and price fairly becomes a bargain a buyer is happy with. Honesty costs you nothing and saves your reputation.

The fourth mistake is slow replies. Okrika buyers message several sellers at once. If you reply in three hours, they have already bought from the seller who replied in three minutes. Set up quick replies on WhatsApp Business for prices and delivery fees, and check your messages often during the day.

The fifth mistake is mixing your business money with personal spending. When you sell a piece for ₦8,000, that money is not all profit. It must cover the next bale, transport, washing, and delivery. Keep your business money in a separate account or wallet, and pay yourself a fixed amount each week instead of spending from the sales pot.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much money do I need to start selling Okrika online?

You can start with between ₦50,000 and ₦150,000, depending on the type of bale you buy. A small first-grade bale and money for washing, ironing, packaging, and delivery is enough to begin. Start with one bale, sell it, and use the profit to buy the next one.

### Where do I buy Okrika bales in Nigeria?

The main bale markets are Yaba and Tejuosho in Lagos, and the large markets in Onitsha and Aba. Visit in person first, compare suppliers, and inspect the stock before you pay. Once you find a reliable supplier, you can order by phone and have the bale sent to you.

### Do I need to wash Okrika before selling it?

Yes. Wash every piece before you photograph and sell it. Washing removes the bale smell, makes the colours look brighter, and shows the buyer that you care about quality. Ironing after washing makes the clothes photograph far better.

### Can I sell Okrika on Instagram and TikTok without a shop?

Yes. Most Okrika sellers start without a physical shop, using Instagram, TikTok, and WhatsApp Business. A shop helps later, when you have regular customers and need space to store and display stock, but it is not required to take your first orders.

### What should I do if a buyer says the item is faulty?

Reply the same day and ask for a photo of the fault. If the fault is real and you did not mention it, offer a replacement or a refund. If you showed the fault clearly before the sale, remind the buyer politely of the description they saw. A calm, fair response protects your reputation.

## What to Do Next

1. Visit Yaba, Tejuosho, Onitsha, or Aba market and compare at least three bale suppliers before you spend anything.
2. Buy one first-grade bale in a category you understand well, such as women's gowns, men's shirts, or children's wear.
3. Sort, wash, iron, and grade every piece, and set aside any item with faults you cannot fix.
4. Work out your cost per piece and set prices by quality, with a clear lowest price for each category.
5. Photograph each item in daylight from three angles, including a close-up, and show any fault clearly.
6. Set up WhatsApp Business with a catalogue and quick replies, and tidy up your Instagram page with a clear bio.
7. Post new arrivals daily on WhatsApp status, Instagram, and TikTok, and mark items sold as soon as they go.
8. Take payment before dispatch, use a reliable rider or courier, and keep a simple record of every buyer and order.
