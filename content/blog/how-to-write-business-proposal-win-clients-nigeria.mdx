A business owner in Lekki needs a new website. A hotel in Abuja wants a marketing plan for its event hall. A school in Port Harcourt is looking for someone to manage its social media. In each case, several businesses will be asked to submit a proposal, and only one will win the job. The difference between winning and losing is rarely talent alone. It is usually the proposal: how clearly it shows that you understand the client's problem, how convincingly it explains your solution, and how professionally it is presented.

A business proposal is a written offer. It tells a potential client what you will do for them, how you will do it, how long it will take, and how much it will cost. It is your sales pitch in document form, and in Nigeria it often decides whether you get invited to a meeting or your email is quietly deleted. Many small businesses lose work they could have won simply because their proposal was vague, too long, badly priced, or sent late.

This guide shows you how to write a proposal that wins clients in Nigeria. You will learn how to understand what the client really wants, how to structure your proposal so it is easy to read, how to price your work in naira without underselling yourself, and how to follow up after you send it. Whether you are a web developer in Ikeja, a caterer in Surulere, a cleaning company in Abuja, a branding consultant in Lekki, or a trainer in Ibadan, the same principles apply.

## What a Good Proposal Actually Does

A proposal has one job: to convince a specific client that you are the right person for their specific job. It is not a brochure about your business. It is not a list of everything you have ever done. It is an answer to one question the client is asking: can this person solve my problem at a fair price, without drama?

A good proposal does four things. First, it shows you listened. It repeats the client's problem back to them in their own terms, so they know you understand. Second, it presents a clear solution, with concrete steps and deliverables the client can picture. Third, it gives a fair price with a clear breakdown, so the client knows exactly what they are paying for. Fourth, it makes the next step easy, telling the client exactly how to accept the proposal and what happens after they do.

## Step 1: Understand the Client Before You Write a Word

Never start writing a proposal until you understand the job. If the client sent a written brief, read it twice and highlight the key requirements. If the request came through a phone call or a WhatsApp message, ask questions before you quote anything. A client who says "I need a website" might want a simple five-page site, or they might want an online store with payment integration. The price difference between those two is enormous, and guessing wrong means you either lose money or lose the client.

Ask about the goal first. What does the client want the work to achieve? A restaurant owner who wants a website may really want more bookings for weekend dinners. A school that wants social media management may really want more admissions. When you understand the goal, you can write a proposal that sells the result, not just the task. Ask about the audience too: who will use the website, eat the food, or attend the training? Ask about the timeline: when do they need it, and is there an event or a launch date driving the deadline? Ask about the budget, politely but directly. A simple question such as "Do you have a budget range in mind for this?" can save you from writing a ₦2,000,000 proposal for a client who has ₦300,000.

## Step 2: Structure Your Proposal So It Is Easy to Read

A clear structure is the difference between a proposal that gets read and one that gets skimmed and forgotten. Use this structure for most proposals, and adjust it to fit the job. Keep each section short and give each one a clear heading, so a busy reader can find what they need in seconds.

Start with a cover page. It should carry the title of the proposal, the client's name and business, your business name, the date, and your contact details. A clean cover page makes a strong first impression and tells the client this is a serious document. Next, write a short introduction or executive summary of one page at most. This is a summary of the whole proposal: the problem, your solution, the price, and the timeline. Many decision makers read only this page before deciding whether to read the rest, so make it count.

Then describe the client's problem or need, in their own words where possible. Follow this with your proposed solution, broken into clear deliverables: exactly what you will provide, such as a five-page website, ten social media posts a month, or a three-day training programme. After that, explain how you will do the work: the process, the timeline, and what you need from the client, such as their logo, photos, or access to their accounts.

Next comes the investment section, which is your price, with a clear breakdown. After the price, add a short section about your business: who you are, what you have done, and why you are qualified. Keep this brief and relevant to this job. Finally, include your terms and conditions, such as payment schedule, revision limits and validity period, and close with a clear call to action: how the client accepts, and what happens next. Put your signature and date lines at the end, ready for the client to sign.

## Step 3: Write an Executive Summary That Sells

The executive summary is the most important part of your proposal, because it is often the only part that gets read carefully. It should be no more than one page, and it should answer three questions: what is the problem, what will you do about it, and what will it cost? Write it last, after you have written the rest of the proposal, so you know exactly what you are summarising.

Start with the client's problem, in one or two sentences. Show that you understand it. For example: "Your restaurant has excellent food and a loyal local following, but your online presence does not bring in new customers, and weekend tables often go unfilled." Then present your solution in two or three sentences: what you will deliver and what result it should produce. Then state the price and the timeline plainly, without hiding them. A client who has to hunt for the price will assume it is too high.

## Step 4: Price Your Work Fairly in Naira

Pricing is where many Nigerian small businesses lose money or lose clients. Price too high without justification, and the client chooses someone cheaper. Price too low, and you either work for almost nothing or the client doubts your quality. Fair pricing starts with knowing your real costs. Before you quote, work out what the job will actually cost you: materials, transport, data, software, staff time, and your own time. Then add your profit. A proposal that does not cover your costs is not a good deal, no matter how much you want the client.

Give the client a breakdown, not just one big number. A website proposal might show design at ₦400,000, development at ₦600,000, content and images at ₦150,000, and one year of hosting and support at ₦150,000, making a total of ₦1,300,000. A breakdown builds trust, because the client can see what they are paying for. It also makes negotiation easier: if the client needs a lower price, you can discuss removing or reducing a specific item instead of arguing about the total.

Offer two or three options when the job allows it. A basic option, a standard option and a premium option let the client choose the level that fits their budget, and most clients choose the middle one. For example, a social media proposal could offer ten posts a month at ₦150,000, twenty posts plus two short videos at ₦280,000, or a full package with daily posts, videos and advertising management at ₦500,000. Options make your proposal feel flexible rather than take-it-or-leave-it, and they often lead to a higher sale than a single price would.

Always state your payment terms clearly. A common and fair structure in Nigeria is 50 percent upfront to start the work, 30 percent at an agreed milestone, and 20 percent on delivery. State this in the proposal so there is no argument later. Also state how long your price is valid, usually 14 or 30 days, because costs change and a quote cannot stay open forever. If the client will pay by transfer, include your account details. If you accept a payment plan for larger jobs, say so, and state the schedule.

## Step 5: Show Proof You Can Deliver

Clients in Nigeria are careful, and for good reason. Many have paid someone who disappeared, delivered late, or produced poor work. Your proposal must give them reasons to trust you, and the best reason is proof. Show them work you have actually done, results you have actually achieved, and people who will vouch for you.

Include a short portfolio section with two or three relevant examples. If you are proposing a website, show two or three websites you have built, with links the client can open. If you are proposing catering, show photos of events you have served, with the number of guests and the type of event. Relevance matters more than quantity: three examples similar to the client's job are worth more than twenty unrelated ones. Describe each example briefly: what the client needed, what you did, and what happened as a result. Numbers help. "We redesigned the booking page and online bookings rose within two months" is more convincing than "we made a great website".

Testimonials add trust too. A short quote from a real client, with their name and business, carries weight. Ask your happy clients for permission to quote them, and keep a small file of testimonials you can use. Do not invent testimonials or client names, and do not claim work you did not do. A client who checks and finds a false claim will never trust you again, and word travels fast in Nigerian business circles.

If you are new and have little past work to show, be honest about it and show what you can do instead. Offer a small sample, a mock-up, or a detailed plan that proves your competence. A well-prepared sample can win a job even without a long track record, because it shows the client exactly what they will get. Honesty about being new, combined with excellent preparation, is far more persuasive than exaggeration.

## Step 6: Present Your Proposal Professionally

How your proposal looks matters almost as much as what it says. A proposal with inconsistent fonts, blurry images, spelling errors and crooked tables tells the client you are careless, even if your work is excellent. You do not need expensive design software to look professional. A clean document with consistent headings, readable fonts, generous spacing and your business logo on the cover is enough.

Use your business letterhead or a simple branded cover. Put your business name, phone number, email and website on every proposal. Check your spelling and grammar carefully, and ask someone else to read it before you send it. Errors in a proposal suggest errors in the work itself. Keep the document to a reasonable length. A proposal for a small job can be three to five pages. A large project might need ten to fifteen pages. If your proposal is thirty pages long for a simple job, the client will not read it.

Send the proposal as a PDF, not as an editable Word document, so the formatting stays the same on the client's phone or computer. Name the file clearly, such as "Proposal-Website-Design-ABC-Hotels-November-2026.pdf", so the client can find it again. If you send it by email, write a short, polite covering message: a greeting, one sentence about what you are sending, the price and timeline in brief, and an invitation to discuss it. If you send it on WhatsApp, send the same short message with the PDF attached. Never send a proposal without a message explaining what it is.

## Step 7: Follow Up Without Being a Nuisance

Sending the proposal is only half the job. Many proposals are won or lost in the follow-up. Clients are busy, and a proposal can sit unread in an inbox for days. A polite follow-up shows you are serious and reminds the client you exist. But follow up badly, and you will annoy the very person you want to impress.

Wait two or three working days after sending the proposal, then send a short message. Keep it brief and helpful: "Good morning, Mrs Okafor. I hope you received the proposal for the website redesign. Please let me know if you have any questions or if you would like me to walk you through it." That is enough. Do not ask "Have you decided yet?" on the first follow-up. The client has not decided, and the question only creates pressure.

If there is no reply after a week, follow up once more, and this time add value. Share a useful idea, a relevant example, or a small improvement to the proposal. For example: "I was thinking about your project and I believe adding a simple online booking form could help your weekend sales. I can include it at no extra cost if we start this month." A follow-up that gives something is far more effective than one that only asks for a decision.

## Common Mistakes That Lose Proposals

The first mistake is copying the same proposal for every client. A client can tell when a proposal was written for someone else, because it talks about your business and never mentions their specific problem. Tailor every proposal, even if you reuse a structure. Change the examples, the language and the deliverables to fit the client in front of you. The second mistake is being vague about what you will deliver. "We will improve your online presence" means nothing. "We will build a five-page website, write the content, set up your Google Business Profile, and deliver it in three weeks" means everything.

The third mistake is hiding the price at the end of a long document or leaving it out entirely. Clients need the price to make a decision. State it clearly, with a breakdown, and do not apologise for it. The fourth mistake is submitting late. A proposal that arrives after the deadline is usually ignored, no matter how good it is. If you cannot meet a deadline, tell the client early and ask whether a short extension is possible. The fifth mistake is promising what you cannot deliver. A deadline you cannot meet or a result you cannot produce will destroy your reputation when you fail. Promise only what you know you can do, and then do it well.

## Write Your Next Proposal This Week

You do not need to wait for a big opportunity to practise. Take a service you already offer and write a proposal for it this week, following the structure in this guide. Write the executive summary, describe the solution, break down the price, and add your proof. Save it as a template you can adapt for real clients, changing the details each time. The first one will take a few hours. The fifth one will take less than one.

A good proposal is a skill, and like any skill, it improves with practice. Each proposal you write teaches you something about your clients, your pricing, and your own business. Keep copies of the proposals that win and the ones that lose, and review them every few months. Look for patterns: which prices were accepted, which solutions excited clients, which follow-ups worked. Over time, you will build a way of writing proposals that fits your business and wins work consistently. In a market where the best proposal often beats the cheapest price, that skill is worth more than any advert you could run.
