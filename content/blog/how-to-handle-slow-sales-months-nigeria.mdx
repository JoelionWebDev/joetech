A fashion designer in Ikeja makes good money in December. Customers order outfits for Christmas, weddings, and end-of-year parties. Then January comes, and the phone goes quiet. February is quiet too. By March, she is borrowing money to pay rent and buy fabric. She is not a bad businesswoman. She simply did not plan for the slow months.

Every Nigerian small business has slow periods. January is slow after Christmas spending. The rainy season slows markets in Lagos and Port Harcourt. School resumption drains family budgets. Fuel price increases make customers cut back. When the economy tightens, small businesses feel it first.

Slow months are normal, but they do not have to break your business. With planning, savings, and smart selling, you can survive them and come out stronger. This guide shows you how.

## First, Understand Your Slow Season

Do not guess when your slow months are. Check your records. Look at your sales for the past twelve months, even if your records are just a notebook or WhatsApp chats. Which months were your best? Which were your worst? Write them down.

Most Nigerian businesses follow a pattern. Food sellers often do well during festive periods and struggle in January. Fashion sellers peak in December and during wedding season. School suppliers peak before resumption in September and January. Builders and furniture makers slow down during heavy rains, when construction stops.

Once you know your pattern, you can plan for it. If January and February are always slow, treat them as expected, not as a surprise. Save during your good months specifically for those slow months. A business that plans for January in November will survive it. A business that discovers January in January will panic.

Also ask why sales are slow. Is it the season, or is something else wrong? If your competitors are still selling and you are not, the problem may be your prices, your stock, or your service. If the whole market is quiet, it is the season. Be honest with yourself, because the fix is different.

## Step 1: Build a Slow-Month Fund in Good Months

The most important survival tool is money saved when sales are good. This is not profit to spend. It is a fund for slow months.

Set a simple rule: every time you make a sale in a good month, put aside a fixed percentage before you spend anything else. Start with 10 percent of profit. If you make 200,000 naira profit in December, move 20,000 naira into a separate account or a locked savings wallet. Do this every good month, and by January you will have a cushion.

Keep this money separate from your daily business account. If it sits in the same account as your spending money, you will spend it. Open a second bank account, use a savings app, or keep it in a trusted cooperative. The key is that it is hard to touch for everyday spending, but available when a slow month arrives.

Decide in advance what the fund is for: rent, salaries, and essential stock. It is not for new clothes, a new phone, or expanding the shop. When a slow month comes, use the fund to pay your fixed costs, and keep the business running without borrowing.

If you have never saved this way, start now, even with small amounts. Saving 5,000 naira a week in good months is better than saving nothing. Over three months, that is 60,000 naira — enough to cover rent or restock a small shop.

## Step 2: Cut Costs Without Killing the Business

When sales drop, the first instinct is to cut everything. That is a mistake. Cutting the wrong costs can make sales drop even further. Cut wisely.

Start with costs that do not bring customers. Reduce waste in the kitchen if you sell food. Buy smaller quantities more often instead of bulk stock that expires. Turn off extra freezers, lights, and fans when they are not needed. Negotiate with your suppliers for a better price or a longer payment period during slow months.

Review your subscriptions. Are you paying for a data plan, a delivery app, or a shop space you barely use? Pause what you can. In Lagos, many business owners pay for a second shop or a storage space they do not need in slow months. Let it go until sales recover.

Do not cut your marketing completely. When sales are slow, many businesses stop advertising, and then sales fall even more. Instead, switch to free or cheap marketing: WhatsApp Status, Instagram Stories, referrals, and word of mouth. Keep your business visible, even if you spend less.

Do not cut quality. If you sell food, do not reduce the portion size suddenly or use cheaper ingredients that spoil the taste. Customers will notice, and they will not return when sales pick up. Protect your reputation first.

Talk to your landlord and your staff honestly. If you have a good relationship with your landlord, ask whether you can pay rent in two parts during a slow month. Many landlords prefer a tenant who communicates to one who disappears. If you have staff, explain the situation and agree on reduced hours or a temporary arrangement, rather than sacking people suddenly and losing trained workers.

## Step 3: Sell Differently When Customers Have Less Money

In slow months, customers still need things. They just have less money and are more careful about how they spend it. Change how you sell to match their pocket.

Offer smaller sizes and smaller packs. A customer who cannot afford a full bag of rice may buy a small measure. A customer who cannot afford a full outfit may buy a ready-made top. A food seller who offers a small pack at 1,000 naira will sell more in January than one who only offers a big pack at 5,000 naira.

Offer bundles and combos. Combine a slow-moving product with a popular one at a small discount. A hairdresser can offer a wash, treatment, and styling package for a fixed price. A provision store can bundle rice, oil, and seasoning at a discount. Bundles help you move old stock and give customers the feeling of a good deal.

Give a reason to buy now. A limited offer, a free delivery day, or a small free gift with every purchase can bring customers back. Keep it simple and honest. A "buy two, get a small free item" offer works better than a confusing discount.

Sell to new groups. If your usual customers are quiet, find new ones. A tailor who serves women can offer school uniforms during resumption. A baker who sells cakes can offer small chops for office meetings. A boutique can sell to churches, schools, or cooperatives that buy in bulk.

Use instalments and pay-small-small where it makes sense. For bigger items like furniture, electronics, or tailoring for a wedding, allow customers to pay in two or three parts. Many Nigerian customers buy more when they can spread payment, and you get cash coming in over several weeks instead of nothing.

## Step 4: Chase Old Customers and Unpaid Money

Your old customers are your cheapest source of sales in a slow month. It costs nothing to message them, and they already trust you.

Go through your WhatsApp chats and your phone contacts. List customers who bought from you before but have not bought in a while. Send each one a short, personal message. Not a broadcast. A personal message that mentions what they bought. "Good morning, Mrs. Eze. It has been a while. We now have new stock of the Ankara you liked, and delivery is free this week. Would you like me to keep one for you?" Personal messages get replies. Broadcasts get ignored.

Follow up on unpaid money. Many Nigerian small businesses are owed money by customers who took goods on credit. A slow month is the time to collect it politely but firmly. Call or message: "Good afternoon, I hope you are well. I am doing my accounts and I noticed a balance of 15,000 naira from last month. Can we arrange payment this week?" Keep it polite, keep records, and offer a small discount for immediate payment if needed. The money you collect can pay your rent.

Ask for referrals. Tell your happy customers: "If you know anyone who needs what I sell, please send them to me. I will give you a small discount on your next order for every person you refer." Referrals cost you nothing until they bring a sale, and they bring customers who already trust you because a friend recommended you.

## Step 5: Use Slow Time to Fix the Business

Slow months give you something busy months do not: time. Use that time to fix things you have been too busy to fix.

Clean and organise your shop. Rearrange your display so your best products are at the front. Throw away expired stock. Fix the broken shelf, the leaking tap, or the signboard that has faded. A clean, organised shop sells more when customers return.

Update your price list and your records. Many small businesses do not know which products make the most profit. Use a quiet week to calculate the profit on each product. You may find that a product you sell a lot of makes almost no profit, while a product you ignore makes good money. Focus on the profitable ones.

Learn a new skill. Use slow time to learn how to take better product photos, how to write better WhatsApp messages, or how to use a simple accounting app. Watch free videos on YouTube, ask a friend who is good at social media, or practise with your phone camera. These skills will help you sell more when business picks up.

Plan your next busy season. If December is your best month, start preparing in October, not in December. Order stock early, plan your offers, and tell your customers what is coming. A slow month is the best time to plan, because you can think clearly without the pressure of daily sales.

## Step 6: Avoid Debt That Makes Things Worse

When money is tight, borrowing looks like the easy answer. Sometimes it is necessary, but borrowing the wrong way can turn a slow month into a long-term problem.

Avoid loan apps with very high interest. Some apps charge interest that doubles your loan in a few weeks. If you must borrow, borrow from a source you understand, with clear terms, and only for something that will bring money back quickly, like stock that you know will sell.

Do not borrow to pay for lifestyle spending. Borrow only for the business, and only an amount you can repay from expected sales. Write down how you will repay it, and by when. If you cannot see how you will repay it, do not borrow it.

Do not take goods on credit from suppliers unless you are sure you can sell them quickly. Supplier credit can help, but if the goods do not sell, you owe money for stock that is sitting in your shop.

If you already have a loan, talk to the lender early. Explain your situation and ask for a new payment plan. Most lenders prefer a customer who communicates to one who hides. Hiding makes the problem bigger.

## A Real Example: A Lagos Food Seller Survives January

A food seller in Yaba sells rice, swallow, and soups to office workers and students. December is her best month. January is always her worst. For years, she spent all her December profit on Christmas clothes and travel, then struggled in January.

One year, she changed her approach. In November and December, she saved 10 percent of every day's profit in a separate account. She also started offering smaller food packs in January, at 1,500 naira instead of only the big pack at 3,500 naira. She messaged her regular customers in the first week of January with a simple offer: free delivery for orders above 5,000 naira.

She also used January to clean her shop, update her menu, and learn to take better photos of her food for WhatsApp Status. By February, her regular customers were back, and two new offices had started ordering lunch from her because they saw her food photos online.

January was still slow, but it was not a crisis. She paid her rent from her savings, she kept selling small packs every day, and she did not borrow money. The difference was planning, not luck.

## Step 7: Find Extra Income Without Losing Focus

When your main product is slow, a small side income can keep cash coming in. The key is to choose something close to what you already do, so you do not need new skills or new stock.

A tailor can offer ready-made clothes or repair services during a slow month. Repairs cost little, customers need them often, and they bring people into the shop who may later order a full outfit. A hairdresser can sell hair products, wigs, or accessories to customers who are not booking a full styling session. A provision store can add phone charging, airtime, or data sales, which bring small daily cash even when big shopping is slow.

Sell online as well as in your shop. If you only sell in a physical shop, a slow street means slow sales. Put your products on WhatsApp, Instagram, and Facebook, and offer delivery within your area. A customer in another part of Lagos or Abuja can still buy from you if they see your products online and you can send them by dispatch rider. Take clear photos, write the price clearly, and reply fast. Many Nigerian customers buy from the seller who replies first.

Consider a seasonal product. If you sell fashion, add school items before resumption. If you sell food, add drinks and snacks for events. If you sell electronics, add accessories that people buy all year. A seasonal addition should be small and easy to stop when your main season returns. Do not invest all your money in a new product line during a slow month. Test it with a small quantity first, see if it sells, and then buy more.

Partner with another business. A baker can supply small chops to a nearby event centre. A graphic designer can offer flyers to a printing shop. A cleaner can work with an estate agent who needs homes cleaned before new tenants move in. Partnerships bring customers you would not reach alone, and they cost nothing to start. Talk to businesses near you and ask how you can help each other.

## Your Slow-Month Action Plan

When sales slow down, do not panic. Follow this simple plan.

First, check your numbers. How much money do you have, how much do you owe, and what are your fixed costs for the next two months? Write them down. Knowing the real numbers is better than worrying about imagined ones.

Second, use your savings fund to cover rent and essential costs. If you do not have a fund yet, start one now for the next slow season.

Third, cut waste, not quality. Reduce unnecessary spending, but keep your product and your service good.

Fourth, message your old customers personally, collect unpaid money politely, and offer smaller packs and bundles that fit a tight budget.

Fifth, use the quiet time to clean, organise, learn, and plan for your next busy season.

Slow months test every Nigerian small business. The businesses that survive are not always the biggest or the richest. They are the ones that save in good times, stay close to their customers in bad times, and use quiet periods to get better. Start your fund today, message five old customers this week, and make a plan for your next slow month before it arrives. Your business will thank you for it.
