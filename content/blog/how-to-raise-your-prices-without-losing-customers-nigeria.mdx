Every Nigerian business owner knows this moment. The price of your raw materials has gone up again. Transport costs more than it did last month. The dollar rate moved, so your imported stock costs more in naira. Your data, your fuel, your packaging — everything has crept upward. But your selling price is exactly where it was a year ago, because you are afraid that the moment you raise it, your customers will leave.

So you absorb the increases yourself. Your profit shrinks a little every month. You work harder for less. And one day you realise the business that used to feed you is now barely covering its own costs.

Raising prices is not greed. It is survival. A business that cannot cover its costs cannot serve its customers for long — it closes, and then those customers have nowhere to go. The question is never whether you should raise your prices when your costs rise. The question is how to do it well: gradually, honestly, and in a way that keeps your customers with you.

This guide walks through exactly that — when to raise prices, how much to raise them, and how to tell your customers in a way that protects the relationship you have built.

## Why Your Prices Must Move When Your Costs Move

Many Nigerian small business owners treat their prices as permanent. They set a price when they started, and it stays there for years, even as everything around it changes. That feels loyal to customers, but it is actually a slow way to kill the business.

Think about what has happened to your costs. If you buy fabric, hair, food items, phone accessories, or skincare ingredients, you have watched suppliers raise their prices repeatedly. If you deliver within Lagos, you know what fuel and dispatch riders cost now compared with two years ago. If you import anything, the exchange rate alone has moved your costs significantly. Meanwhile, your rent, your electricity, and your own cost of living have all gone up too.

If your costs have risen by a third and your prices have not moved at all, you are not being kind to your customers — you are quietly going out of business. And when a business closes, its customers lose a seller they trusted, and its owner loses their livelihood. A fair price increase protects both sides.

There is another reason to raise prices that has nothing to do with costs: your skill has improved. The cake you bake today is better than the one you baked two years ago. Your designs are sharper, your delivery is faster, your customer service is better. Customers are not only paying for materials — they are paying for the experience and reliability you have built. Prices should reflect that.

The fear that holds most owners back is simple: "If I raise my price, they will go to my competitor." Sometimes a few will. But most customers do not buy from you only because you are the cheapest. They buy because they trust you, because your quality is consistent, because you deliver on time, because you answer your phone. Price is one reason among many — and for a good business, it is rarely the only one.

## Step 1: Know Your Numbers Before You Change Anything

You cannot price properly by feeling. Before you raise anything, sit down with your actual numbers for one week and work out what each product or service really costs you.

For each product or service, list everything that goes into it: the cost of materials or stock, packaging, transport or delivery, data and airtime used to serve the customer, and any fees — for example, the charges on a Paystack or Flutterwave payment, or the commission on an Opay transfer. Then add a share of your fixed costs: rent, electricity, internet, and your own time.

Many Nigerian sellers discover something surprising when they do this properly: some products they thought were profitable are actually losing money, while others are carrying the whole business. That knowledge alone is worth the exercise, because it tells you where a price increase matters most.

Work out your current profit on each item. If you sell a dress for ₦25,000 and it costs you ₦20,000 to make and deliver, your profit is ₦5,000. If your material costs have risen so the same dress now costs ₦23,000, your profit has fallen to ₦2,000 without you changing anything. A price rise to ₦28,000 does not make you rich — it restores the margin you used to have.

Write these numbers down in a notebook or a simple spreadsheet. You do not need accounting software. You need honest figures, even if they are rough. A price increase based on real numbers is a business decision. A price increase based on panic is a gamble.

## Step 2: Decide How Much to Raise — Small Steps Beat Big Jumps

The golden rule of raising prices is this: small, regular increases are far easier for customers to accept than one big jump. A customer who sees a 10 percent increase will usually accept it without a second thought. The same customer, faced with a 40 percent increase all at once, will feel shocked — even if the final price is exactly the same as it would have been after four small increases.

As a guide, an increase of between 5 and 15 percent is a sensible step for most small businesses. If your costs have risen sharply, you may need more than one step — raise by 10 percent now, and plan another small increase in three or four months. Customers adjust to a new price within a few weeks; what they do not adjust to is a sudden shock.

There are two approaches, and both work:

**Raise everything a little.** This is simplest. Every item goes up by the same percentage. It is easy to explain — "our prices have gone up by 10 percent across the board" — and easy to apply to your price list, your catalogue, and your status posts.

**Raise selectively.** Increase the prices of your most popular items or your lowest-margin items first, and leave a few items unchanged. This is useful if you have one flagship product that customers compare across sellers. Leaving one or two prices untouched gives price-sensitive customers something to hold on to, while the increases on your other products restore your margins.

Whichever approach you choose, round your prices to clean numbers. Nigerians pay by transfer and POS far more than they used to, so exact change matters less than it once did, but a price of ₦28,000 still looks and feels cleaner than ₦27,850. Clean numbers also make your price list easier to remember and easier to quote in a chat.

And one more decision: decide whether your new price includes delivery or not, and say so clearly. Nothing damages trust faster than a customer agreeing to a price and then discovering delivery is extra. If delivery is separate, state the delivery fee to Lagos mainland, Lagos Island, and outside Lagos upfront, so there are no surprises.

## Step 3: Improve Something at the Same Time

The easiest price increase to sell is one that comes with something better. When customers can see an improvement, the new price feels earned rather than imposed.

The improvement does not have to be expensive. It could be better packaging — a branded bag or box instead of a plain nylon. It could be faster delivery, or free delivery within a certain area. It could be a small free sample with each order, a thank-you note, or a more careful finish on the product itself.

A food vendor in Abuja who raised her prices added a neatly labelled container and a small serviette pack with every order. The cost was tiny, but customers noticed — the food felt more premium, and nobody complained about the increase. A fashion seller in Lekki who raised her prices started steaming every item before delivery and including a small care card. Again, a small cost, a visible improvement, and a smoother price change.

If you cannot improve the product itself, improve the service around it: reply faster, confirm orders more clearly, follow up after delivery to ask if everything was fine. Service improvements cost you time, not money, and customers remember them.

This is also the right moment to fix anything customers have quietly complained about — late deliveries, unclear prices, slow replies. A price increase paired with better service sends a message: you are paying a little more, and you are getting a better business in return.

## Step 4: Tell Your Customers Before the New Prices Start

Never surprise your customers with a new price at the moment they are about to pay. That is how trust breaks. A customer who has agreed to buy at one price and is then told a higher price at the point of payment feels tricked, even if the increase is completely fair.

Give notice. A week or two before the change, tell your customers — on your WhatsApp status, in your broadcast messages, on your Instagram page, and in person if you have a shop. Keep the message short, honest, and warm. Something like:

"Dear customers, thank you for your continued support. Due to the rising cost of materials and delivery, our prices will be adjusted slightly from the 1st of December. We remain committed to giving you the quality and service you trust us for. Thank you for understanding."

That is all it takes. You do not need to apologise excessively, and you do not need to explain your costs in detail. Customers in Nigeria live with rising prices in every part of their lives — they understand that your costs have risen too. What they want is honesty and notice, not a lecture.

For your most loyal customers, tell them personally before the general announcement. A short message — "I wanted you to hear this from me first" — makes a regular customer feel valued, and valued customers rarely leave over a small increase.

If you take pre-orders or deposits, honour the old price for orders already paid for, even partly. A customer who paid a deposit last week should not be asked for more because your prices changed today. That is the kind of fairness people remember and tell their friends about.

## Step 5: Handle the Pushback Calmly

Even with notice and a small increase, some customers will push back. Expect it, and prepare for it, so you are not caught off guard.

The most common reaction is the customer who says your price is now too high, or who mentions a competitor who sells cheaper. Do not argue, and do not panic-discount. A calm, confident answer works best: "I understand. Our prices reflect the quality of our materials and the service we give. We would love to keep serving you, and this is the best we can do."

Some customers will genuinely not be able to afford the new price. That is not a failure. You can offer them a smaller size, a simpler version, or a lower-priced alternative from your range. A skincare seller can offer a smaller bottle. A food vendor can offer a smaller pack. A fashion seller can offer a simpler design. Keeping a lower-priced option in your range means you keep the customer, even if they spend less for now.

A few customers will leave, and that is normal. The honest truth is that a small number of customers always leave when prices rise — and those are usually the customers who were most price-sensitive and least profitable anyway. The customers who stay are the ones who value what you do, and they are the foundation of a sustainable business.

What you must not do is reverse the increase the first time someone complains. If you drop your price for one complaining customer, word spreads, and soon everyone expects the old price. Stand by your new price. If you need to help a loyal customer, help them in another way — a small extra, a flexible payment plan, a discount on their next order — not by undoing the increase.

## Step 6: Watch What Happens and Adjust

After the new prices take effect, watch your sales for two to four weeks. You are looking for three things: how many customers stayed, how many orders you are getting, and whether your total profit has improved.

Do not judge by the first few days. There is always a short period of adjustment — a few complaints, a few quiet days — and then things settle. Judge by the end of the first month.

If your sales volume fell slightly but your profit per sale rose enough to more than cover it, the increase worked. For example, if you used to sell 100 items at ₦2,000 profit each (₦200,000 profit) and now you sell 85 items at ₦3,000 profit each (₦255,000 profit), you are better off — with less work and less stock to manage.

If sales fell sharply and have not recovered after a month, look honestly at why. It may be that the increase was too large, or that it came at a bad time, or that a competitor is genuinely cheaper for the same quality. You may need to adjust — perhaps by lowering one or two prices, or by adding a budget option, or by improving something visible about your product.

Keep a simple record: sales before the change, sales after the change, and the profit on each. This record is what makes the next price increase easier, because next time you will be deciding with evidence instead of fear.

## What to Do Next

1. **Work out your real costs this week.** Pick your five best-selling products or services and calculate the true cost of each, including materials, packaging, transport, and fees. Circle the ones where your profit has shrunk or disappeared — those are your priority for a price increase.

2. **Choose your new prices and your date.** Decide on a modest increase — 5 to 15 percent is a good range — and pick a start date one to two weeks from now. Update your price list, your WhatsApp catalogue, and any printed menus or flyers before that date.

3. **Tell your customers in advance.** Post a short, honest notice on your status and send a message to your regular customers, especially your most loyal ones. Then implement the new prices on the date you promised, and review your sales after one month to confirm the increase worked.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much should I increase my prices by?

A modest increase of 5 to 15 percent is a good starting point for most small businesses. Small, regular increases are easier for customers to accept than one large jump. If your costs have risen sharply, raise by a modest amount now and plan another small increase in a few months rather than shocking customers with a single large rise.

### How do I tell my customers about a price increase?

Give them notice of one to two weeks before the new prices start, and keep the message short and honest — mention rising costs, thank them for their support, and confirm your commitment to quality. Tell your most loyal customers personally before the general announcement. Never surprise a customer with a higher price at the point of payment.

### What if customers leave when I raise my prices?

A small number of customers may leave, and that is normal. Most customers stay when the increase is modest, announced in advance, and paired with good quality and service. The customers who leave over a small increase are usually the most price-sensitive and least profitable. Focus on keeping your loyal customers happy rather than chasing those who only buy on price.

### Should I raise all my prices at once or one at a time?

Both approaches work. Raising everything by the same small percentage is simplest and easiest to explain. Raising selectively — your most popular or lowest-margin items first, while leaving one or two prices unchanged — can be gentler, because price-sensitive customers still have a lower-priced option to choose from.

### When is the best time to raise prices in Nigeria?

Natural turning points work best: the start of a new year or quarter, the launch of a new product, or a moment when a major cost has visibly risen and customers understand why. If you are regularly selling out or have a waiting list, that is also a strong signal that your prices are too low and should go up.
