## Rent Is Usually Your Biggest Fixed Cost — Treat It That Way

For most small businesses in Nigeria, shop rent is the largest single cost after stock. A shop in a busy part of Lekki or Ikeja can cost ₦2 million to ₦5 million a year. In Surulere or Yaba, a decent shop might be ₦800,000 to ₦1.5 million a year. In smaller towns, you might pay ₦300,000 to ₦600,000. Whatever the figure, it comes out of your pocket every year whether your sales are good or bad — and for many small businesses, it is the cost that decides whether the year ends in profit or in loss.

Yet most small business owners accept the first rent figure a landlord or agent mentions. They do not ask questions, they do not compare, and they do not negotiate — often because they feel they have no power, or because they are afraid the landlord will give the shop to someone else.

Here is the truth: rent is almost always negotiable in Nigeria. Landlords prefer a reliable tenant who pays on time to an empty shop earning nothing. Agents expect negotiation. And a landlord who refuses to discuss the price at all is telling you something important about what it will be like to deal with them later.

This guide shows you how to research a fair rent, how to negotiate with confidence, how to get better payment terms, and how to protect yourself with a proper written agreement.

## Step 1: Know What the Shop Is Really Worth

You cannot negotiate well if you do not know the market. Before you discuss a single naira with a landlord, find out what similar shops in the same area actually cost.

### Compare at least three similar shops

Walk the area and ask about at least three shops that are similar in size, location and condition to the one you want. You are looking for the real price, not the advertised price. A shop advertised at ₦2 million a year might have been rented for ₦1.6 million — the advertised price is a starting point, not a fact.

For each shop, note:

- The exact location and how busy the street is
- The size of the shop
- The condition — does it need repairs, painting, or a new ceiling?
- The rent asked and, if you can find out, the rent actually paid
- Whether there is a separate service charge, and how much
- Whether the shop has its own toilet, water, and a secure shutter

Ask other shop owners in the area. Traders talk, and most will tell you roughly what they pay if you ask respectfully. An agent in the area can also give you a sense of the range, though remember that an agent earns a commission, so their figures may lean high.

### Understand what drives the price

Rent is not random. The main factors are:

- **Foot traffic:** A shop on a busy road with hundreds of people passing daily costs more than a shop inside a quiet street. Decide honestly how much foot traffic your business needs. A provision store needs it. A business that sells mostly on WhatsApp and delivers may not.
- **Location and neighbourhood:** Lekki Phase 1 costs more than Ajah; Allen Avenue costs more than a side street in Ikeja. Make sure the premium you pay matches the customers that location actually brings you.
- **Size:** Bigger is not always better. A shop that is twice as big as you need costs twice as much in rent and is harder to fill with stock. Rent the size you need, not the size that impresses people.
- **Condition:** A shop that needs ₦400,000 of repairs before you can open is not the same price as a shop that is ready to use. Repairs are part of the real cost, and they are a negotiating point.

### The affordability test

Before you fall in love with any shop, do this simple calculation. Your annual rent should not be more than roughly 10 to 15 percent of your expected annual sales, and you should be able to cover three months of rent from your savings without touching your stock money.

Worked example: if your shop sells about ₦500,000 of goods a month — ₦6 million a year — then a rent of ₦900,000 a year is 15 percent of sales, which is at the top of the range. A rent of ₦1.5 million would be 25 percent, which is dangerous unless your profit margins are very high. If the numbers do not work, the location does not work, no matter how beautiful the shop is.

## Step 2: Prepare Before You Meet the Landlord

Negotiation is won before the meeting starts. The business owner who walks in with facts, options and a clear budget negotiates far better than the one who walks in hoping for a favour.

### Know your budget and your walk-away price

Decide two numbers before you meet anyone:

- **Your target rent:** The price you would be happy to pay.
- **Your maximum rent:** The highest price you can afford. If the landlord will not go below this number, you walk away — politely, but you walk away.

Write both numbers down. In the heat of a negotiation, it is easy to agree to a price you cannot afford. A written limit protects you from yourself.

### Have a backup option

Never negotiate for a shop when it is the only shop you have looked at. If the landlord knows you have nowhere else to go, they have no reason to reduce the price. Always have at least one other shop in mind, and let the landlord know — gently — that you are considering other options. You do not need to threaten anyone. Simply saying "I am also looking at a shop on the next street" changes the balance of the conversation.

### Gather your documents

Landlords and agents take you more seriously when you look like a serious, reliable tenant. Bring:

- A valid ID
- Evidence of your business — your business name registration, or simply your records of sales and your social media page
- A reference from a previous landlord if you have one, showing you paid on time
- Proof that you can pay — a bank statement or evidence of savings

A landlord's biggest fear is a tenant who does not pay. Anything that shows you are reliable is a negotiating tool.

### Visit the shop at different times

Visit in the morning, in the afternoon, and in the evening. A street that is busy at noon may be empty at 6 p.m. Check whether the area floods when it rains — this is Lagos, and flooding is a real business risk. Check the water supply, the power situation, and whether the area is safe at night. Note every defect: a leaking roof, a broken shutter, a cracked wall, a toilet that does not flush. Each defect is a legitimate reason to ask for a lower rent or for the landlord to fix it before you move in.

## Step 3: Negotiate the Price

Now you are ready to talk numbers. The goal is a fair price that works for both sides — not to "win" by crushing the landlord, because you will be dealing with this person for years.

### Start below your target

If your target rent is ₦1.2 million and the asking price is ₦1.6 million, do not open with ₦1.2 million. Open lower — say ₦1 million — and let the landlord negotiate you up. If you open at your target, the only direction is up, and you will end up above it.

This is not dishonest. It is how negotiation works, and the landlord is doing the same thing from the other side: the asking price is above what they will accept.

### Use facts, not feelings

The strongest arguments in a rent negotiation are facts:

- "Shops of this size on this street are renting for ₦1.1 to ₦1.3 million. I would like to agree a price in that range."
- "The shop needs a new ceiling and painting, which will cost me about ₦250,000. Can we reflect that in the rent?"
- "I am ready to pay today and to stay for two years. Can we agree a price that works for a long-term tenant?"

Compare that with weak arguments: "The rent is too much for me" or "Please help me, I am a small business." Landlords hear those every day, and they do not move the price. Facts do.

### Trade things, not just money

If the landlord will not reduce the rent, ask for something else of value:

- **A rent-free period:** One or two months free at the start while you set up the shop. On a ₦1.5 million annual rent, two months free is worth ₦250,000.
- **Repairs by the landlord:** The landlord fixes the roof, the toilet, or the shutter before you move in, instead of you paying for it.
- **A longer agreement:** A two-year agreement at the current price, with a written promise that the rent will not increase in the second year.
- **A cap on future increases:** A written agreement that any future increase will not be more than a set percentage, for example 10 percent.
- **Flexible payment:** Paying in two instalments instead of one lump sum (more on this below).

Sometimes the landlord will give you one of these more easily than a lower rent, and it can be worth just as much.

### Be calm, polite and patient

Never negotiate when you are desperate or angry. Speak calmly, listen to the landlord, and do not be afraid of silence — after you state your price, stop talking. Many people lose a negotiation by filling a silence with a higher offer. Let the landlord respond.

If the landlord says no, do not take it personally. Thank them, leave your phone number, and say you will think about it. Sometimes the landlord calls you back in a few days with a better offer — especially if the shop stays empty.

## Step 4: Negotiate the Payment Terms

In Nigeria, landlords often ask for one or two years' rent paid in advance. For a small business, that is a huge amount of cash to hand over at once — cash that could be buying stock. The payment terms matter as much as the price.

### Ask for instalments

Many landlords will accept payment in two instalments — for example, 60 percent now and 40 percent in three to six months — if you ask, especially if you are a reliable tenant or if the shop has been empty for a while. Some will accept quarterly payments. It costs nothing to ask, and the cash flow difference is enormous.

Worked example: a rent of ₦1.2 million paid at once means ₦1.2 million leaves your account on day one. Paid as ₦700,000 now and ₦500,000 in four months, you keep ₦500,000 in your business for four extra months — money that can buy stock and generate profit. Always ask for instalments, and get the agreed schedule written into the agreement.

### Watch out for hidden costs

Before you agree, ask about every extra charge:

- **Agency fee:** Usually 10 percent of the annual rent in many parts of Lagos, sometimes more. Confirm it before you commit, and confirm who pays it.
- **Legal fee:** Sometimes charged for preparing the agreement. Ask how much and what it covers.
- **Service charge:** Some plazas and malls charge a monthly or annual service charge for security, cleaning and power. Find out the amount and what it includes — a service charge of ₦20,000 a month is ₦240,000 a year, which changes the real cost of the shop.
- **Caution fee or deposit:** Some landlords ask for a refundable deposit. Confirm the amount, confirm it is refundable, and confirm the conditions for getting it back.
- **Renovation or "key money":** Be careful of vague extra charges that are not written down. If a payment is not in the agreement, think twice before paying it.

Add up the rent plus all the extras to get the true first-year cost. A shop at ₦1 million rent with ₦100,000 agency fee, ₦50,000 legal fee and ₦240,000 service charge actually costs ₦1.39 million in the first year — not ₦1 million.

## Step 5: Get Everything in Writing

This is the most important step in the whole process, and the one small business owners skip most often. A verbal agreement with a landlord is worth very little when there is a disagreement. Get it in writing.

### What a good tenancy agreement should include

- The names of the landlord and the tenant
- The exact address and description of the shop
- The annual rent and the total amount paid
- The payment schedule, if paying in instalments
- The start date and the end date of the tenancy
- What happens at renewal, and how much notice the landlord must give before any increase
- Who is responsible for which repairs — the landlord usually handles structural repairs like the roof, while the tenant handles day-to-day maintenance
- Whether you can make changes to the shop, like putting up a signboard or painting
- The notice period if either side wants to end the tenancy
- The refund terms for any caution fee or deposit

You do not need an expensive lawyer for a simple shop agreement, but it is wise to have a lawyer look at it if the rent is large or the terms are complicated. A small legal fee now can save you a large dispute later. At minimum, read the whole agreement yourself before you sign it, and do not sign anything you do not understand.

### Keep your receipts

Pay by bank transfer, not cash, whenever possible. A bank transfer leaves a record. If you must pay cash, get a signed receipt on the spot, with the date, the amount, the purpose, and the landlord's signature. Photograph every receipt and keep the originals in a safe place. If there is ever a dispute about payment, your receipts are your proof.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Renting a shop you cannot afford because the location looks impressive.** A beautiful shop that eats all your profit is a beautiful trap.
- **Paying before seeing the agreement.** Never pay the full rent before you have read and signed the agreement.
- **Ignoring the condition of the shop.** Take photos of every defect on the day you move in, and share them with the landlord. Otherwise, you may be blamed for damage you did not cause.
- **Not budgeting for the total cost.** Rent plus agency fee plus service charge plus the cost of setting up the shop — chairs, shelves, signboard, painting — can easily add 30 to 50 percent to the first-year cost. Budget for all of it.
- **Signing a long agreement without an exit clause.** If the business struggles, a three-year agreement with no exit option can trap you. A one-year agreement with a renewal option is usually safer for a small business.
- **Forgetting to plan for renewal.** Start the renewal conversation early, and keep some savings aside for it. The worst time to negotiate is when you have no money and no alternative.

## Checklist: Before You Sign for a Shop

- [ ] At least three similar shops compared for price
- [ ] Affordability test done — rent is 10 to 15 percent of expected annual sales
- [ ] Shop visited at different times of day, defects noted
- [ ] Target rent and maximum rent written down
- [ ] Backup shop option identified
- [ ] Payment terms discussed — instalments requested
- [ ] All extra charges confirmed: agency fee, legal fee, service charge, deposit
- [ ] True first-year cost calculated
- [ ] Tenancy agreement read in full, renewal and repair terms understood
- [ ] Payment made by bank transfer with a receipt kept

Your shop is where your business lives. The rent you agree today will shape your profit for the next year or more, so it deserves the same care you give to choosing your stock or serving your customers. Research the market, know your limits, negotiate calmly, and always get it in writing. A fair rent, agreed properly, is one of the best investments your business can make.
