Your website could be losing you customers right now, quietly, every single day. Not because your business is bad, and not because people are not finding you. Because of three common mistakes that make visitors leave before they ever contact you: a website that loads too slowly, a website that never tells visitors what to do next, and a website that is hard to use on a phone. Most Nigerian business owners have at least one of these problems, and many have all three, without realising it.

The painful part is that these mistakes are invisible to the owner. You open your own website on your office Wi-Fi, on a computer you know well, and everything looks fine. Your customers meet the same website very differently: on a phone, on mobile data, in a hurry, comparing you with the next business in their search results. This guide explains each of the three mistakes, why it costs you customers, and exactly how to fix it — most of the fixes are free and take less than a day.

## Mistake 1: Your Website Is Too Slow

Speed is the first mistake, and it is the most expensive, because it decides whether anyone sees the rest of your website at all. Google's own research found that more than half of mobile visitors leave a page that takes longer than three seconds to load. Three seconds. That is the window you have. If your homepage is still loading after that, the visitor is already gone — back to Google, on to your competitor, and you will never know they were there.

Slow websites lose customers in two ways. The first is direct: people leave. The second is through Google itself. Google measures how fast your pages load and uses it when deciding which pages to show in search results, so a slow site does not only lose the visitors it gets — it gets fewer visitors in the first place. Speed is not a technical luxury. It is the front door of your business, and a slow front door turns people away before they see anything inside.

Why are so many business websites slow? The usual causes are easy to find. The biggest one is images: photos uploaded straight from a phone camera, several megabytes each, sometimes five or ten of them on a single page. Then there are the extras that load before your content does — chat widgets, pop-ups, sliders, social media feeds, and tracking tools, all competing to load first. Add a cheap hosting plan that struggles under traffic, and a homepage that should appear in one second can take eight or ten.

Start by measuring. Open Google PageSpeed Insights, type in your homepage address, and run the test. It gives you a score for mobile and for desktop, and a plain-language list of what is slowing each page down. Run the same test on two or three other important pages, such as your services page and your contact page, because a fast homepage does not guarantee fast inner pages. Write down the scores so you can compare them after you fix things.

Then fix the easy causes first. Resize your images before you upload them — a photo that displays at 800 pixels wide does not need to be 4,000 pixels wide — and compress them with a free tool. Remove anything from your homepage that does not help a visitor decide to contact you: the extra slider, the third pop-up, the feed that nobody reads. If your site is built on WordPress, check how many plugins are running and remove the ones you do not use, because every plugin adds loading time. If your pages are still slow after that, your hosting plan may be the bottleneck, and moving to a better host is often the single biggest speed improvement a small business can make.

Finally, test the way your customers actually browse. Open your site on your own phone, using mobile data instead of office Wi-Fi, and time how long the homepage takes to appear. If it feels slow to you, it is slow to them. A fast website is one of the cheapest competitive advantages a small business can buy, because most of your competitors have not fixed theirs either.

## Mistake 2: Your Website Does Not Tell Visitors What to Do Next

The second mistake is quieter, and it is the one most business owners never notice. A visitor arrives, reads your homepage, likes what they see — and then does nothing. Not because they are not interested, but because the website never told them what to do. There is no clear button to call you, no WhatsApp button, no order button, no contact form in sight. So they leave, meaning to come back later, and they never do.

Every page on your website should answer one question for the visitor: what should I do now? That instruction is called a call to action, and it needs to be obvious, specific, and easy. "Call us now" with a phone number that works as a tap-to-call link. "Chat with us on WhatsApp" with a button that opens the chat directly. "Order now" on a product page. "Book a free consultation" on a service page. One clear action, written in plain words, placed where the visitor is already looking.

The most common version of this mistake is a beautiful homepage with the contact details hidden at the very bottom, or on a separate contact page the visitor never opens. Put your main action where it cannot be missed: at the top of the page, next to your phone number, and again at the end of every important section. A visitor who has just read about your service should not have to scroll back to the top or hunt through a menu to reach you. The button should be right there, at the moment they decide.

Keep it to one main action per page. A page that says "call us, WhatsApp us, email us, fill this form, download this brochure and follow us on Instagram" is really saying nothing, because a visitor faced with six choices picks none of them. Choose the one action that matters most for that page — usually a call or a WhatsApp message for a Nigerian service business — make it big and clear, and let the other contact options live quietly in the footer.

Then test it yourself. Open your homepage as a first-time visitor and try to contact your own business in under ten seconds. Can you do it? Is the button visible without scrolling? Does tapping the phone number actually start a call? Does the WhatsApp button open a chat, or does it lead to an error? Does the contact form send, and does the message actually arrive? A broken contact button is worse than no button at all, because it wastes the interest of a visitor who was ready to buy. Test every action on your phone as well as on a computer, because that is how most of your customers will use it.

## Mistake 3: Your Website Is Bad on a Phone

The third mistake is the one that affects the most visitors. Most of your customers will meet your website on a phone first — often a mid-range Android phone, on mobile data, in the middle of something else. If your website was designed for a laptop screen and never properly checked on a phone, it is failing most of its visitors before they read a single word.

Bad on a phone takes many forms, and you have probably experienced all of them on other people's websites. Text so small you have to pinch and zoom to read it. Buttons so close together that you tap the wrong one. A menu that does not open, or opens over the content. A contact form with tiny fields that are painful to fill on a small screen. Images that overflow the screen and force sideways scrolling. Any one of these is enough to send a visitor away, and a site with two or three of them loses almost everyone.

The fix starts with a simple habit: check your website on a real phone, regularly. Not in a simulator on your laptop — on an actual phone, using mobile data. Open the homepage. Can you read the headline without zooming? Is the phone number a link you can tap to call? Does the menu work with a finger? Fill in your own contact form and send a test message. Click through to your services and product pages. If anything is awkward for you, it is awkward for your customers too, and they are less patient than you are.

There are a few specific things to check. First, your text should be large enough to read comfortably on a phone without zooming, and your paragraphs should be short — two or three lines each — because long walls of text are hard to follow on a small screen. Second, your buttons and links should be big enough to tap with a finger, with space between them so people do not hit the wrong one. Third, your images should resize to fit the screen instead of overflowing it. Fourth, your phone number and WhatsApp link should work as tap-to-call and tap-to-chat. And fifth, your forms should ask for as little as possible: name, phone number, and message is usually enough to start a conversation. Every extra field is another reason to abandon the form.

If your website was built years ago, or built by someone who only ever showed it to you on a computer, there is a good chance it has never been properly tested on a phone. That is worth fixing before you spend money on adverts, because adverts will only send more people to a website that cannot serve them. A site that works beautifully on a phone converts visitors that a desktop-only site loses, and in Nigeria, where the phone is the first and often the only screen, that difference is the whole business.

## How the Three Mistakes Work Together

These three mistakes are worse together than they are alone, because they attack the visitor at every step. A slow website loses people before they arrive. A website with no clear next step loses the people who do arrive. A website that is bad on a phone loses most of the people who arrive on the device they actually use. Fix one and you keep more visitors; fix all three and your website starts doing the job it was built for — turning strangers into customers.

The good news is that none of these fixes needs a redesign or a large budget. Compress your images, remove what slows the page down, put one clear button on every page, and test everything on a real phone. That is an afternoon's work, and it is the highest-return work most small business websites ever get.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How do I know if my website is too slow?**

Run it through Google PageSpeed Insights and look at the mobile score. If the page takes longer than three seconds to load on a phone, it is too slow, and you are losing visitors. Also test it yourself on your own phone using mobile data, not office Wi-Fi, because that is how your customers experience it.

**What is the single best call to action for a Nigerian business website?**

For most service businesses, a tap-to-call phone number or a WhatsApp chat button, because that is how Nigerian customers prefer to make first contact. For a shop, it is an order button on each product. Whatever you choose, make it the one clear action on the page and put it where the visitor is already looking.

**My website looks fine on my laptop. Do I really need to worry about phones?**

Yes. Most of your visitors will come from a phone, and a site that looks fine on a laptop can be unreadable on a small screen: tiny text, buttons too close together, forms that are hard to fill. Check your site on a real phone, with mobile data, and fix what you find. It is the single most useful test you can run.

**Will fixing these three mistakes improve my Google ranking?**

Yes, indirectly and sometimes directly. Google uses loading speed and mobile-friendliness when ranking pages, so fixing those two helps directly. Fixing your calls to action keeps visitors on your site longer and turns more of them into customers, which is the result the ranking was supposed to deliver in the first place.

**How often should I check my website for these problems?**

Check speed and mobile experience every few months, and every time you change something on the site — a new page, new images, a new feature. A website is a living asset, not a poster you print once and forget.

## What to Do Next

Do these three things today, in this order. First, test your speed: run your homepage through PageSpeed Insights, write down the mobile score, and compress the largest images on the page. Second, add one clear action to your homepage — a tap-to-call number or a WhatsApp button at the top of the page and again at the end of your main section — and test it yourself from your phone. Third, open your site on your phone with mobile data and click through it like a customer: read, tap, fill in the form, and fix anything that is awkward.
