Mama Nkechi sells foodstuff in Onitsha main market. Her stall is not big, her prices are not the lowest on her row, and she has never posted anything on Instagram in her life. But her customers pass three other stalls selling the same rice and the same crayfish to reach her. When you ask them why, they give the same answer in different words: "She knows me. She keeps my card."

The card is a small piece of thick paper, stamped every time they buy. Nine stamps, and the tenth bag of rice comes with a free measure of crayfish. That is the whole programme. No app, no phone, no password. And it has kept her stall full for years, through price rises, through new competitors, through rainy seasons when the market is half empty.

This guide is for businesses like Mama Nkechi's: market stalls, walk-in shops, barbershops, food vendors, laundries, provision stores — businesses whose customers come to them in person, pay cash, and may not have a smartphone or data to spare. Loyalty for these businesses does not need technology. It needs a card, a stamp, and a shopkeeper who runs it properly.

## Why paper still wins at a stall

A paper card works where an app fails, for three simple reasons. First, your customer does not need anything to use it. No phone, no data, no account, no password she will forget. She buys, you stamp, she puts the card in her purse. It works for the grandmother buying foodstuff and the student buying gala alike.

Second, the card advertises you every day. It sits in her purse next to her money. Every time she opens her purse, she sees your shop's name and phone number. No app on a phone does that without being opened.

Third, a stamp card is honest in a way a screen is not. The customer can see her progress with her own eyes: seven stamps, two to go. She does not need to log in to check a balance. She does not need to trust a system she cannot see. The proof is in her hand.

## Designing the card

The card itself matters more than most owners think. A flimsy paper card that falls apart after two weeks tells the customer the programme does not matter to you. Print it on thick card, the kind that survives months in a purse. A local printer in Lagos, Onitsha or Kano will print 500 cards for roughly ₦10,000 to ₦25,000, depending on size and paper. That is your only setup cost, and it lasts for months.

Put four things on the card, and nothing else. Your business name, big and clear. Your phone number and your location, so the card works as a business card too. The reward, in one short sentence: "Buy 9 plates, get the 10th free." And the stamp boxes — ten small squares in a row, numbered, with space for a date under each stamp. Do not cover the card with long rules. The rules live in your head and in your notebook, not on the card.

Choose the reward carefully. It should be something the customer wants and something you can afford every single time. A free plate after nine plates. A free haircut after eight. A free wash after nine. A free measure of crayfish with the tenth bag of rice. The reward should feel reachable within a month or two of normal buying. A customer who needs six months to earn it will lose the card long before she earns the reward.

Number your cards. Write a small number on each card before you hand it out, starting from 001, and keep a list of which numbers you have issued. Numbering is free, and it is your best protection against fakes.

## Running it at a busy stall

A loyalty card only works if it is offered every single time. The rule for you and for anyone who works in your shop is simple: when a customer pays, ask one question — "Do you have your card?" Say it the same way you say thank you. If she has it, stamp it there and then, in front of her, with the date. If she does not have it, offer her a new one and stamp the first box immediately. Never stamp from memory later. A stamp given an hour later is a stamp that gets forgotten, and a forgotten stamp is a broken promise.

Keep your own record, in a notebook behind the counter. One line per customer: name, phone number, card number, and the number of stamps. When a customer says she lost her card, you can check the notebook and replace it with the stamps she had. When a customer claims ten stamps but your notebook says six, the notebook settles it politely, without an argument.

At the end of each day, count the stamps you gave. It takes two minutes. If you gave out forty stamps and only took money for twenty qualifying purchases, something is wrong — usually a staff member stamping for friends. Check it daily, not monthly, while you still remember the day.

## Stopping fraud without accusing anyone

Fraud is the quiet killer of stamp cards. It rarely looks like theft. It looks like a staff member stamping a friend's card, or stamping twice "to be nice", or using the shop stamp on a card at home. The cost creeps up slowly, and by the time you notice, the programme is losing money.

Three habits prevent almost all of it. First, use a stamp that only you control — a custom rubber stamp with your business name, not a biro tick that anyone can copy. Keep it behind the counter, never in a drawer customers can reach.

Second, write the date under every stamp. A card with ten stamps all dated the same day is a card that was filled in one sitting, not earned over ten visits. Dates make cheating visible.

Third, keep the duplicate record in your notebook, and check it every week. If a card number appears in the notebook with six stamps and the customer presents the same card with ten, you know something happened. You do not need to accuse anyone. You just need the record to be there, and for your staff to know the record is there. Most fraud stops the moment people know someone is counting.

## Getting customers to carry the card

The biggest reason stamp cards fail is not fraud. It is the card sitting at home in a drawer. Three things fix that.

Put a sign where customers pay. A small board or even a handwritten notice: "Ask for your loyalty card — every 10th meal is free." Customers waiting in line will read it and ask about it themselves.

Remind people when they are close. When a customer has eight stamps and needs two more, tell her: "Aunty, two more plates and the next one is free. Come on Saturday." That sentence brings people back more reliably than any advert.

Make the card worth carrying. A card that is only a record is easy to leave at home. A card that also has your phone number, your opening hours, and a small discount on the back is something a customer keeps in her purse on purpose.

## When a customer loses her card

It will happen, often. Handle it well and you gain a loyal customer for life. Handle it badly and you lose one over a piece of paper.

Check your notebook first. If the record shows she had seven stamps, issue a new card with seven stamps and write the date. Tell her: "No problem, I have your record here." That sentence is worth more than the free plate, because it tells her that her loyalty is remembered even when the card is not.

Replace a lost card once, free. If the same customer loses it a second time, replace it again, but start her from zero and explain why with a smile. Most people accept that. What they do not accept is being told their stamps are gone because of a lost card when you have the record right there in your notebook.

## Beyond the card: what really keeps people coming

The card is the system. But the reason Mama Nkechi's customers pass three other stalls is not the card. It is that she remembers them. She knows who buys the big bag and who buys the small one. She knows whose child just started school. She sets aside the good stock for her regulars before the market gets busy. The card gives that care a shape. It does not replace it.

So use the card, but do not hide behind it. Greet regulars by name. Remember their usual order. Add a small extra sometimes — a handful of crayfish on top, an extra wrap of moi-moi — not as a reward, but as a surprise. Surprises are not in the rules, and that is why they work. A customer who expects the free tenth plate is satisfied. A customer who gets an unexpected extra on the fifth visit tells her friends.

Start this week. Print the cards. Write the rule in one sentence. Stamp the first card yourself, for your best customer, and watch what happens when she comes back with eight stamps and two visits to go. That walk back to your stall, past three other stalls selling the same thing, is the whole business. The card just makes it happen on purpose.
