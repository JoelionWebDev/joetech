On Glovo and Chowdeck, your food photo is your shop front. A customer scrolling through dozens of restaurants in Lekki, Ikeja, or Abuja will stop for a photo that looks fresh, hot, and generous. They will scroll past a dark, blurry photo, even if the food tastes better. It is not fair, but it is how delivery apps work. People order with their eyes first.

Many Nigerian food vendors know this, but they think good food photography needs a studio, a professional camera, and a big budget. It does not. With a clean phone camera, natural light, a tidy background, and a few styling habits, you can take photos that compete with bigger restaurants. This guide shows you how, step by step, using tools you already have.

It covers phone settings, light, backgrounds, plating for Nigerian dishes like jollof rice, fried rice, amala, pepper soup, shawarma, and small chops, how to shoot for Glovo and Chowdeck specifically, common mistakes to avoid, and a simple weekly routine to keep your menu looking fresh.

## Why Your Photo Matters More Than Your Description

A delivery app gives you a small square photo and a few lines of text. The customer cannot smell the pepper soup or taste the chicken. The photo has to do all of that work. A bright photo of jollof rice with well-grilled chicken, fresh pepper, and a clean plate tells the customer the food is fresh and the vendor cares. A dark photo in a plastic container with oil stains tells a different story, even if the food inside is delicious.

Good photos also reduce complaints. When the photo shows the real portion, the real packaging, and the real extras, the customer knows what to expect. They are less likely to complain that the portion was small or that the food looked different. Honest, attractive photos build trust and bring repeat orders.

On Instagram and TikTok, the same photos work as marketing. A good food photo can be shared, saved, and used in a Reel. A poor photo will not be shared, no matter how good the caption is. Investing an hour a week in food photography is one of the cheapest ways to grow a food business in Nigeria.

## Start With Your Phone — You Do Not Need a Camera

You do not need to buy a camera to start. Most phones sold in Nigeria in the last few years take good enough food photos if you use them correctly. The problem is usually not the phone. It is dirty lens, low light, and shaky hands.

Before every shoot, wipe your phone camera lens with a soft, dry cloth. A fingerprint or oil smudge makes photos look hazy, and many vendors never notice. Turn off your flash. Phone flash makes food look flat and oily, and it creates harsh shadows. Use natural light instead, which we cover next.

Use the back camera, not the selfie camera, because it is sharper. Tap on the food on your screen to focus, and hold your finger to lock the focus if your phone allows it. Then slide the brightness control slightly down if the photo looks too bright, or slightly up if it looks too dark. Take several shots from slightly different angles, and choose the best one later. Do not rely on a single photo.

Turn on the grid lines in your camera settings. The grid helps you place the plate in the centre or slightly off-centre in a pleasing way. Use portrait mode carefully. It can make the food stand out by blurring the background, but it sometimes blurs the edge of the plate too. For delivery apps, a clear, sharp photo is usually better than a heavily blurred one.

Keep your phone steady. Rest your elbows on the table, or place the phone on a stack of books if you need a top-down shot. Take the photo in the highest quality your phone allows, and do not use heavy filters. Customers want to see the real colour of the food, not an orange filter that makes everything look artificial.

## Light Is Everything: Use Natural Light Near a Window

If you remember one thing from this guide, make it this: natural light matters more than any other factor. Good light makes food look fresh and appetising. Bad light makes even the best jollof look dull.

Shoot near a window during the day, between about 9 am and 4 pm, when the light is bright but not harsh. Place the food on a table next to the window, so the light falls from the side. Side light creates gentle shadows that show texture — the grains of rice, the grill marks on chicken, the shine of stew. Avoid shooting directly under a ceiling bulb at night. Yellow bulbs make food look orange and old, and they create ugly shadows.

If the sun is very strong, move the food a little away from the window or use a thin white curtain to soften the light. Do not shoot in direct harsh sun, which creates black shadows and washed-out colours. On a cloudy day, the light is actually ideal for food photography because it is soft and even.

Never mix light sources. Do not shoot with window light plus a yellow kitchen bulb plus a white ring light all at once. The colours will clash. Choose one main light source — daylight — and turn off the others. If you must shoot at night, use a single white LED light from the side, and keep it consistent.

For vendors who cook early in the morning or late at night, plan a weekly photo session in daylight. Cook a fresh batch of your bestsellers on Saturday morning, photograph them by the window, and use those photos for the whole week. Fresh food photographed in good light is better than old food photographed in bad light.

## Backgrounds and Props: Keep It Clean and Nigerian

A busy background distracts from the food. A clean background makes the food the hero. You do not need to buy expensive backdrops. A plain wooden table, a white tablecloth, a clean tray, or a sheet of plain coloured paper can work well.

Choose a background that contrasts with the food. Jollof rice looks good on a white or dark plate against a light background. Amala and ewedu look good on a dark tray that makes the green soup stand out. Shawarma looks good on a wooden board with a clean napkin beside it. Avoid patterned tablecloths, bright plastic tables, and cluttered kitchen counters. Remove bottles, rags, and other items from the frame.

Use props sparingly. A small bowl of pepper, a sprig of scent leaf, a fork, or a glass of Chapman can add context, but too many props make the photo look messy. For Nigerian food, one or two local touches work well: a small calabash, a woven mat under the plate, or fresh ugu leaves. Do not add props that are not part of the order, or customers will ask for them and be disappointed.

Show the food in the packaging the customer will actually receive, at least once. Many vendors photograph food on a fine ceramic plate, but deliver it in a plastic container. The customer feels misled when the delivery looks different. Take one beautiful plated photo for the main menu image, and one honest photo in the delivery pack for the description. For small chops, show the box open with the items arranged neatly. For soups, show the container sealed and labelled, so customers know it will travel well.

## Plating Nigerian Dishes So They Photograph Well

Nigerian food is colourful and generous, which is an advantage. The goal is to show that generosity without making the plate look messy.

For jollof and fried rice, use a wide, shallow plate or a clean takeaway pack. Mound the rice neatly, not flat and spread out. Place the protein — chicken, turkey, or fish — on top or at the side where it is clearly visible. Add a small garnish: sliced tomato, cucumber, or fried plantain at the edge. Wipe the edge of the plate before you shoot. A clean rim makes the photo look professional.

For soups like egusi, ogbono, or efo riro, serve a small portion in a clean bowl and show the meat or fish on top. Take the photo from a slight angle, not straight from above, so the customer can see the thickness of the soup. Add a small wrap of amala, pounded yam, or eba beside the bowl to show the full meal. Make sure the soup is hot and fresh, with a light shine of palm oil, not a layer of cold oil on top.

For pepper soup, use a dark bowl and photograph while it is steaming. Steam is hard to capture, but a fresh, hot bowl with clear broth and visible fish or goat meat tells the story. Take the photo quickly before the steam fogs your lens.

For shawarma, burgers, and sandwiches, cut one in half and show the filling. A closed shawarma from the outside looks like a wrap. A cut shawarma shows chicken, vegetables, and sauce, and it makes the customer hungry. Place the two halves leaning against each other on a board.

For small chops, arrange them in a neat box: spring rolls, samosas, puff puff, and chicken in rows, not piled in a heap. Show one box open and one box closed. Customers order small chops for parties, so show the quantity clearly.

For cakes and desserts, photograph the whole cake first, then a slice on a plate. Show the layers, the cream, and any topping. Use a clean cake stand or white plate, and shoot in daylight so the colours are true.

## Shoot for Glovo and Chowdeck: What the Apps Want

Glovo, Chowdeck, and other delivery apps show your food as a small square or rectangle on a phone screen. Your photo must work at that size. A photo that looks good on a big screen may be unreadable when it is small.

Use a square crop for your main menu photo. Fill the frame with food. Do not leave large empty spaces around the plate. The food should take up at least two-thirds of the image. If you shoot from too far away, the food becomes a tiny dot on the customer's screen.

Keep the same style across your menu. If one photo is bright and one is dark, your restaurant page looks untidy. Use the same background, the same light, and the same angle for similar dishes. This consistency makes your brand look professional and helps customers trust you.

Upload a different photo for each menu item. Do not use the same jollof photo for jollof rice, fried rice, and white rice with stew. Customers notice, and they will assume you are careless. If two dishes look similar, photograph them separately with their own protein and garnish.

Update your photos when you change your recipe, portion, or packaging. An old photo that no longer matches the food will cause complaints. Review your Glovo and Chowdeck menus once a month, and replace any photo that is dark, blurry, or out of date.

Check your photos on a real phone, not just on your laptop. Open your restaurant page on Glovo or Chowdeck as a customer would, and scroll through it. If a photo looks dark or confusing on a phone, retake it. Ask a friend to look at your menu and tell you which dish they would order based on the photo alone. That answer tells you which photos are working.

## Editing: Light Touch Only

Editing should make a good photo slightly better, not turn a bad photo into a fake one. Use a free app like Snapseed or Lightroom Mobile. Adjust brightness, contrast, and sharpness a little. Increase saturation slightly if the colours look dull, but do not overdo it. Food that is too red or too orange looks unnatural and puts customers off.

Crop out distractions. Straighten the photo if the plate looks tilted. Sharpen the image a little so the details are clear. Do not use heavy filters, beauty filters, or text overlays on your main menu photo. The app already adds your dish name and price. A filter that changes the colour of the stew is misleading, and it can lead to complaints.

Save your edited photo in high quality. Do not screenshot a photo and upload the screenshot, because it reduces quality. Export the original file and upload it directly to the app. Keep a folder on your phone or Google Drive with the original and edited versions, labelled by dish name, so you can reuse them on Instagram, WhatsApp, and your website.

## Common Mistakes That Cost Orders

Many food vendors make the same photography mistakes. Avoid these, and your menu will already stand out.

First, shooting in the dark. A photo taken at night under a yellow bulb will almost always look bad. Plan your shoots for daylight. Second, using a dirty plate or container. Stains, oil marks, and fingerprints on the edge of a plate are very visible in a close-up. Wipe everything before you shoot. Third, photographing food that has gone cold. Cold rice looks dry, cold soup looks dull, and cold chicken looks pale. Photograph food when it is fresh and hot.

Fourth, taking the photo from too far away. If the customer cannot see the food clearly, they will not order it. Move closer. Fifth, using a cluttered background. A kitchen counter with bottles, a gas cooker, and a rag in the background makes the food look unhygienic, even if your kitchen is clean. Sixth, using stock photos from the internet. Customers will know when the food they receive does not match a perfect stock photo, and they will leave a bad review. Always use photos of your own food.

Seventh, forgetting to show portion size. A photo of a small bowl taken very close can look like a large portion. Add a familiar item for scale, like a spoon, a bottle of water, or a standard takeaway pack, so customers understand the size. Be honest about portions. It is better to get fewer orders from customers who know what they are getting than many orders followed by complaints.

## A Simple Weekly Photo Routine

You do not need to photograph everything every day. Set aside one hour a week for food photography, and it will cover your menu and your social media.

Pick your top five bestsellers. Cook them fresh on a Saturday or Sunday morning. Set up by a window with a clean background and your best plates. Photograph each dish in two ways: one plated photo and one in delivery packaging. Take several shots of each, choose the best, and edit them lightly.

Use the best photos to update Glovo, Chowdeck, Instagram, and WhatsApp Business. Post one photo a day on Instagram with a short caption about the dish, the price, and how to order. Save the rest for TikTok and Facebook. When you launch a new dish, photograph it the same day and add it to your menu immediately. Fresh photos for a new dish create excitement and early orders.

Keep your props and backgrounds in one box: a white cloth, a wooden board, a clean tray, a small bowl, and a fork. When the box is ready, a photo session takes minutes to set up. Over time, you will get faster, and your photos will get better. Review your best and worst photos each month, and note what worked. Good light, clean plate, fresh food, close shot — that is the formula.

## Final Thoughts: Let Your Food Sell Itself

Customers in Nigeria have many food choices on delivery apps. The vendor with the clearest, freshest, most honest photos wins the first order. The vendor with the best taste wins the second order. You need both, but the photo comes first.

Start with what you have. Clean your lens, find a window, use a clean plate, and photograph your food while it is fresh. Fill the frame, keep the background tidy, and show the real portion in the real packaging. Edit lightly, upload the best shot to Glovo and Chowdeck, and check how it looks on a phone.

Do one photo session this week for your top five dishes. Replace your darkest menu photos first. Then watch your orders. You may be surprised how much a better photo changes what customers choose. Your food already tastes good. Now let the photo prove it before the customer takes the first bite.
