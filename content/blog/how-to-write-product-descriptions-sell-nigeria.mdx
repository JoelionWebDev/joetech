In one week in October, two sellers I know each lost a sale they should never have lost. Bisi, who sells ready-to-wear Ankara dresses from her shop in Yaba, sent a size 14 dress to a customer in Abuja. The woman is a size 14 everywhere else, but Bisi's dresses run small, and her description said nothing about it. The dress came back on a bus, Bisi paid the return dispatch, and the customer bought from someone else.

In the same week, Chiamaka, who sells wigs and skincare in Ikeja, posted a honey-blonde wig that looked golden and bright under her shop light. The woman who bought it opened the box in Enugu and found a colour closer to dark honey — still fine, still a good wig, but not the colour in the photo. She did not ask for a refund. She just never ordered again, and she told two friends not to bother.

Neither seller did anything dishonest. Both descriptions were simply missing the details that matter most in fashion and beauty: the real measurements, the real fabric, the real colour, and the real hair. This guide puts those details in, using Bisi's dresses and Chiamaka's wigs and creams as the running examples.

## Fabric First: Name What It Actually Is

A buyer who cannot touch the fabric has only your words, so your words have to do the touching. "Good material" tells her nothing. "Pure cotton Ankara" tells her a lot: it will breathe in Lagos heat, it will feel soft against the skin, and it needs a cool iron. "Polyester Ankara print" tells her something else: it will not shrink, it will dry fast on the line, and it will feel lighter but less soft than cotton. Both are fine fabrics. They are not the same fabric, and a buyer who expects one and gets the other feels misled.

Say what the fabric does, not just what it is called. Bisi's best-selling midi dress is pure cotton Ankara with a cotton lining, and her description says so in plain words: "Soft cotton Ankara, lined inside, so it sits well and does not stick to your skin in the heat. The fabric holds its colour after washing — wash cold, dry in shade." She washed the sample dress six times before writing that. If you have not tested how a fabric washes, do not promise anything about it.

Stretch matters as much as fabric, because it decides the fit. "No stretch — order your exact size" and "small stretch at the waist" are two of the most useful sentences in a fashion description. A dress with no stretch and a dress with stretch are different garments, even when they look identical in a photo. Bisi marks every dress one way or the other, and her returns fell by half in the month she started.

For beauty products, the fabric is the ingredient list. Chiamaka's body cream sells for ₦12,500, and her description names what is inside it instead of calling it "very good cream": "Shea butter and vitamin E, no bleaching agents. For dry and normal skin. Rubs in without a white cast." A woman with oily skin reads that and knows it is not for her, which is a sale lost — and a complaint, a breakout and a bad review avoided. A woman with dry skin in harmattan reads it and knows it is exactly for her.

## Fit and Measurements: The Centimetres Are the Description

Sizes lie. A size 14 in one shop is a size 12 in another, and every Nigerian woman who buys clothes online has a drawer full of proof. The fix is to stop asking the customer to trust your size label and start giving her the centimetres.

Bisi measures every dress herself with a tailor's tape and writes the numbers into the description. Her midi dress, ₦28,500, carries this block of facts:

Bust 96 cm. Waist 80 cm. Hips 104 cm. Length 110 cm from shoulder to hem. Fits a size 12 comfortably, a size 14 snugly.

That block is the whole sale. A woman who knows her own measurements — and most women who buy online regularly do, because they have been disappointed before — can compare in ten seconds and order with confidence. A woman who does not know them can measure herself with a tape and a YouTube video in two minutes. Either way, she is not guessing.

Tell her how to measure, in one line, because many buyers have never done it. "Measure your bust around the fullest part, tape flat, not tight." That single sentence, copied into every dress description, has saved Bisi more returns than any other line she has written.

Say who the model is, every time. "The model is size 12, 168 cm tall, wearing size 12." A photo of a tall size 10 model wearing a dress that a size 16 woman wants to buy tells the size 16 woman nothing unless the description bridges the gap. If the dress runs small, say so plainly and say what to do: "This style runs small. If you are between sizes, order one size up." That sentence may lose you one sale today. It will save you the return dispatch, the refund argument and the customer who never comes back.

## Wigs and Hair: Length, Density and Lace, Spelled Out

Wigs are the most returned item in Nigerian online beauty selling, and almost every return comes down to a description that said "bone straight wig" and nothing else. A wig buyer needs four facts before she pays, and Chiamaka now puts all four in every listing.

Length first, in inches, measured the way the customer will measure it: "22 inches, measured from the crown to the tip when straight." A 22-inch wig that is curly will look like 16 inches on the head, and a buyer who does not know that will feel cheated when the box opens. Say it: "This curl pattern makes the wig look shorter than its 22 inches. Stretched, it reaches the bra strap."

Density second: "180% density — full, not heavy." Density is the word buyers are learning to ask about, because a thin wig and a full wig at the same length are different products at different prices. Chiamaka's bone-straight wig is ₦85,000 at 180% density, and she says so, because the woman comparing it with a ₦60,000 wig elsewhere deserves to know why one costs more.

Lace third: "4 by 4 lace closure, free part. Glueless cap with an adjustable band, fits head sizes 21 to 23 inches." The lace size decides how natural the parting looks and whether the buyer needs glue, a stylist, or neither.

And fourth, what the hair can take: "Human hair. You can straighten it, curl it, wash it and colour it. It will last over a year with care." If it is not human hair, say so in the same breath as the price. "High-quality synthetic, heat-safe up to 180 degrees" is an honest product. A synthetic wig sold as human hair is a refund, a report and a reputation gone.

## Shade Matching: Skin Tones Are Not "Fair" and "Dark"

Foundation, powder, lipstick and even some creams live or die on shade, and Nigerian sellers lose sales by describing shades the way a market trader would: "fair", "dark", "chocolate". Those words mean different things to different people.

Describe the shade against real skin. "Suits medium-brown skin with a warm undertone. If your foundation usually runs orange on you, this one will not — it is neutral, not warm." That sentence helps a buyer who has bought the wrong foundation three times and is tired of it. Name the undertone — warm, cool or neutral — because two women with the same depth of skin can wear different shades.

Show the shade on brown skin, not only on the back of a light-skinned hand in a stock photo. Chiamaka photographs her creams and foundations on her own arm and on her sister's, and she writes the shade name and number next to the photo: "Shade 340, Toffee."

For lipsticks, say how the colour looks on dark lips and dark skin — a nude that looks soft on a light model can vanish on a deep-skinned woman.

## Colour Accuracy: Say What the Photo Cannot

Every fashion and beauty seller has heard it: "Does it look like the picture?" The honest answer is usually "almost." Photos lie a little, even good ones — shop lights are yellow, phone cameras warm up reds, and a wine dress photographs as red. The sellers who keep their customers are the ones who admit the gap before the customer finds it.

Bisi photographs her dresses in daylight, by the shop door, and she writes the colour the way it looks in real life: "Wine — deeper than the photo, closer to burgundy than to red." That one line has ended the colour complaints on that dress entirely, because the buyer who orders wine knows she is getting wine, and the buyer who wanted bright red orders something else.

Do the same for prints. Ankara patterns vary from batch to batch, and a customer who falls in love with one exact arrangement of flowers will notice if hers is different. "Ankara print, pattern placement varies slightly from dress to dress — the colours are the same" sets the expectation before the parcel arrives.

Video helps more than a second photo. A ten-second clip of the dress turning in daylight, or the wig being brushed, shows movement, shine and true colour in a way a still photo cannot — write "video available on request" in the description, so the asking itself becomes easy.

## Making "Does It Look Like the Picture?" Easy to Answer Yes

Trust in fashion and beauty is built from small, specific promises that turn out to be true. Show the dress on a real person and laid flat, so the buyer sees the fit and the fabric at once. Show the wig on a head and held in a hand, so she sees the length and the density. State what is included: "Dress only. Belt in the photo is not included." That one sentence has prevented more arguments than any policy, because the belt in the photo is exactly what a buyer assumes is included.

State your exchange terms in the description, not only in your bio. "Exchange within 3 days if the size is wrong, tags still on, unworn. No refund on wigs once the lace is cut." A buyer who knows the rules before she pays can buy with confidence. A buyer who finds them out after a problem feels trapped.

Then watch what your customers ask after they buy, and fold the answers into the next description. When three women asked Bisi whether the midi dress has pockets, she added "two side pockets" to the description, and the question stopped coming. When two women asked Chiamaka whether the wig cap would fit a big head, she added the head-size range. Your descriptions get better every time a customer teaches you something, if you write it down instead of answering the same question for the twentieth time.

Start with two products this week: your best-selling dress and beauty product. Measure the dress, name the fabric, write the colour as it looks in daylight. Write the wig's length, density and lace, and the cream's shade and skin type. Read both descriptions as if you were the woman in Abuja opening the parcel. If you would feel sure about what is inside before you open it, the description is doing its job.
