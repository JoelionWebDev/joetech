A young woman in Lekki makes beautiful handmade bags. Online, her Instagram page gets likes, but sales are slow. Customers ask the same questions again and again: is the leather real, how big is it inside, will the colour fade? She cannot answer those questions properly through a screen.

Then she books a table at a weekend trade fair in Lekki Phase 1. By midday, people are touching the bags, trying them on, and asking questions she can answer in seconds. By evening, she has sold twelve bags, collected forty WhatsApp numbers, and taken five custom orders. One day at a market did more for her business than three months of posting online.

That is the power of a pop-up shop or a market stall. In Nigeria, where trust is everything and customers love to see, touch, and negotiate before they buy, selling face to face is still one of the fastest ways to grow. A pop-up lets you test a product, meet real customers, and make real money without signing a shop lease or spending millions on rent.

This guide walks you through the whole process, step by step, from choosing where to sell to counting your profit at the end of the day.

## What Is a Pop-Up Shop, and Is It Right for You?

A pop-up shop is a temporary shop. You sell in a place for a day, a weekend, or a few weeks, then you pack up and leave. A market stall is similar, but usually in a regular market like Balogun, Mile 12, Computer Village, or a neighbourhood market, often on a fixed market day.

Pop-ups work best for fashion, beauty products, handmade goods, snacks and food, accessories, thrift clothes, shoes, home decor, and small gifts. They also work well for launching a new product, clearing old stock, testing a new area, or building a customer list before you open a permanent shop.

A pop-up is right for you if you sell a product people want to see or touch before buying, if you are not yet sure your product will sell, if you want new customers outside your online following, or if you cannot yet afford shop rent. It is less useful if your product is purely digital, if you sell a service that needs a private consultation, or if your margins are so thin that transport and stall fees eat all your profit. Do the maths first, and be honest with yourself.

## Step 1: Set a Clear Goal for the Day

Do not book a stall just because a fair looks exciting. Decide what success looks like before you pay for a table.

Your goal might be sales: sell a set number of items or make a set amount of money. It might be customers: collect a set number of WhatsApp contacts. It might be testing: find out whether people in Abuja like your product as much as people in Lagos do. It might be clearing stock: sell off last season's items at a discount so your money is not tied up in old goods.

Write your goal down with a number. "Sell 30 pieces and make at least 150,000 naira in sales" is a goal. "Sell well" is not. A clear goal tells you how much stock to carry, what price to set, and whether the day was worth repeating.

Also set a budget for the day and stick to it. Your budget should cover the stall fee, transport to and from the venue, feeding for yourself and any helper, packaging, and a small float of cash for change. If your total costs are 60,000 naira, you need to sell more than that before you make a profit. Many first-time sellers forget transport and feeding, then wonder why a busy day still lost money.

## Step 2: Choose the Right Location

Location decides whether you sell out or sit alone all day. In Nigeria, the right location is not always the most expensive one. It is the place where your customers already gather.

Start by thinking about who buys your product. If you sell luxury abayas, a trade fair in Lekki or a pop-up inside a high-end salon makes sense. If you sell affordable children's clothes, a busy neighbourhood market in Surulere, Ikorodu, or Kubwa in Abuja is better. If you sell office wear, look for fairs near offices, or weekend markets that working women attend.

Here are the main options, with their honest pros and cons.

**Regular markets.** Places like Balogun Market, Onitsha Main Market, Bodija Market in Ibadan, and Mile 12 have huge daily foot traffic and customers who came to buy. Stalls can be cheap, especially if you rent a table for a day rather than a permanent shop. The downside is competition: you will be surrounded by sellers of similar goods, and customers will haggle hard. Security and storage also need care.

**Trade fairs and exhibitions.** Lagos hosts many weekend fairs: fashion fairs, food fairs, made-in-Nigeria exhibitions, and Christmas markets. These attract buyers who came to discover new brands and are willing to pay more than market prices. Stall fees are higher, often 50,000 to 250,000 naira for a weekend, and you usually need to book weeks ahead. Ask the organiser for the expected number of visitors, pictures of previous editions, and the list of other vendors. If they cannot give you those, be careful.

**Pop-ups inside existing businesses.** A salon, a gym, a cafe, or a boutique may let you set up a small table for a day for a small fee or a percentage of sales. This works well because the host business already has your kind of customer walking in. A skincare brand inside a Lekki salon, or a snack seller inside a busy office building in Ikeja, can sell very well with almost no advertising.

Before you pay, visit the location if you can and talk to other vendors about sales there.

## Step 3: Understand the Costs Before You Commit

Many pop-ups fail on money, not on sales. The stall fee is only one cost. List every cost before you commit, and add a small buffer for surprises.

Your costs will usually include the stall or table fee, transport of goods to the venue and back home, transport for yourself and your helper, food and water for the day, packaging such as nylon bags, paper bags, or boxes, a float of small notes and coins for change, and any POS or transfer charges. If you are printing a banner, flyers, or a price list, add that too.

Then work backwards from your prices. If your average item sells for 8,000 naira and your cost per item is 4,500 naira, you make 3,500 naira per sale. If your total day costs are 70,000 naira, you need to sell at least 20 items just to break even. If that number looks impossible for one day, either choose a cheaper location, raise your prices, or bring higher-priced items.

Keep a simple record on the day: a notebook with two columns, money in and money out. Write every expense as it happens. At the end of the day, you will know exactly whether the pop-up made money, and you will be able to decide whether to repeat it.

## Step 4: Pick the Right Stock

Do not carry everything you own. Carry what sells, in the sizes and colours your customers actually buy.

Look at your last three months of sales, online and offline. Which five products sold best? Those are your pop-up heroes. Bring plenty of them. Bring fewer of the slow sellers, or leave them at home entirely. A crowded table with too many choices confuses customers. A focused table with your best products sells faster.

Bring variety in price, not just in product. Have a few affordable items that people can buy without thinking, for example items between 2,000 and 5,000 naira. These are your crowd-pullers. Have your main products in the middle range. And have one or two premium items for customers who want the best. When a customer says an item is too expensive, you can offer the affordable version instead of losing the sale completely.

Check sizes and colours carefully. For clothes and shoes, bring the most common sizes: for women, sizes 10 to 16 sell fastest in many Nigerian markets; for shoes, sizes 39 to 42 are common. For skincare and food, bring your most popular shades and flavours, and bring samples people can try.

Pack your stock the night before, in labelled bags or boxes, and count everything. Write the count in your notebook. At the end of the day, count again. The difference is what you sold. This simple habit prevents losses and arguments later.

Also bring a small emergency kit: extra polythene bags, a pen, a marker, tape, scissors, a phone charger or power bank, and a small first-aid item like pain relief. You will use at least three of these, guaranteed.

## Step 5: Price for the Market, Not Just for Profit

Pricing at a pop-up is different from pricing online. Customers can see your competitors a few steps away, they can haggle face to face, and they expect a market-day deal.

Set a clear price for every item, and display it. Use a price list on a small board or printed cards. When prices are visible, customers do not need to ask for every item, you do not waste time repeating prices, and customers trust you more. Hidden prices make people suspicious.

Build a small haggling margin into your price, especially in a regular market. If your lowest acceptable price is 10,000 naira, you might display 12,000 naira and allow negotiation down to 10,000. In a trade fair or a premium pop-up, keep prices firmer, but offer a bundle deal instead: buy two, get a small discount, or buy three and get a free small item.

Offer a clear pop-up-only deal. "Today only, 10 percent off" or "Buy two, get a free sample" gives people a reason to buy now instead of saying "I will come back later" and never returning. Many customers who say they will return do not. Make the deal good enough to close the sale today.

Accept multiple payment methods. Cash is still king in many Nigerian markets, so bring enough change in 100, 500, and 1,000 naira notes. Also accept transfers and have a POS if you can, because many customers now pay by transfer. Write your account details clearly on a card so customers can copy them easily, and confirm every transfer before the customer leaves. Do not hand over goods on a promise that the transfer "will come." Wait for the alert.

## Step 6: Set Up a Stall That Stops People

You have about three seconds to catch a passer-by's attention. Your stall must look inviting, tidy, and professional from a distance.

Start with a clean table and a cloth. A plain, neat tablecloth in a colour that matches your brand looks far better than a bare, stained table. Arrange products by height: taller items at the back, shorter items at the front, so everything is visible. Do not pile goods on top of each other. Customers will not dig through a pile to find what they want.

Use a banner or a sign with your business name, what you sell, and your WhatsApp number or Instagram handle, written large enough to read from a few metres away. If you sell food, let people see it or smell it. If you sell clothes, hang a few pieces up so people can see the shape and colour properly. If you sell skincare, set out testers and a mirror.

Keep your stall tidy all day. Every hour, take two minutes to straighten the display, remove empty packaging, and refill the front row. A messy stall tells customers you do not care. A tidy stall tells them your products are worth their money.

Dress the part. Wear something neat that matches your brand, or a branded T-shirt if you have one. Customers judge the seller as much as the product, and a smart, friendly appearance builds trust fast.

Arrive early. Set up at least one hour before the market opens. The early customers are often the serious buyers, and a half-finished stall loses them. If the fair opens at 10 in the morning, aim to be fully set up by 9. Test your phone, your POS, and your payment details before the first customer arrives.

## Step 7: Sell Like a Human, Not a Hawker

The way you talk to customers at a pop-up decides how much you sell. Aggressive shouting drives people away. Warm, confident conversation draws them in.

Greet people with a smile and a simple line: "Good morning, welcome. Please feel free to look around." Do not block their path or grab their hand. Let them browse for a few seconds, then offer help: "Are you looking for something for yourself or as a gift?" A question is better than a sales pitch, because it starts a conversation.

Listen before you pitch. If a woman is looking at a bag, ask what she needs it for: work, church, a wedding? Then show her the bag that fits that need. If a man is buying snacks, ask whether it is for home or for an office, and suggest the right pack size. Customers buy solutions, not products.

Let people touch, try, and taste. For clothes, encourage trying on over their clothes or in a small changing area if the venue allows it. For food, offer small samples on toothpicks or in small cups. For skincare, let them test a small amount on the back of their hand. A customer who has tried your product is far more likely to buy it than one who only looked at it.

Handle objections calmly. When a customer says "it is too expensive," do not argue. Ask what budget they have in mind, show them a cheaper option, or explain the quality difference in simple terms. When a customer says "I will think about it," give them a reason to decide today: a small discount, a free sample, or a reminder that you have only a few pieces left in that size.

Collect contacts from everyone, even those who do not buy. Ask politely for their WhatsApp number so you can send new arrivals, and note what they liked. That list is worth more than a single day's sales.

## Step 10: Follow Up After the Pop-Up

The real money from a pop-up often comes after the day is over, in the follow-up.

The evening of the pop-up, send a thank-you message to everyone who bought from you or left their number. Keep it short and personal: "Thank you for stopping by our stall today. We hope you enjoy your bag. We will send you our new arrivals on Friday." Do not send a sales message the same night. Thank them first.

Within two or three days, send a soft follow-up to people who showed interest but did not buy. Share a photo of the product they liked and remind them of the price, with a small incentive: "This is the dress you liked. We have two pieces left in your size. If you order this week, delivery within Lagos is free." Many of those sales will close in that follow-up message.

Add every new contact to your broadcast list or customer list, with a note about what they liked. Then keep in touch once a week or once every two weeks with useful updates: new arrivals, a restock, a discount, or a helpful tip. Do not spam them daily. One good message a week keeps you in their mind without annoying them.

Ask happy customers for a review or a photo of the product in use. A short video of a customer wearing your dress or using your product is powerful marketing, and it costs you nothing. Always ask permission before you post someone's photo or video.

## Start Small, Then Scale

You do not need a big fair to start. Your first pop-up can be a small table at an estate event, a church bazaar, or a friend's salon for a day. Learn from that small day, fix what went wrong, and try a bigger fair next time. Each pop-up teaches you which products sell, which prices work, and which locations bring your customers. Bring a helper so the stall is never empty, keep your cash safe, confirm every transfer before handing over goods, and follow up with every contact you collect that same evening. That is how a single table becomes a growing business.
