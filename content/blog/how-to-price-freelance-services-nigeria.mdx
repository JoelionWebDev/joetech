## Why Pricing Feels So Hard for Nigerian Freelancers

Ask a freelancer in Lagos how much they charge and you will often get a pause or a vague answer. "It depends." "We can negotiate." "Just tell me your budget." Pricing feels personal. It feels like a judgement on your worth. And in Nigeria, where many clients expect a discount before you have even started, it can feel easier to quote low and hope the work leads to something better.

That approach rarely works. Charge too little and you work long hours for money that does not cover rent, data, transport and food. You take on too many clients to survive, your quality drops, and you burn out. When you charge without a clear method, every quote becomes a stressful negotiation, and you never know if you are making a profit or slowly losing money.

Good pricing is about knowing your costs, knowing the value you deliver, and presenting a price with confidence.

## Start With Your Real Costs

Before you can set a price, you need to know what it costs you to live and work. Many freelancers in Nigeria skip this step. They look at what other people charge, copy it, and hope it works. But your costs are not the same as another freelancer's costs. A graphic designer in Abuja with a studio, a generator and a staff member has different expenses from a writer working from home in Ibadan with a laptop and a phone.

List your monthly costs honestly. Include rent or a share of it, electricity and generator fuel, internet data, phone credit, transport, food, software subscriptions, equipment replacement, and any money you set aside for tax or savings. Add a figure for health and emergencies, even if it is small. Then add the business costs that are easy to forget: bank charges, printing, meeting a client for coffee, and the time you spend on work that nobody pays you for, such as sending proposals and chasing invoices.

### A Worked Example: A Designer in Lagos

Suppose Ada is a freelance brand designer in Lekki. Her monthly costs look like this: rent share ₦120,000, electricity and fuel ₦45,000, internet ₦30,000, transport ₦40,000, food and household ₦90,000, software ₦25,000, phone ₦10,000, savings and emergencies ₦40,000. That totals ₦400,000 a month. If she wants to earn that much and she can realistically do about 100 hours of paid client work a month — the rest goes to marketing, admin and learning — she needs to earn at least ₦4,000 per hour of client work just to cover her costs.

That is her floor, not her price. Her price should be higher, because she also needs profit, time off, and room for slow months when work is scarce. If she charges ₦7,500 per hour, those 100 hours bring in ₦750,000. After costs of ₦400,000, she has ₦350,000 left for tax, growth, rest and the months when clients pay late. That is a healthier position than charging ₦3,000 an hour and wondering why she is always broke despite working every day.

Your numbers differ. A beginner in Enugu may have lower costs than a senior developer in Victoria Island. The method is the same: add up your real monthly costs, estimate your realistic billable hours, and divide. That gives you the minimum hourly rate you cannot go below without losing money.

## Choose a Pricing Model That Fits the Work

Freelancers in Nigeria usually price in one of three ways: hourly, per project, or on retainer. Each has a place, and the best choice depends on the work and the client.

### Hourly Pricing

Hourly pricing works well when the scope is unclear, or when the client wants ongoing help and does not know how much work there will be. A social media manager who replies to comments, a virtual assistant, or a developer fixing bugs on an existing website may find hourly pricing fair. The client pays for the time you actually spend, and you are not punished for work that takes longer than expected.

### Project Pricing

Project pricing is often the best choice for defined work: a logo, a website, a set of ten social media designs, a written report, or a video edit. You agree a fixed fee for a defined deliverable, and the client knows the total cost upfront. You benefit because you can earn more as you get faster, and the client benefits because there are no surprises.

To price a project, estimate the hours it will take, multiply by your hourly rate, then add a margin for revisions, communication and risk. If a website will take 30 hours and your rate is ₦8,000 per hour, the labour cost is ₦240,000. Add ₦60,000 for revisions, meetings and project management, and your quote is ₦300,000. If the client wants extra pages or extra revisions later, those are charged separately, because they were not in the original scope.

Always define the scope in writing. How many pages, how many designs, how many revision rounds, and what is included? A website quote that says "5 pages, 2 revision rounds, contact form, delivered in 3 weeks" is far safer than a quote that just says "website — ₦300,000". Scope creep — the slow addition of extra work without extra pay — is one of the biggest profit killers for Nigerian freelancers.

### Retainers

A retainer is a fixed monthly fee for ongoing work. A business in Port Harcourt might pay you ₦150,000 a month to manage its Instagram account, post three times a week, and reply to messages. A company in Abuja might pay ₦250,000 a month for regular blog articles and email newsletters.

Retainers give you predictable income, which is gold for a freelancer. They also deepen the relationship, because you become part of the client's team. Price a retainer by estimating the monthly hours, multiplying by your rate, and offering a small discount for the commitment — perhaps 10% off your normal hourly total — because the client is guaranteeing you work. Be clear about what is included each month and what counts as extra. If the retainer covers 20 hours and the client asks for 30 hours of work, the extra 10 hours should be billed separately.

## Research the Market, Then Price Your Value

Your costs tell you your floor. The market tells you what clients in Nigeria are willing to pay. You need both. Research what others charge, but do it properly. Ask fellow freelancers what they charge for similar work. Look at job posts that state a budget. Check what agencies charge, remembering that an agency price includes overheads you do not have, so your price can be lower without being cheap.

Do not copy the lowest price you find. The cheapest freelancer in the market is often the one who is struggling most. Instead, look for a range. If logo design in Lagos ranges from ₦50,000 to ₦250,000, ask yourself where you fit. A beginner with a small portfolio might start at ₦60,000 to ₦80,000. A designer with strong work and happy clients can charge ₦150,000 and above. Your price should reflect your skill, your speed, your reliability and the results you deliver, not just your costs.

Value-based pricing takes this further. If your work helps a client make money, your price can reflect that. A sales page that helps a business in Ikeja sell ₦2,000,000 worth of products is worth more than the hours it took to write it. A brand identity that helps a new restaurant in Lekki stand out and fill tables is worth more than the cost of the design files. When you can explain the result — more sales, more bookings, more trust — you can charge for the result, not just the time.

## How to Write a Quote That Clients Accept

A quote is a sales document, not just a number. How you present your price affects whether the client says yes. A message that says "Logo is ₦100,000" gives the client nothing to say yes to except the price. A short, professional quote gives them reasons to choose you.

Include these elements in every quote:

- A brief summary of what you understand the client needs.
- The deliverables, listed clearly.
- The total price, and what is included.
- The payment terms: deposit, balance, and due dates.
- The timeline.
- How many revision rounds are included.
- What happens if the scope changes.

### Example: A Quote for a Small Business Website

"Thank you for the brief. I will build a 5-page business website for Greenleaf Foods: homepage, about, products, testimonials and contact page, with a WhatsApp button, contact form and basic search optimisation. Delivery in 3 weeks. Two revision rounds included. Total: ₦350,000. Payment: 50% (₦175,000) to start, 50% on delivery. Extra pages are ₦25,000 each."

That quote is clear, professional and easy to accept. The client knows exactly what they are getting, when, and for how much. If they compare it with a cheaper quote that says nothing, yours will often win, because it feels safer.

Always put a validity period on your quote — "This quote is valid for 7 days" — so a client cannot return three months later and expect the same price after your costs have risen.

## Handling "Your Price Is Too High"

You will hear this often. It does not always mean your price is wrong. Sometimes it means the client cannot afford you, and that is fine. Sometimes it means they do not yet understand the value. Your job is to find out which it is, without dropping your price immediately.

First, stay calm and ask a question: "Thank you for letting me know. What budget did you have in mind for this work?" That tells you whether they are close to your price or far away. If they budgeted ₦80,000 and you quoted ₦100,000, you may be able to adjust the scope — fewer pages, fewer revisions, a simpler design — to meet their budget. If they budgeted ₦20,000 for a ₦100,000 job, no amount of negotiation will make it work, and you should politely decline.

Second, explain the value in their terms. Instead of saying "My rate is ₦8,000 an hour", say "This website will let customers order directly from you on WhatsApp, and it will be ready in three weeks with two revision rounds so you are happy with the result." People pay for outcomes, not hours.

Third, never apologise for your price. Saying "Sorry, but I charge ₦150,000" makes the client think even you believe it is too much. Say "The fee for this project is ₦150,000" in a calm, matter-of-fact tone. Confidence is part of the service.

### When to Offer a Discount, and When Not To

Discounts have a place, but they should be strategic. A discount for a long-term retainer, for upfront payment in full, or for a client who brings you regular work can make sense. A discount because a client asked for one, with no change in scope, teaches the client that your first price was inflated.

If you do discount, reduce the scope to match. "I can do a 3-page website for ₦250,000 instead of the 5-page version at ₦350,000" is a fair trade. "I can do the same work for ₦250,000" is not — it tells the client you were overcharging before.

Be especially careful with "exposure" offers. A client who promises to recommend you to others in exchange for free or cheap work is rarely worth it. If the work is genuinely valuable exposure — a well-known brand, a large audience, a portfolio piece you need — you can decide to accept a lower fee, but make that decision yourself, not because you were pressured.

## Raising Your Prices Without Losing Clients

If you have not raised your prices in a year, you have effectively taken a pay cut. Your costs have risen, your skills have improved, and the market has moved. Raising prices is normal, and good clients expect it.

Start with new clients. Quote your new rate to the next person who contacts you. That is the easiest test, because there is no existing relationship to protect. If new clients accept the new rate, your old clients are probably underpaying too.

For existing clients, give notice. A month before the change, send a short message: "Thank you for working with me this year. From 1 January, my rate for social media management will move from ₦120,000 to ₦150,000 a month, reflecting the additional reporting and strategy work we have added. I value our work together and I am happy to discuss this with you." Most clients will accept a modest, well-explained increase. Those who leave were often the ones who valued low cost over quality.

Raise prices in small steps rather than large jumps. A 15% to 20% increase once a year is easier for clients to absorb than a 50% jump after three years of no change. Keep track of your results, so you can show the client what they are paying for: more followers, more enquiries, more sales, faster delivery.

## Get Paid Properly: Deposits, Milestones and Records

Pricing means nothing if you do not get paid. Always ask for a deposit before you start, usually 50% for project work. For larger projects, use milestones: 40% to start, 30% at the midpoint, 30% on delivery. For retainers, payment should be in advance each month, not at the end, so you are not funding a client's marketing for 30 days.

Send an invoice for every job, with a clear due date and your bank details. Keep a record of every quote, invoice and payment. At the end of each month, review what you earned, what you are owed, and which clients pay on time. That review will show you which kinds of work are most profitable and which clients are worth keeping.

If a client is late paying, follow up politely but promptly. A short WhatsApp message two days before the due date, and a phone call the day after if payment has not arrived, will solve most delays. Do not keep working for a client who has not paid for the last job. Pause the work, explain why, and resume once payment is made.

## A Simple Pricing Worksheet

Use this worksheet the next time you price a job:

1. List your monthly costs and divide by your billable hours to find your minimum hourly rate.
2. Estimate the hours the job will take, honestly, including meetings and revisions.
3. Multiply hours by your rate, then add a margin of 15% to 25% for risk and profit.
4. Compare the result with the market range for similar work in Nigeria.
5. Adjust for value: if the work will help the client earn significantly more, price higher.
6. Write the quote with deliverables, timeline, revisions and payment terms.
7. Send it, and do not apologise for the price.

Run this worksheet for your next five quotes. You will quickly develop a feel for what your work is worth, and you will stop guessing. Over time, keep notes on which quotes were accepted and which were rejected, and at what price. That record becomes your most useful pricing tool.

## Conclusion: Charge What Your Work Is Worth

Pricing your freelance services in Nigeria is a skill, and like any skill it improves with practice. Know your costs so you never work at a loss. Choose a pricing model that fits the work. Research the market, but price your value, not someone else's rate. Write clear quotes, ask for deposits, and raise your prices as your skills grow. The clients who respect your work will respect your price. The ones who only want the cheapest option were never your ideal clients.

Start today. Calculate your minimum rate, review your last three quotes, and ask yourself whether they truly covered your costs and your time. If not, your next quote is your chance to fix it. Charge with confidence, deliver excellent work, and let your price reflect the value you bring.

## Checklist: Pricing Your Freelance Work

- Calculate your monthly costs and your minimum hourly rate.
- Choose hourly, project or retainer pricing to suit the job.
- Define the scope, deliverables, revisions and timeline in writing.
- Compare your price with the market range, then adjust for value.
- Quote with confidence and never apologise for your fee.
- Ask for a deposit before starting, and use milestones on larger jobs.
- Reduce scope rather than cutting price when a client has a lower budget.
- Raise your rates regularly, starting with new clients.
- Track accepted and rejected quotes to refine your pricing over time.
