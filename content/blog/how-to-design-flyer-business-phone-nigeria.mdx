Chidi sells phone accessories from a small shop in Computer Village, Ikeja — cases, chargers, earphones, screen guards, the whole table. He has never owned a laptop. Everything happens on his phone: orders, payments, photos. So when he needs a flyer for a weekend sale, it has to be made on the phone too, between customers, with one thumb, on a screen the size of his palm.

That is not a limitation to apologise for. It is how most Nigerian small businesses actually work. But it is also not the same as designing on a laptop, and pretending it is will cost you. Text that looks fine on your screen turns out tiny on your customer's screen. A photo that looked sharp gets crushed by WhatsApp. An edge you never noticed gets cropped by Instagram. This article is the whole job done on a phone, in the Canva app.

## Set the phone up before you open Canva

A flyer session on a phone fails before it starts if the phone is not ready. Chidi learned this one Friday morning: he spent forty minutes on a flyer, his battery died at 4%, and Canva had not saved the last ten minutes of work.

So the first few minutes of a phone design session are not design at all. Charge the phone, or plug it in while you work. Turn the brightness up to at least three-quarters — designing on a dim screen is like proofreading in the dark, and you will miss pale text and dull photos that your customers will see clearly. Close the apps you do not need, because Canva with six apps running behind it is slow, and slow means you will tap twice and move the wrong thing. Canva needs the internet to load templates, photos, and fonts, and to save your work. Do the heavy work once, while the connection is good. If there is Wi-Fi in the shop, use it. A flyer that fails to save is a flyer you design twice.

Storage matters as well. The Canva app, your photo gallery, and WhatsApp can eat a small phone's storage fast, and a full phone saves slowly or not at all. Before a session, delete the blurry photos and old flyers you will never use again.

## Finding photos with your thumb

Photos are the hardest part of phone-only design, because on a phone your photos live in three different places and none of them is organised: the camera roll, Canva's photo library, and the internet, where the wrong photo is one tap away.

Start with your own camera, and take the photo for the flyer on purpose. Chidi's best-selling flyer used a photo he took in two minutes: a phone case laid flat on his glass counter in the morning light coming through the shop door, with the price tag beside it. He wiped the counter and his camera lens — a smudged lens is the reason most phone photos look hazy — and took five shots. One was sharp, bright, and honest.

Uploading it to Canva is one tap on the plus button, then Uploads, then the photo. But here is the phone-specific trap: do not use a screenshot of your product, and do not use a photo someone sent you on WhatsApp. A screenshot is low resolution, and a WhatsApp photo has already been compressed once, so it will be compressed again when you share the finished flyer. Your own camera photo, taken fresh, is the only one that survives two rounds of compression looking decent.

If you have no good photo of your own, Canva's library is the backup, and the search box is the skill. Searching "phone case flat lay" or "earphones product photo" gives you pictures that look like they belong on a flyer. Pick one that looks like what you actually sell. Chidi sells ₦8,000 earphones, so a photo of ₦150,000 gold headphones is a lie that will cost him a customer.

## Building in the app: thumbs, layers, and the zoom you will need

Open Canva on your phone, tap the plus button, and choose Instagram Post for a square flyer. The app sets the size for you: 1080 by 1080 pixels. Pick a template that is clean and close to what you want, not the busiest one on the screen.

Designing on a phone is a zooming job. On a laptop you can see the whole flyer and the details at the same time. On a phone you cannot. You pinch out to see the whole flyer, then pinch in to move one small element, then pinch out again to check the whole thing. Work in passes. First pass, zoomed out: headline, photo, colours, big shapes. Second pass, zoomed in: prices, phone number, small details. Third pass, zoomed out again: does it still look right as a whole?

Layers are the other phone-specific headache. On a laptop you can see a layers panel and pick exactly the element you want. On the phone, elements sit on top of each other and you select them by tapping, which means the element on top wins. When Chidi's phone number kept disappearing behind a coloured band, the fix was to tap the band, use the position controls to send it backwards, and then tap the number to bring it forward. If something will not move, or will not select, it is almost always sitting underneath something else.

Text on a phone needs a different discipline too. Read every word after you type it — autocorrect will turn "Ikeja" into "Ikea" if you are not watching. And keep the text big. A font size that looks comfortable while you are editing is often too small for a customer scrolling past it. Chidi's rule: the headline should be readable at arm's length, and the phone number should be readable as a thumbnail in a chat list. If either fails, make it bigger, and delete a line of text to make room.

## One design, two sizes: status versus feed

This is where most phone flyers quietly break. A square flyer made for the Instagram feed looks fine on the feed, and then Chidi posts it to his WhatsApp status, where it appears small with empty space above and below it, and his phone number is suddenly unreadable.

The sizes are simple, and worth writing down. WhatsApp status and Instagram stories want portrait: 1080 by 1920 pixels, tall, filling the whole phone screen. Instagram feed posts want square, 1080 by 1080, or portrait at 1080 by 1350.

Chidi's workflow, all in the app, is this. He designs the square version first, because it is the safest all-round shape. Then he duplicates the design — duplicate, not start again — and on the copy he changes the size to the tall story format. Canva on the free plan has no one-click resize, so he does it by hand: the photo gets taller, the headline moves up, the phone number moves down. Five minutes, and you have two flyers that look like siblings.

The edges deserve their own warning, because this is the most phone-only mistake of all. WhatsApp and Instagram both crop the edges of an image slightly, and on a phone you cannot see it happening until it is too late. Keep every important element — the headline, the price, the logo, the phone number — at least a finger's width away from every edge. Chidi once lost a Saturday of orders because the last digit of his number was cut off on status.

## Exporting without losing quality

When the flyer looks right, the export is the last place a phone can ruin it, and it ruins it quietly. In the Canva app, tap Share, then Download, and choose PNG. PNG keeps text sharp, which matters more than file size for a flyer. JPG is smaller and uploads faster, but the text goes slightly soft — and soft text can turn ₦8,000 into ₦3,000 in a customer's eyes. Download the PNG on Wi-Fi and post it later.

Then check the download in your gallery before you post it anywhere. Open it, zoom in, and read every word. Check the number, the price, and the spelling of your business name. A typo on a posted flyer is hard to undo once people have screenshotted it, and on a phone you are more likely to make one, because the keyboard is small and your thumb is doing the work of a mouse.

There is one more export trap, and it belongs to WhatsApp alone. When you send a flyer as a regular image in a chat, WhatsApp compresses it to save data, and the text gets softer. But when you send a flyer to a customer or a broadcast list and you want it to arrive sharp, send it as a document instead: in the chat, tap attach, choose Document, and pick the flyer from your files. Chidi sends his price list this way, and his regular flyers as normal images, because the price list is the one where a blurry number costs real money.

## The mistakes that only happen on mobile

Tiny text is the first. On a phone you are close to the screen, so small text looks readable while you are making it. If you have to zoom in to read your own flyer, your customer will not bother. Make it bigger, and cut words until it fits.

The third is the accidental drag. On a phone, your thumb is a blunt instrument. The undo button — the curved arrow at the top of the Canva app — is your best friend, and so is saving after every big change. Canva autosaves, but autosave needs a connection.

The fourth is designing on low brightness to save battery, and then posting a flyer whose colours look completely different on a customer's bright screen. The fifth wastes the most time: designing the same flyer twice because you did not save it as a template. Next week, when earphones go from ₦8,000 to ₦9,500, he changes one number and exports a new flyer in three minutes.

## A phone, a flyer, and a Saturday

Chidi makes his weekend flyer on Friday evening now, sitting in his shop after the last customer leaves, phone plugged in, brightness up, shop Wi-Fi on. He takes one fresh photo, duplicates last week's design, swaps the photo, changes the price and the date, checks the margins, exports a PNG, and sends it out. Fifteen minutes. The first time he did the whole job on his phone, it took two hours and three attempts. The tenth time, it took fifteen minutes and one attempt, because the workflow was the same and the mistakes were the ones he had already made.

That is the real promise of phone-only design. Not that a phone is as good as a laptop — it is not, and pretending otherwise helps nobody — but that a phone, used properly, is enough. Your flyer can be made on your phone too, as long as you respect what the small screen hides: the true size of your text, the true edges of your design, and the true quality of the file you are about to send into the world.
