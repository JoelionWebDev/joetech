A tailor in Port Harcourt agrees to sew ten outfits for a wedding. The price is agreed by phone, the fabric is bought, and the work begins. Two weeks later, the client changes the design, adds five more outfits, and asks for a discount because "we are family." The tailor has no written agreement, so she cannot prove what was agreed. She does the extra work for free, loses money, and the relationship is damaged.

This happens every day in Nigeria. Small businesses work on trust and verbal agreements, and most of the time it works. But when it does not work, the business owner loses money, time, and peace of mind. A simple written contract prevents most of these problems. It does not need to be long, expensive, or full of legal language. It needs to be clear, fair, and signed by both sides before work starts.

This guide shows you how to write a simple service contract in plain English, what to include, what to avoid, and how to use it in real Nigerian business.

## Why You Need a Contract, Even for Small Jobs

Many Nigerian small business owners think contracts are only for big companies. That is a mistake. A contract is even more important for a small business, because you cannot afford to lose money on a job that goes wrong.

A contract protects you in four ways. First, it makes the agreement clear. Both you and the client know the price, the work, and the dates. There is no room for "I thought you said" arguments. Second, it helps you get paid. A client who has signed a contract with a payment date is more likely to pay on time. Third, it limits extra work. If the client asks for more than what was agreed, you can point to the contract and charge for the extra work. Fourth, it gives you evidence if there is a dispute. A signed contract, even a simple one sent on WhatsApp and confirmed, is far stronger than a memory of a phone call.

A contract also protects the client. It tells them exactly what they will receive, when they will receive it, and what happens if something goes wrong. Clients trust a business that uses a contract, because it shows professionalism. Many corporate clients in Lagos and Abuja will not work with a supplier who has no contract. Having one can win you bigger jobs.



## Step 1: Put the Basics at the Top

Every contract starts with the basic facts. Write them clearly at the top of the document.

Start with the title: "Service Agreement" or "Service Contract." Then write the date. Then write the names of both parties. "This agreement is between [Your Business Name], of [Your Address], phone [Your Number] (the Service Provider), and [Client Full Name], of [Client Address], phone [Client Number] (the Client)."

If you are a registered business, use your business name. If you are not yet registered, use your full name and add "trading as [Your Business Name]." Use the client's full name, not a nickname. A contract with "Mama T" is hard to enforce. A contract with "Mrs. Ngozi Eze" is clear.

Add a short line that says what the contract is about. "The Service Provider agrees to provide the services described below, and the Client agrees to pay for them on the terms in this agreement." That one sentence sets the purpose of the whole document.

Keep this section short. The basics should fit in a few lines. The details come next.

## Step 2: Describe the Work Clearly

This is the most important part of the contract. Describe the work in enough detail that a stranger could understand what you will do, and what you will not do.

Be specific. Do not write "website design." Write "Design and build a five-page business website, including a homepage, About page, Services page, Contact page, and a blog page, with the client's logo and photos, and a contact form." Do not write "catering for a wedding." Write "Provide food and drinks for 200 guests at a wedding on 12 December 2026, including jollof rice, fried rice, chicken, small chops, and soft drinks, served by four staff from 12 noon to 5pm."

Include quantities, sizes, and materials where they matter. A tailor should write the number of outfits, the style, the fabric, and the measurements. A builder should write the size of the work, the materials, and the finish. A photographer should write the number of hours, the number of edited photos, and how they will be delivered.

Also write what is NOT included. This prevents the client from asking for extra work for free. "This price does not include the cost of fabric, which the client will provide." "This price does not include printing or delivery outside Lagos." "Extra pages beyond five will be charged at 20,000 naira per page." When the client asks for more later, you can point to this section and agree on a new price.

If the client will provide something — fabric, photos, content, access to a venue — write that down too, with a date. "The client will provide all photos and text for the website by 20 November 2026. If the client delays, the delivery date will move by the same number of days." This protects you when a delay is the client's fault, not yours.

## Step 3: State the Price and Payment Terms

Money causes most disputes, so be very clear about money. Write the total price in words and in numbers. "The total price is One Hundred and Fifty Thousand Naira (N150,000)." Writing it in words prevents confusion and tampering.

State the deposit and the payment schedule. "The Client will pay a deposit of 75,000 naira (50 percent) before work starts. The balance of 75,000 naira is due on delivery, before the final work is handed over." For longer jobs, break the payment into stages. "30 percent to start, 40 percent after the first draft, 30 percent on completion."

State how payment will be made. Give your bank name, account number, and account name. State whether you accept cash, transfer, or both. State who pays bank charges, if any. Keep it simple: transfer to the account named in the contract.

State what happens if payment is late. You can write a simple clause: "If payment is more than 7 days late, the Service Provider may pause work until payment is received, and the delivery date will move accordingly." You can also add a late fee, but keep it reasonable and clear. "A late fee of 5 percent of the unpaid amount will apply after 14 days." A late fee is fair only if the client agreed to it before signing.

State what happens if the client cancels. "If the Client cancels after work has started, the deposit is non-refundable, and the Client will pay for work already done." This protects you from a client who cancels after you have bought materials or spent days on the work. Also state what happens if you cancel, so the contract is fair to both sides. "If the Service Provider cannot complete the work, the deposit will be refunded, minus the cost of materials already bought."

## Step 4: Set Dates, Deadlines, and Delivery

A contract without dates is incomplete. Write down every important date: the start date, the delivery date, and any dates in between.

Write the start date: "Work will start on 20 November 2026, after the deposit is received." Write the delivery date: "The completed work will be delivered on or before 10 December 2026." If there are stages, write a date for each stage. "First draft by 25 November. Final delivery by 10 December."

Be realistic about dates. In Nigeria, power failure, traffic, fuel scarcity, and supplier delays can affect your work. Give yourself a small buffer. It is better to promise delivery in two weeks and deliver in ten days than to promise one week and deliver late. A client who receives work early is happy. A client who receives work late is unhappy, even if the work is good.

State how the work will be delivered. Will you deliver it in person, by dispatch rider, by email, or by WhatsApp? Who pays for delivery? Write it down. "Delivery within Lagos is included. Delivery outside Lagos will be charged at cost." This avoids an argument on delivery day.

State what happens if the client is late in giving you what you need. If you are waiting for the client's photos, content, measurements, or approval, the delivery date should move. Write: "If the Client delays in providing materials or approval, the delivery date will be extended by the same number of days." This is fair and prevents you from being blamed for a delay you did not cause.

## Step 5: Add Revisions, Quality, and Acceptance

Most service work involves revisions. A client may want changes after seeing the first draft. Agree in advance how many revisions are included, and what counts as a revision.

Write something like: "The price includes two rounds of revisions. A revision means small changes to the work already done, such as changing colours, text, or sizes. A new design, a new style, or extra items are not revisions. They will be charged separately." This prevents a client from asking for endless changes for free.

State how the client will confirm the work is accepted. "The Client will confirm acceptance in writing, by WhatsApp or email, within 3 days of delivery. If the Client does not respond within 3 days, the work will be treated as accepted." This prevents a client from holding your final payment by simply not replying.

State what quality you promise, in simple terms. "The Service Provider will do the work with reasonable care and skill, using the materials agreed in this contract." Do not promise what you cannot guarantee. Do not write "the best quality in Nigeria" or "guaranteed to bring you customers." Promise what you can control: your skill, your time, and your materials.

If your work involves materials, state who is responsible if materials are faulty. If the client provides the fabric, and the fabric is faulty, that is not your fault. Write: "The Service Provider is not responsible for faults in materials provided by the Client." This simple line can save you from a costly argument.

## Step 6: Add a Simple Clause for Ending the Contract

Sometimes a job needs to end early. A client may change their mind. You may fall ill. The contract should say what happens then.

Write a simple termination clause. "Either party may end this agreement by giving 7 days written notice. If the Client ends the agreement after work has started, the Client will pay for work already done and for materials already bought. If the Service Provider ends the agreement, the deposit will be refunded, minus the cost of work already done."





## Step 7: Sign, Date, and Keep Copies

A contract is not complete until both parties sign it. Sign at the bottom, with your full name, signature, and date. The client should do the same. If the client is a company, the person signing should write their name and their position, such as "Managing Director" or "Procurement Officer."

In Nigeria, a contract sent on WhatsApp and confirmed can be valid, but a signed paper copy or a signed PDF is stronger. Print two copies, sign both, and give one to the client. Keep one for yourself in a safe place. Take a photo of the signed contract and save it on your phone and in your email, so you have a backup if the paper copy is lost.

If you cannot meet in person, you can send the contract as a PDF on WhatsApp or email, and ask the client to print, sign, photograph, and send it back to you. Then send them your signed copy the same way. Keep the messages where the client confirms they agree. Those messages are part of your evidence.

Do not start work before the contract is signed and the deposit is paid. This is the hardest rule to follow, especially when a client is in a hurry. But starting without a signed contract and a deposit is how most payment problems begin. A client who is serious will sign and pay. A client who refuses to sign is telling you something important.

Review your contract template every six months. Update your prices, your bank details, and any clauses that have caused problems. A contract that grows with your business will protect you better than one you wrote years ago and never updated.

## A Simple Contract Template You Can Copy

Here is a simple template you can adapt for your own business. Replace the parts in brackets with your own details.

Service Agreement. Date: [Date]. Between: [Your Name / Business Name], of [Your Address], phone [Your Number] (Service Provider) and [Client Full Name], of [Client Address], phone [Client Number] (Client).

Services: The Service Provider will [describe the work clearly, with quantities, sizes, and materials].

Price: The total price is [Amount in words] (N[Amount in numbers]).

Payment: The Client will pay [Deposit amount] before work starts. The balance of [Balance amount] is due on [Date / On delivery]. Payment will be made by transfer to [Bank, Account Number, Account Name].

Delivery: Work will start on [Start date] and be delivered on or before [Delivery date]. Delivery will be by [method]. If the Client delays in providing materials or approval, the delivery date will be extended by the same number of days.

Revisions: The price includes [Number] rounds of revisions. Extra work will be charged separately, as agreed in writing.

Cancellation: If the Client cancels after work has started, the deposit is non-refundable, and the Client will pay for work already done.

Disputes: Both parties will first try to resolve any dispute by discussion.

Signatures: Service Provider: Name, Signature, Date. Client: Name, Signature, Date.

This template is a starting point, not a final legal document. For a large or complex job, ask a lawyer to review it. For everyday small business work, this simple agreement is far better than no agreement at all.


## A Real Example: A Photographer in Lagos Learns the Hard Way

A photographer in Lagos agrees to cover a wedding for 250,000 naira. The price is agreed by phone, and no deposit is paid. He covers the wedding, edits the photos, and delivers them on a flash drive. The client says the photos are beautiful, but asks for more edits, then more photos, then a video. The photographer does the extra work, hoping to be paid. Months pass, and the client pays only 100,000 naira, saying the rest will come "soon." It never comes.

The photographer changes his system. Now every job starts with a written contract: the price, a 50 percent deposit, the number of hours, the number of edited photos, two rounds of revisions, and the delivery date. He does not start work until the contract is signed and the deposit is paid. He delivers a preview with a watermark, and hands over the full photos after the balance is paid.

He loses a few clients who do not want to sign. But his business is healthier. He is paid on time, he does not do endless free revisions, and his clients respect him more. His contract did not make him unfriendly. It made him professional.

## Conclusion: A Contract Is a Sign of Professionalism

A simple service contract does not mean you do not trust your client. It means you respect your client, and you respect your own business, enough to write down what you agreed.

Start with your next job. Use the template in this guide, fill in the details, and ask the client to sign it before you start. It will take you ten minutes. Those ten minutes can save you weeks of chasing payment, hours of arguing about extra work, and thousands of naira in lost income.

Your work has value. A contract protects that value. Write it down, sign it, keep a copy, and do great work with confidence.
