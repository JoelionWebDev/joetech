# How to Manage Petty Cash in Your Small Business in Nigeria

Every small business in Nigeria runs on small cash spending. Transport fare to deliver an order in Lekki. Pure water and snacks for the shop. A printing job at the business centre. Fuel for the generator when NEPA takes light. A quick recharge card for the business phone. None of these is a big amount on its own. Together, they quietly eat your profit.

Ask many shop owners in Lagos, Abuja or Onitsha how much they spent on small things last month and they cannot tell you. The money just went. That is the petty cash problem. It is not fraud alone, although that happens too. It is mostly the absence of a system. When there is no record, no limit and no check, small spending grows until it becomes a real hole in your cash flow.

The good news is that petty cash is one of the easiest money problems to fix. You do not need an accountant or expensive software. You need a fixed float, a simple rule for who can spend it, a voucher or logbook for every naira, and a weekly check that takes ten minutes. This guide walks you through setting that up, step by step, with amounts and examples that fit a real Nigerian small business.

## Step 1: Decide Your Petty Cash Float

The float is the fixed amount of cash you keep in the petty cash box. It is the single most important decision in this whole system. Pick an amount that covers about one week of small expenses, no more.

For most Nigerian small businesses, a good starting float is between ₦20,000 and ₦50,000. A small provision shop or roadside business may manage on ₦20,000 a week. A busy salon, restaurant or delivery-heavy business in Lagos may need ₦50,000. Start small. You can always increase the float later if the box keeps running dry before the week ends. Starting too big is worse than starting too small, because a big float invites careless spending.

Here is how to pick your number. Look back at the last two weeks and estimate what you spent on small cash items. If you have no records, track every small cash expense for one week in a notebook first. Add it up. That total, rounded up slightly, is your float. If you spent ₦32,000 in that week, set the float at ₦35,000 or ₦40,000.

The float stays fixed. When the box is empty, you top it back up to exactly the float amount, not more. This is called the imprest system, and it is the backbone of petty cash control. If your float is ₦30,000 and ₦22,500 was spent during the week, you add exactly ₦22,500 to bring it back to ₦30,000. You never just throw in a random amount of money. The top-up always equals the amount spent, and the amount spent must be supported by vouchers and receipts.

Keep the float in a lockable cash box, a small safe, or a locked drawer. Never mix it with your personal money, and never mix it with the day's sales cash. Mixing is how money disappears without a trace. If you carry your business float in the same purse as your personal money, you have no petty cash system at all.

## Step 2: Appoint One Custodian

One person controls the petty cash box. Not two, not whoever is around. One person. In a one-person business, that is you. In a business with staff, pick your most trusted employee and make them the petty cash custodian.

The custodian's job is clear: keep the cash safe, pay out only against a proper request, record every payment, collect receipts, and present the box for a weekly check. The custodian does not approve their own spending. If the custodian needs petty cash for something, someone else — you, the owner, or a supervisor — approves it.

This separation matters. When everyone can dip into the box, nobody is responsible for it. When one person is responsible, the money has a guardian. Even in a family business, name one custodian. Informality is fine for many things, but not for cash.

If you have more than one branch or location, each location gets its own float and its own custodian. A shop in Lekki and a shop in Ikeja should not share one petty cash box. Each box is checked separately, and each custodian reports separately.

## Step 3: Set Spending Rules Before the First Naira Leaves

Rules written down before spending starts are easy to follow. Rules invented after money goes missing cause arguments. Write your petty cash rules on one page and stick them near the cash box or in the shop's operations notebook.

Your rules should answer these questions. What is the maximum amount for one petty cash payment? A common limit is ₦10,000 per transaction. Anything above that must be paid by transfer from the business account with your approval. What can petty cash be used for? List the allowed items: transport, fuel, printing, stationery, cleaning, refreshments, small repairs. What can it never be used for? Personal expenses, staff loans, supplier payments, and owner withdrawals are the usual forbidden items. Can staff borrow from petty cash? The answer should be no, always. A petty cash box is not a loan facility. If a staff member needs an advance, that goes through salary advance procedures, not the cash box.

Also decide what happens without a receipt. In Nigeria, not every small purchase comes with a receipt. The bus conductor will not give you one. The woman selling pure water will not give you one. So your rule must cover receipt-less spending. The practical approach is this: expenses above ₦5,000 must have a receipt, a printed invoice, or a transfer confirmation. Expenses below ₦5,000 without a receipt must be recorded immediately in the logbook with the date, amount, purpose, and the name of the person who spent it, and signed by the custodian. Transport fares are the classic example. If your dispatch rider spends ₦1,500 on bike fare to deliver an order in Victoria Island, he writes it in the logbook the moment he returns, with the order details.

Owner withdrawals deserve a special mention. Many Nigerian small business owners take money from the till or the petty cash box for personal needs — lunch, fuel for the personal car, a family expense — and never record it. This is the single biggest leak in most small businesses. Make a hard rule: the owner never takes from petty cash without recording it as an owner withdrawal, and owner withdrawals are reviewed at the end of the month. If you need personal money, take a fixed monthly amount from the business account by transfer, on a set date, and record it as drawings. Your business is not your pocket, and your pocket is not your business.

## Step 4: Use Vouchers and a Logbook for Every Payment

Every naira that leaves the petty cash box must leave a paper trail. There are two parts to this: a voucher for each payment, and a logbook that records all payments in order.

A petty cash voucher is a small slip, and you can make them yourself. Buy a petty cash voucher booklet from a stationery shop in Lagos for a few hundred naira, or print your own. Each voucher records the date, the voucher number, the amount, the purpose, who received the money, and the signature of the person who received it and the person who approved it. The receipt, where one exists, is stapled or attached to the voucher.

The logbook is the master record. It can be a hardcover notebook or a simple Google Sheet. Each line records the date, voucher number, description, amount paid out, and the running balance. The custodian fills it in at the moment of payment, not at the end of the day from memory. Memory is where petty cash records go to die.

If you prefer digital, a Google Sheet works well and costs nothing. Create columns for date, voucher number, description, category, amount, receipt attached (yes or no), and balance. Share it with yourself so you can check it from your phone. But keep the physical vouchers too. The sheet is the summary; the voucher and receipt are the proof.

Here is what a typical week might look like for a small fashion business in Lekki with a ₦40,000 float. Monday: ₦3,000 fuel for the generator, receipt attached. Tuesday: ₦2,500 transport to deliver two orders in Ikate, no receipt, logged with order numbers. Wednesday: ₦4,000 printing of price tags at the business centre, receipt attached. Thursday: ₦1,500 pure water and cleaning items, no receipt, logged. Friday: ₦5,000 recharge card and data for the business phone, receipt from the vendor. Saturday: ₦2,000 packaging bags from the market, no receipt, logged. Total spent: ₦18,000. Balance remaining: ₦22,000. Top-up on Monday morning: ₦18,000, bringing the float back to ₦40,000. Clean, traceable, and it took less than a minute per transaction to record.

## Step 5: Do a Weekly Count and Reconciliation

Once a week, at the same time every week, count the cash in the box. Add up all the vouchers for the week. The cash in the box plus the vouchers must equal the float. If your float is ₦40,000, you have ₦22,000 cash and ₦18,000 in vouchers, the system balances. If it does not balance, find out why before the next week starts.

Do this count yourself as the owner, or have someone other than the custodian do it. The person who holds the cash should not be the only person who checks it. This is not about distrust. It is about a system that works even when everyone is honest, because honest people also make mistakes and forget to record things.

Pick a fixed day and time. Monday morning before the shop opens works well for many businesses. Friday evening after close works for others. The day matters less than the consistency. Write the result of each weekly check in the logbook: date, cash counted, vouchers total, difference if any, and your signature. Over time this creates a history that shows whether spending is rising, which categories cost the most, and whether the float needs adjusting.

When you find a shortage, do not ignore it. A shortage of ₦500 might be a recording error. A shortage of ₦5,000 every week is a pattern. Ask the custodian, check the vouchers, and look for missing records. If shortages keep happening, change the custodian or tighten the rules. If you find a surplus, that usually means a payment was recorded twice or a voucher was counted wrongly. Fix the record.

Monthly, take the weekly totals and add them up by category. This is where petty cash control becomes real business intelligence. You might discover you spend ₦25,000 a month on transport alone, which tells you to negotiate a better delivery arrangement or raise your delivery fee. You might find ₦15,000 a month on printing, which tells you to buy a small printer. Without the records, you would never know.

## Step 6: Reduce Cash With Transfers and Mobile Money

The best way to control petty cash is to need less of it. Every payment you move from cash to transfer becomes automatically traceable. Your bank statement becomes a record. Paystack and Flutterwave transfers, bank transfers, and POS payments all leave evidence that cash does not.

In practice, this means changing small habits. Pay the printing shop by transfer instead of cash, even for ₦4,000. Buy fuel at a filling station that accepts transfer or POS instead of the roadside seller who only takes cash. Order packaging materials from a supplier on Jumia or Konga and pay online instead of sending someone to the market with cash. Recharge the business phone with a transfer or a data bundle bought through your bank app.

You will not eliminate cash completely. Bike fares, market purchases and small roadside expenses will stay cash for a long time in Nigeria. But if you can move half your petty cash spending to transfers, you have halved your leak risk and halved your record-keeping work.

## Common Petty Cash Mistakes Nigerian Businesses Make

The first mistake is having no float at all and just taking from the day's sales. When sales cash and petty cash are the same pile, you cannot know what you spent, what you earned, or whether the business made a profit. Separate them on day one.

The second mistake is topping up with random amounts. Someone puts ₦10,000 in today, ₦25,000 tomorrow, ₦5,000 next week. The box never balances because there is no fixed float to balance against. Fix the float and top up only to that amount.

The third mistake is no receipts and no logbook. "I will remember" is not a record. By Friday, nobody remembers what Tuesday's ₦3,000 was for. Record at the moment of payment or do not pay.

The fourth mistake is letting too many people access the box. If four staff members can open the drawer, the money has four possible explanations and zero accountability. One custodian, one key.

The fifth mistake is treating the owner's personal spending as business spending. Fuel for your personal car, lunch for your family, and a gift for a relative are not business expenses. Record them as drawings or pay for them from your personal account.

The sixth mistake is never checking. A system nobody checks is not a system. If you set up vouchers and a logbook but never count the box, the records become decoration. The weekly count is the part that makes everything else work.

## A Simple Petty Cash Policy You Can Copy

Write this on one page and keep it in the shop. Adapt the amounts to your business.

Petty cash float: ₦30,000, kept in a locked cash box. Custodian: one named person. Maximum single payment: ₦10,000. Allowed expenses: transport, fuel, printing, stationery, cleaning, refreshments, small repairs, packaging. Not allowed: personal expenses, staff loans, supplier payments, owner withdrawals without recording. Every payment needs a voucher with date, amount, purpose, receiver name, and signatures. Receipts required above ₦5,000. Cash counted and reconciled every Monday morning by the owner. Top-up equals the amount spent that week, bringing the float back to ₦30,000. Any shortage above ₦1,000 must be explained the same day.

That is the whole policy. It fits on one page, costs almost nothing to run, and it will save most small businesses tens of thousands of naira every month.

## What Good Petty Cash Control Gives You

The benefit is not just stopping theft, although that matters. The real benefit is knowing your numbers. When petty cash is recorded, you know your true costs. When you know your true costs, you can price properly. Many small businesses in Nigeria underprice because they do not count the small costs — the delivery fares, the printing, the fuel — that sit on top of the cost of goods. Petty cash records expose those costs.

Good records also make your business bankable. If you ever apply for a loan, a grant, or an investor's support, the first thing anyone asks for is your records. A business with a petty cash logbook, a sales record, and a monthly summary looks serious. A business with no records looks like a gamble.

Start this week. Buy a cash box and a voucher booklet, set your float, name your custodian, and write your one-page policy. Record every payment for one month. At the end of that month, add up what you spent and compare it with what you would have guessed. Most business owners are surprised. Then keep the system going, and let the small savings compound into real profit.
