Delivery can make or break a small business in Nigeria. A customer in Lekki orders a product from a seller in Surulere, the product itself is fairly priced, and then the delivery fee arrives — and the customer quietly disappears. Or the opposite happens: the seller charges too little for delivery, absorbs the difference out of their own pocket, and slowly loses money on every order without realising it. Both mistakes are common, and both are avoidable. Delivery pricing is not guesswork. It is a simple calculation built on your real costs, your location, and what your customers are willing to pay.

Many Nigerian small businesses sell mainly through WhatsApp, Instagram and Facebook, and deliver with dispatch riders or logistics companies. The delivery fee is often the last thing the seller thinks about, mentioned only when the customer asks, "How much is delivery to Ikeja?" By then a surprise fee feels like a price increase. This guide shows you how to work out what delivery really costs, set fees customers accept, and explain them clearly.

## Start by Knowing What Delivery Really Costs You

You cannot price delivery fairly until you know what it costs you. For most small businesses, the delivery cost is whatever you pay the rider or logistics company, plus the small hidden costs around it. Start by writing down what you actually pay. If you use dispatch riders in Lagos, note the charge for each area you deliver to regularly. If you use a logistics company, note their rates for different weight bands and destinations. If you deliver yourself or send a staff member, count the fuel, transport fare, and the time spent on the road, because time on the road is time not spent selling or packing orders.

Next, add the hidden costs that are easy to miss. Packaging protects your product on a bike in Lagos traffic, and good packaging costs money — boxes, bubble wrap, tape, and branded bags all add up. Failed deliveries cost money too: a rider goes to an address, nobody answers the phone, and you pay again for a second attempt. Returned orders cost money as well, because the rider still has to bring the goods back. Even the phone calls and WhatsApp messages to confirm an address take time. None of these costs is large on its own, but together they can turn a delivery fee that looked profitable into a loss.

Track one month of deliveries in a notebook or spreadsheet: each order, where it went, what the rider charged, what you charged the customer, and whether anything went wrong. At the end of the month, add up what you paid riders and what you collected. If you paid more than you collected, your fees are too low. If you collected far more, you may be overcharging. This record is the most honest guide to your pricing, because it is based on your real business.

## Price by Distance Zones, Not by Guesswork

The simplest fair system for most Nigerian small businesses is delivery zones. Divide the places you deliver to into a few clear groups based on distance from you, and set one fee for each group. For example, if your shop is in Lekki, your zones might be Lekki and its immediate neighbourhoods as Zone One, the wider Lagos Island and Victoria Island area as Zone Two, Ikeja and the Mainland as Zone Three, and places outside Lagos as Zone Four. A customer in your own neighbourhood pays the least, and a customer far away pays more, because the rider travels further and spends more time and fuel.

Zones work because they are easy to explain and remember. When a customer asks for the fee, you look at your zone list and answer immediately, without calling a rider for a quote. That speed matters on WhatsApp, where a customer who waits too long often moves on to another seller. Keep it to three or four zones — too many confuse customers.

Update your zones when your business changes. If you move your shop, your distances change, and your zones should change with them. If you start selling to customers in Abuja, Port Harcourt or Ibadan, add a zone for deliveries outside Lagos, and be clear that those take longer and cost more. State the zones in your WhatsApp catalogue, Instagram highlights and website, so the fee is never a surprise.

## Decide Who Pays: You, the Customer, or Both

There are three basic choices in delivery pricing: the customer pays the full delivery fee, you pay it, or you share it. Each choice sends a different message, and each one fits a different kind of business. When the customer pays the full fee, your profit on the product is protected, and the delivery cost is transparent. This is the most common choice for small businesses in Nigeria, especially for lower-priced products, because absorbing delivery on every small order would quickly eat your margin. The risk is that a high delivery fee can put customers off, especially if the fee is close to the price of the product itself.

When you pay the delivery fee yourself, usually called free delivery, the customer feels they are getting a good deal, and free delivery is a strong reason to order. But free delivery is never actually free — you pay for it out of your profit. It works best when the product price is high enough to absorb the cost, when the customer is ordering in bulk, or when you are running a promotion to win new customers. Free delivery on a small, low-margin product is a fast way to lose money, so check your numbers before you offer it.

The middle option is sharing the cost: you pay part of the delivery fee and the customer pays the rest, or you offer free delivery above a certain order value. For example, you might say delivery is free for orders above a stated amount, and charged at the normal rate below it. This encourages customers to buy more in one order, which is good for you and for them, because one larger delivery costs less per item than several small ones. Whichever option you choose, be consistent. Changing your delivery policy from customer to customer causes arguments and damages trust. Pick a policy, write it down, and apply it the same way to everyone.

## Use Real Examples to Test Your Prices

Examples make delivery pricing concrete, so let us work through a few. These are examples only, not fixed rates — your actual costs will depend on your location, your rider, and the size and weight of your goods. Suppose you sell a product for ₦15,000 and a rider charges ₦2,500 to deliver it within your area. If you charge the customer ₦2,500 for delivery, your product profit is untouched, and the customer pays a fair fee for a short trip. If you charged only ₦1,000, you would be paying ₦1,500 from your own pocket on every order, which adds up quickly over a month of orders.

Now suppose the same product goes to a customer much further away, and the rider charges ₦5,000 for the trip. If you charge the customer ₦5,000, the sale is fair, but the customer may feel the delivery fee is too high compared with the product price. Here you have a choice: you could absorb part of the fee, for example charging ₦3,500 and paying ₦1,500 yourself, or you could encourage the customer to order two items at once so the delivery fee is shared across a bigger order. There is no single right answer — it depends on how much profit you make on the product and how much you want that customer.

A third example is a bulky or heavy product. A small parcel and a large carton do not cost the same to deliver, even to the same address, because the rider may need a bigger bike, a van, or a second trip. If you sell heavy or fragile goods, ask your rider or logistics company how they charge for weight and size, and set your fees to match. A flat delivery fee for every product sounds simple, but it means you lose money on heavy orders and overcharge on light ones. A small difference in the fee for bulky items is fair, and customers understand it when you explain it plainly.

## Make the Fee Clear Before the Customer Orders

Most delivery arguments in Nigerian small businesses do not start with the fee itself. They start with surprise. The customer agreed to the product price, then discovered the delivery fee at the last minute, and felt tricked. The fix is simple: show the delivery fee early, in the same message as the price, every time. When a customer asks, "How much is this?" your answer should be the product price plus the delivery fee to their area, in one message. That way the customer decides with the full cost in front of them.

Put your delivery fees where customers can find them without asking. A short delivery price list in your WhatsApp catalogue, a saved highlight on Instagram called "Delivery", and a delivery section on your website all do the same job: they answer the question before it is asked. Include the zones, the fee for each zone, and how long delivery usually takes. Customers in Lagos expect same-day or next-day delivery within the city, and a few days for deliveries outside Lagos. If you promise a time, keep it, and if a delay happens, tell the customer before they ask you.

Be clear about what the fee covers. Does it cover delivery to the customer's door, or to a pickup point? Does it include packaging? What happens if the rider cannot reach the customer? Write these answers down once, in plain language, and send the same answers to everyone. A short delivery policy saved in your phone — a few lines you can copy and paste — saves time and prevents arguments. It also protects you: if a customer later says they did not know about the fee, you can show them where it was stated.

## Handle Failed Deliveries and Returns Without Losing Money

Failed deliveries are a normal part of selling online in Nigeria. A customer gives an incomplete address, their phone is switched off, they are not at home, or they change their mind when the rider arrives. Each failed delivery costs you the rider's fee, and sometimes a second fee to bring the goods back. The way to reduce this cost is confirmation. Before you dispatch an order, call or message the customer to confirm the full address, a landmark, and a working phone number. Confirm the total amount they will pay, including delivery, so there is no argument at the door.

Agree in advance who pays for a redelivery. A fair rule is that if the rider could not deliver because of the customer's fault — wrong address, phone off, nobody at home — the customer pays for the second attempt. If the failure was your fault or the rider's fault, you pay. State this rule in your delivery policy so it is not a surprise. Some sellers ask customers to pay the delivery fee in advance, especially for cash-on-delivery orders, because a customer who has already paid the delivery fee is far more likely to be available when the rider arrives. That small advance payment is a reasonable policy, and most serious customers accept it.

Returns need a clear rule too. If the product is faulty or wrong, you should collect it and replace it or refund the customer, and you should cover the return delivery yourself, because the mistake was yours. If the customer simply changed their mind, it is fair for them to pay the return delivery fee. Write this down before you need it, not after a dispute has started. A customer who knows your return rule in advance is far less likely to argue about it, and a clear rule protects your profit on the many orders that go smoothly.

## Review Your Delivery Fees Every Few Months

Delivery costs change. Fuel prices move, rider rates go up, and your business grows into new areas. A delivery fee that was fair six months ago may be too low today, and a fee that was fair last year may now be overcharging your customers. Review your fees every three months. Look at your delivery records, check what you paid riders against what you collected, and ask your riders whether their rates have changed. If your costs have gone up, increase your fees a little and tell your customers clearly — a small, explained increase is accepted far more easily than a sudden large one.

Watch your customers' reactions, too. If many customers abandon their orders when they hear the delivery fee, the fee may be too high for your product, or the way you present it may be the problem. Try offering free delivery above a minimum order value, or try bundling products so one delivery covers more items. If customers in a particular area keep ordering despite a higher fee, that fee is probably fair. Your customers' behaviour is honest feedback, and it is free.

Also consider pickup. Some customers would rather collect from your shop or a pickup point, which saves you the delivery cost entirely. Offer clear opening hours and a phone number to call on arrival, and give customers the choice between delivery and pickup.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How do I work out the right delivery fee for my business?**

Start with what you actually pay the rider or logistics company for each area, then add your packaging and failed-delivery costs. Set your fee to cover that total, and check it against one month of real delivery records. If you collect less than you pay, your fee is too low.

**Should I offer free delivery?**

Free delivery works when your product price is high enough to absorb the cost, or when you set a minimum order value, for example free delivery on orders above a stated amount. It is a strong way to win customers, but it is not free for you — you pay for it out of your profit, so check your numbers first.

**What is the best way to charge for delivery outside Lagos?**

Use a separate zone for interstate deliveries, with a higher fee and a longer delivery time, and use a logistics company that offers tracking. Confirm the full address and phone number before dispatch, and tell the customer the expected delivery day in advance.

**What should I do when a customer says the delivery fee is too high?**

Explain what the fee covers, offer the customer a choice — for example, ordering more items in one delivery, or collecting the order from your shop — and stay calm. Do not drop your fee for one customer and keep it for others, because that creates unfairness and arguments.

**Who pays when a delivery fails and the rider has to come back?**

Agree the rule in advance and write it in your delivery policy. A fair rule is that the customer pays for a redelivery when the failure was their fault, such as a wrong address or a switched-off phone, and you pay when the failure was yours or the rider's.
