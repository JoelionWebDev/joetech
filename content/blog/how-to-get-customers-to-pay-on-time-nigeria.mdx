## Why Late Payment Hurts Small Businesses in Nigeria

If you run a small business in Lagos, Abuja or Port Harcourt, you already know the feeling. You deliver the work, you send the goods, the customer is happy — and then the money does not come. You call. You send a WhatsApp message. You hear "I will send it tomorrow", "my bank app is misbehaving", or "send your account number again". Tomorrow becomes next week. Next week becomes next month. Meanwhile your rent is due, your supplier wants payment, your staff expect salary, and your dispatch rider needs fuel money.

Late payment is one of the biggest reasons small businesses in Nigeria struggle to grow. When money that should be in your account is sitting in a customer's pocket, you cannot restock, you cannot take on new jobs, and you cannot plan. You end up borrowing from your own savings or taking a loan to cover a gap that should never have existed. Some businesses even close, not because they had no customers, but because their customers did not pay on time.

The good news is that late payment is mostly a systems problem, not a character problem. Most customers are not trying to cheat you. They are busy, they forget, they are waiting for their own money to come in, or they were never clear about when payment was due in the first place. When you put clear terms, simple processes and polite follow-up in place, most people pay faster. This guide walks through exactly how to do that, step by step, with examples that work in the Nigerian market.

## Start Before the Job: Agree Payment Terms in Writing

The best time to prevent late payment is before you start the work, not after you have delivered it. Many Nigerian small businesses lose money because the payment conversation happens too late, or never happens at all. A customer says "do it, I will pay you", you do it, and then you discover that "I will pay you" meant something different to them than it meant to you.

Before you lift a finger, agree on four things in writing, even if it is just a WhatsApp message or a short document:

1. The total price, in naira, including delivery or any extra charges.
2. How much is paid upfront, and how much is paid on delivery or completion.
3. The exact due date for the balance.
4. What happens if payment is late.

Written agreement does not have to be a lawyer-drafted contract. A clear WhatsApp message that the customer replies to with "OK" or "Confirmed" is a good start. For bigger jobs, a simple one-page agreement or a proper invoice with terms printed on it is better. The point is that both sides can look back and see what was agreed. If you only discussed it on a phone call, memories will differ when money is involved.

### Example: A Simple Payment Agreement on WhatsApp

Imagine you run a catering business in Lekki and a customer orders food for 100 guests at ₦4,500 per person. That is ₦450,000 in total. Before you buy ingredients, send a message like this:

"Good afternoon, Mrs Adeyemi. Confirming your order: catering for 100 guests at ₦4,500 per person, total ₦450,000. Payment terms: 60% deposit of ₦270,000 to confirm the booking, balance of ₦180,000 due on the day of delivery before serving starts. Please confirm so we can lock in your date."

When she replies "Confirmed", you have a record. If she later says she thought she could pay after the event, you can politely point back to the message. That is not being difficult. That is running a professional business.

### Set Terms That Match Your Costs

Your payment terms should protect your cash flow. If you need to buy materials before you start, you need a deposit that covers those materials. A tailor in Surulere who needs ₦80,000 worth of fabric for a ₦150,000 outfit should not accept a ₦20,000 deposit and then fund the rest from personal savings. A web designer in Ikeja who will spend three weeks on a project should not wait until the very end to receive the first naira.

As a general guide, many Nigerian service businesses ask for 50% upfront and 50% on completion. For product businesses, payment before dispatch is normal, especially for custom or made-to-order items. For larger corporate jobs, a common structure is 40% on approval, 40% at a midpoint milestone, and 20% on delivery. Choose a structure that fits your business, then use it consistently. Customers respect consistency. When you change your terms for every customer, you train people to negotiate you down.

## Always Take a Deposit

A deposit does two things. First, it gives you money to start the work without dipping into your own pocket. Second, it commits the customer. A person who has paid ₦100,000 towards a ₦200,000 job is far more likely to pay the balance than a person who has paid nothing and can walk away without losing anything.

Some business owners in Nigeria feel shy about asking for a deposit. They worry the customer will go to a competitor who does not ask for one. In practice, serious customers expect a deposit. It signals that you are professional and that your time is valuable. The customers who refuse to pay any deposit at all are often the same customers who will delay the balance payment later. Losing them at the start is cheaper than chasing them for months after delivery.

### How Much Deposit Should You Ask For?

There is no single correct percentage, but these ranges work well for many Nigerian small businesses:

- **Custom or made-to-order products:** 50% to 70% upfront. If you are making Ankara outfits, custom furniture, cakes or branded items, the product cannot easily be sold to someone else if the customer disappears. Protect yourself.
- **Services with upfront costs:** 50% upfront. This covers designers, photographers, event planners, caterers and consultants who buy materials or book venues before the job.
- **Standard retail products:** Full payment before dispatch, unless you have a long trading history with the customer. For online sales, payment before delivery is the norm in Nigeria, and most buyers accept it when your business looks trustworthy.
- **Corporate clients:** 40% to 60% upfront, with the balance on delivery or within 7 to 14 days of invoicing, depending on what you agree.

If a customer genuinely cannot pay the full deposit at once, you can split it: for example, 30% to book and another 20% before production starts. The key is that some money moves before you commit your time and materials. Never start a custom job with zero payment unless you are prepared to absorb the full loss if the customer vanishes.

### Make Paying the Deposit Easy

Do not make the customer work hard to pay you. Share your bank account details clearly, including the bank name, account name and account number. If you accept transfers, POS, or mobile payment apps, say so upfront. Many customers delay simply because they do not have your account number saved, or they are unsure which account to pay into. Send the details in the same message as the invoice, not in a separate message they have to search for later.

If you run a business in Lagos and your customer is in Abuja, a bank transfer is usually the simplest route. If you deliver in person, a POS terminal or transfer on delivery can work, but confirm the method before you leave. Nothing is more frustrating than arriving with goods worth ₦250,000 and discovering the customer only has cash and you have no change, or their transfer fails because of network issues.

## Send Professional Invoices, Not Just a Chat Message

An invoice is more than a request for money. It is a document that tells the customer exactly what they are paying for, how much, and by when. Many small businesses in Nigeria skip invoices and just send a WhatsApp message saying "please pay ₦150,000". That is easy to ignore, easy to forget, and hard to track. A proper invoice feels official, and official things get paid faster.

Your invoice should include:

- Your business name, phone number and address.
- The customer's name.
- An invoice number and date.
- A clear description of the goods or services.
- The total amount, any deposit already paid, and the balance due.
- The due date, written clearly.
- Your bank details.
- A polite thank-you line.

You do not need expensive software to do this. A simple template in a document or spreadsheet, saved as a PDF, is enough. What matters is consistency: every job gets an invoice, every invoice has a due date, and every invoice is sent promptly. Send the invoice the same day you agree the job, or the same day you deliver, not a week later when the customer has moved on to other priorities.

### Use Due Dates That Create Urgency

"Pay soon" means nothing. "Pay by Friday, 28 November 2026" means something. Always put a specific date on your invoice. For most small business transactions in Nigeria, a due date of 3 to 7 days after delivery is reasonable for retail customers. For corporate clients, 7 to 14 days is common, but try to negotiate shorter terms when you can. A 30-day payment term might be standard for a large company, but it can kill a small supplier who needs that money to restock next week.

If you offer a small incentive for early payment, say so on the invoice. For example, a 2% discount for payment within 48 hours can motivate a customer who was going to delay. On a ₦500,000 invoice, that is a ₦10,000 discount — a small cost to you, but a real reason for the customer to pay now instead of later. Only offer early-payment discounts if your margins can absorb them. Do not discount yourself into a loss.

## Follow Up on Time, Every Time

Many business owners hate chasing payment. It feels awkward, and they worry about offending the customer. But follow-up is not begging. It is part of doing business. Companies that get paid on time are usually the ones that follow up on time. If you never remind a customer, you are telling them that your invoice is not urgent.

Set up a simple follow-up schedule and stick to it:

- **Two days before the due date:** Send a friendly reminder. "Good morning, Mr Okafor. Just a gentle reminder that invoice 042 for ₦180,000 is due on Friday. Thank you."
- **On the due date:** If payment has not arrived, send a short message. "Good afternoon. Your payment of ₦180,000 is due today. Please let me know once you have sent it. Thank you."
- **One day after the due date:** Call the customer. A phone call is harder to ignore than a message, and it lets you hear whether there is a genuine problem.
- **Three days after the due date:** Send a firmer message, restating the amount, the due date and the invoice number.
- **Seven days after the due date:** Escalate politely. Explain that you need the payment to continue serving them, and ask for a specific date when the money will arrive.

The tone matters. Start friendly and stay professional. Most late payers respond well to a calm, clear reminder. You are not accusing them of dishonesty. You are helping them remember an obligation they already agreed to. Keep every message short, include the amount and invoice number, and always end with a clear next step, such as "Please send payment today" or "Kindly confirm the date you will pay."

## Handle Common Excuses Calmly and Firmly

You will hear the same excuses again and again. Prepare your responses in advance so you are not caught off guard.

**"I will pay tomorrow."** Thank them and ask for a specific time. "Thank you. What time tomorrow should I expect it? I will check in the morning." Then follow up at that time. If tomorrow comes and there is no payment, call again. A vague promise without a follow-up is worth very little.

**"My bank app is not working."** This happens genuinely in Nigeria, but it is also a convenient excuse. Offer an alternative: a different bank account, a POS payment, or a transfer from another account. If they say they will try again later, agree on a time and follow up.

**"Send your account number again."** Send it immediately, in the same conversation, and ask them to confirm once sent. Do not let this become a reason for a three-day delay.

**"I am waiting for my own customer to pay me."** Empathise, but remind them that your agreement was with them, not with their customer. You can offer a short extension if the relationship is strong, but agree on a firm new date in writing. "I understand. Can we agree on Wednesday, 3 December as the new payment date?" If they miss that date too, you need a firmer conversation.

**"The goods are not what I expected."** If there is a genuine quality issue, address it quickly. Offer to fix, replace or adjust what you can. But do not let a small complaint become a reason to withhold the full payment indefinitely. Agree on what will be fixed, when, and what portion of the payment is due now.

## Keep the Relationship While Being Firm

Getting paid on time is not just about money. It is about respect. When you are clear, professional and consistent, most customers will respect your terms. When you are vague, apologetic and inconsistent, some customers will take advantage, even without meaning to.

You can be firm and friendly at the same time. "I value our relationship, and I want to keep working with you. To do that, I need payments to come in on the agreed dates so I can keep delivering quality work." That sentence is honest, respectful and clear. It tells the customer that late payment has consequences, without threatening them.

If a customer repeatedly pays late despite reminders, you need to change the terms for that customer. That might mean asking for full payment upfront next time, or a larger deposit, or declining credit entirely. You are not punishing them. You are adjusting to the reality of how they pay. A customer who always pays 30 days late is not a bad person, but they are a customer who needs upfront terms.

### When to Pause Work or Stop Supply

This is the hardest decision for a small business owner. You have a customer who owes you ₦300,000 and wants another job worth ₦200,000. Do you do it? In most cases, the answer should be no — not until the old balance is settled, or at least a payment plan is agreed and the first instalment is paid. Continuing to work for a customer who has not paid for the last job is how small debts become large debts.

Say it politely: "I would love to take on this new job. Before we start, let us clear the outstanding ₦300,000 from the last project so we can start fresh." Most customers will understand. If they do not, that tells you something important about how they value your work.

## Conclusion: Clear Terms, Consistent Follow-Up, Healthier Business

Getting customers to pay on time in Nigeria is not about being aggressive or chasing people. It is about being clear before the job, professional during the job, and consistent after the job. Agree terms in writing. Take a deposit. Send a proper invoice with a real due date. Follow up on schedule. Keep a tracker. Offer easy payment options. And when a customer repeatedly pays late, adjust their terms instead of absorbing the cost yourself.

Your work has value. Start with one change today — a written agreement for your next job, or a reminder to an overdue customer — and build from there.

## Checklist: Getting Paid on Time

- Agree price, deposit, balance and due date in writing before starting work.
- Take a deposit that covers your upfront costs.
- Send a professional invoice with a clear due date and your bank details.
- Follow up two days before the due date, on the due date, and after it if needed.
- Keep a payment tracker and review it weekly.
- Offer more than one payment method.
- Handle excuses calmly, agree a new firm date, and follow up on it.
- Adjust terms for repeat late payers — larger deposit or payment upfront.
- Pause new work for customers with an overdue balance until it is settled.
