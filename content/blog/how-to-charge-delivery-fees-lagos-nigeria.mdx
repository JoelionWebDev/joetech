If you sell anything in Lagos — food, clothes, cosmetics, electronics, cakes — you have had this conversation. A customer in Lekki orders a ₦12,000 dress from your shop in Ikeja, and then asks, "How much is delivery?" You say ₦3,500, and they reply, "Ah, that is too much, I will think about it." And they never come back. Or you say ₦1,500 to keep the customer, pay the rider ₦3,000 from your own pocket, and realise at the end of the month that delivery has eaten your profit.

Delivery is one of the hardest parts of running a small business in Lagos. The city is large, traffic is unpredictable, fuel is expensive, and customers compare your delivery fee with the free delivery they see on big platforms. But delivery is also a cost you cannot ignore. If you absorb it silently, you lose money. If you charge it badly, you lose customers. This guide explains how delivery pricing works in Lagos, how to calculate what delivery really costs you, and how to charge fees that customers accept without killing your profit.

## Why Delivery Fees Cause So Many Lost Sales

The first reason is sticker shock. A customer who has already decided to buy your product sees the delivery fee as a separate cost, and it feels like an extra charge on top of what they already agreed to pay. A ₦3,000 delivery fee on a ₦8,000 order feels like a 37 percent increase, even though the fee has nothing to do with your product price. The smaller the order, the bigger the delivery fee looks in comparison.

The second reason is comparison. Customers in Lagos have been trained by large platforms to expect cheap or free delivery, and they do not see the difference between a company with its own fleet of vans and a small business that hires a dispatch rider for each order. When a customer says "Jumia delivers for ₦1,000", they are comparing your single-order rider cost with a platform that delivers hundreds of parcels on one route.

The third reason is trust. Many Lagos customers have been disappointed by delivery before — late riders, damaged goods, wrong items, or riders who demanded extra money on arrival. When they see a high delivery fee, they are not just judging the price; they are judging whether the delivery will actually be worth it.

Understanding these three reasons changes how you handle delivery. You cannot just announce a fee and expect customers to accept it. You have to set the fee fairly, explain it clearly, and deliver well enough that the customer feels the fee was worth paying.

## What Delivery Really Costs You in Lagos

Before you can set a fair fee, you need to know what delivery actually costs your business. Most small business owners in Lagos underestimate this, because they only count the rider's fee and forget the rest.

The rider's fee is the biggest part. As a rough guide, a dispatch rider in Lagos currently charges around ₦1,500 to ₦2,500 for a short trip within the same area, ₦2,500 to ₦4,500 for a medium trip across nearby areas, and ₦4,000 to ₦8,000 or more for a long trip — for example, from Ikeja to Lekki, Ajah, or Sangotedo. Prices rise when it rains, when there is heavy traffic, and during festive periods. These are guide prices, not fixed rates, and you should confirm current rates with your own riders, because fuel prices and rider charges change often.

But the rider's fee is not the only cost. There is also your time — the time you spend packaging the order, calling the rider, tracking the delivery, and handling the customer on WhatsApp while the rider is on the road. There is packaging: nylon bags, boxes, bubble wrap, and tape, which can cost ₦200 to ₦1,000 per order depending on the product. There is the cost of failed deliveries — the customer is not at home, the phone is switched off, or the customer changes their mind — and you still pay the rider. And there is the cost of damaged or lost items, which you may have to replace.

Add all of these together, and the true cost of delivering one order in Lagos is usually 20 to 40 percent higher than the rider's fee alone. If the rider charges ₦3,000, your real delivery cost is probably ₦3,600 to ₦4,200. If you charge the customer only ₦3,000, you are losing money on every order without realising it.

## How to Calculate Your Delivery Fee

The simplest way to set delivery fees is by zone. Divide Lagos into zones based on distance from your shop, and set a fixed fee for each zone. For example, if your shop is in Ikeja, your zones could be: Zone 1, within Ikeja and nearby areas like Ogba, Allen, and Maryland — ₦1,500. Zone 2, Surulere, Yaba, and Ikorodu Road — ₦2,500. Zone 3, Lekki Phase 1, Victoria Island, and Ikoyi — ₦4,000. Zone 4, Ajah, Sangotedo, and Epe — ₦5,500 or more. Adjust the zones and prices to your own location, but the principle is the same: the further the delivery, the higher the fee, and the fee is fixed and predictable.

The second method is to charge the actual rider cost plus a small handling fee. When a customer orders, you check the rider's price for that trip, add ₦300 to ₦500 for packaging and handling, and quote that as the delivery fee. This is fair and transparent, and it protects you when rider prices rise. The disadvantage is that the fee changes from order to order, which some customers find confusing.

The third method is to build delivery into your product price and offer "free delivery". This works well if your average order is large enough to absorb the cost. For example, if you sell a cake for ₦25,000 and delivery costs ₦3,000, you can price the cake at ₦28,000 and advertise free delivery. Customers love the words "free delivery", even though they are paying for it in the product price. The risk is that your product price starts to look expensive compared with competitors who show a lower price and charge delivery separately.

Whichever method you choose, write it down and use it consistently. A delivery price list that you can send to a customer on WhatsApp — with zones, fees, and delivery times — makes you look professional and stops the back-and-forth negotiation on every order. It also protects you from customers who try to haggle the delivery fee down to nothing.

## Free Delivery: When It Works and When It Destroys Your Profit

Free delivery is the most powerful sales tool a small business has, and also the most dangerous. It works when your profit margin is large enough to absorb the delivery cost and when it increases your average order value. For example, if you offer free delivery on orders above ₦30,000, customers who were going to spend ₦22,000 will often add another item to reach the threshold. Your delivery cost is covered by the extra sale, and the customer feels they got a deal.

Free delivery destroys your profit when you offer it on small orders, when your margins are thin, or when you offer it to far locations. If you sell an item for ₦5,000 with a profit of ₦1,500, and delivery to Ajah costs ₦5,000, you have lost ₦3,500 on that sale. That is not marketing — that is giving your money away.

The rule is simple: only offer free delivery when you have done the maths and the numbers work. Set a minimum order value that is high enough to cover the delivery cost and still leave you a profit. Limit free delivery to nearby zones if long-distance delivery is expensive. And set a clear time limit — "Free delivery this weekend only" — so it feels like a promotion, not a permanent promise you cannot afford.

If you run free delivery as a promotion, track the results. Did your sales increase enough to cover the delivery costs? Did customers order more, or did the same customers just pay less? If the promotion does not increase your total profit, stop it. Free delivery should make you more money, not less.

## How to Tell Customers About Delivery Fees

When you tell a customer the delivery fee matters as much as the fee itself. The worst approach is to let the customer order, get excited, and then surprise them with a delivery fee at the end. That feels like a trick, and many customers will cancel.

The best approach is to state the delivery fee early. Put it on your Instagram bio or your WhatsApp Business catalogue, so customers see it before they order. When a customer asks for a price, give the product price and the delivery fee together: "The dress is ₦15,000, and delivery to Lekki is ₦3,500, so the total is ₦18,500." Customers accept a total price far more easily than a product price followed by a surprise fee.

Explain the fee in one short sentence when a customer questions it. "Delivery to Lekki costs ₦3,500 because it is a long trip from our shop in Ikeja, and the rider brings it to your door." That is honest and reasonable, and most customers will accept it. Do not argue, do not apologise excessively, and do not immediately drop the price — that teaches customers that your fees are negotiable and that complaining works.

If a customer genuinely cannot afford the delivery fee, offer alternatives instead of a discount. They can pick up from your shop, meet the rider at a closer location, or combine their order with a friend's order in the same area so they share one delivery fee. These options keep your price intact while still helping the customer.

## Working with Dispatch Riders

Your rider is your delivery service, and the quality of your rider decides the quality of your customer's experience. A rude rider, a late rider, or a rider who damages goods will cost you customers, even though the problem was not yours.

Find two or three reliable riders and build a relationship with them, rather than using a different random rider for every order. A rider who knows your shop, your packaging, and your customers will be faster and more careful. Pay them promptly, treat them well, and give them clear instructions: the customer's name, phone number, address, the exact amount to collect if it is payment on delivery, and what to do if the customer is not available.

Consider using a dispatch company for regular deliveries. Companies like Gokada, Kwik Delivery, and other Lagos logistics platforms offer rider services with tracking, insurance, and fixed price lists. They are often more expensive than an independent rider, but they are more reliable for high-value orders and for customers who want to track their delivery.

Always agree the rider's fee before the rider picks up the order. Confirm the pickup address, the delivery address, and the fee on WhatsApp so there is a written record. If the rider tries to increase the price midway, you have the agreed price in writing. And always get the customer's phone number right — a wrong digit means a failed delivery and a wasted rider fee.

For payment on delivery, be careful. The rider collects cash or confirms a transfer, and then brings the money to you. Use riders you trust for this, because the money passes through their hands. For high-value orders, ask the customer to pay by transfer before the rider leaves, and confirm the payment in your bank app before you release the goods.

## Delivery Options That Reduce Your Costs

You do not have to deliver every order by rider. There are cheaper options that many Lagos businesses use. The first is pickup. Let customers collect from your shop, your home, or a pickup point in a busy area. Many customers prefer pickup because it is free and they can inspect the item. Offer a small discount for pickup if you want to encourage it.

The second is batch delivery. Instead of sending a rider for each order, collect all the orders for one area and send them together on one trip, once or twice a day. If you have five orders going to Lekki, one rider trip that costs ₦4,000 can deliver all five, instead of five separate trips costing ₦20,000. Tell customers your dispatch times — for example, "Orders placed before 12 noon go out at 2 pm" — so they know when to expect delivery.

The third is a delivery day for far areas. If you get occasional orders from Epe, Ikorodu, or Badagry, do not send a rider for each one. Choose one day a week for deliveries to that area, and tell customers their order will arrive on that day. Most customers will wait a few days if they know the date in advance.

The fourth is partnering with another business. If your shop is in Ikeja and a friend's shop is in Lekki, you can hold each other's stock or act as a pickup point for each other. This is common among Lagos traders, and it can cut delivery costs sharply for both businesses.

## Handling Delivery Problems

Things will go wrong. A rider will be late, a cake will be damaged, a customer will not be at home. How you handle these problems decides whether you keep the customer or lose them.

When a delivery is late, tell the customer before they ask. A WhatsApp message that says "Your rider is on the way, traffic is heavy on Lekki-Epe Expressway, he should reach you in 30 minutes" is far better than silence. Customers forgive delays when they are informed; they do not forgive being ignored.

When goods are damaged in transit, apologise, replace or refund quickly, and do not argue about whose fault it was. The customer bought from you, not from the rider, so the responsibility is yours. Then take it up with the rider or the dispatch company afterwards. A customer who gets a fast replacement after a problem often becomes more loyal than a customer who never had a problem at all.

When a customer is not available, agree a clear policy in advance and state it when they order. For example: "If you are not available when the rider arrives, the rider will wait 10 minutes and then return the item. A second delivery attempt costs the delivery fee again." This is fair, and it protects you from paying for the same delivery twice.

Keep a simple record of delivery problems — the date, the order, what went wrong, and what it cost you. If the same rider is late every week, replace the rider. If deliveries to one area keep failing, change your approach for that area. The record helps you fix the real problem instead of blaming bad luck.

## Your Delivery Pricing Checklist

Before you set or change your delivery fees, run through this list. Have you calculated your true delivery cost, including packaging and failed deliveries? Have you set delivery zones with fixed fees that a customer can see before ordering? Have you set a minimum order value for free delivery, and checked that the maths works? Do you have two or three reliable riders, and have you agreed their fees in advance? Do you state the delivery fee early, with the product price, so there are no surprises? And do you have a plan for late, damaged, or failed deliveries? If the answer to all of these is yes, your delivery system is ready. Charge what delivery really costs, explain it clearly, deliver well, and your customers will pay the fee — because a business that delivers reliably is worth paying for.
