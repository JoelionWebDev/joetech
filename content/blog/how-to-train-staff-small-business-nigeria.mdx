Most small businesses in Nigeria do not struggle because the owner lacks a good product. They struggle because the people serving customers every day were never properly trained.

You hire a shop assistant in Lekki, a sales girl in Ikeja, an apprentice in Onitsha, or a delivery rider in Port Harcourt. You show them where things are kept, tell them to be careful with money, and hope they learn the rest on their own. Then a customer complains, money goes missing, stock does not balance, or the worker quits after three weeks — and you start again from zero.

Training does not have to mean a formal classroom, a consultant, or a big budget. For a business with two, five, or ten staff, training means a clear first day, simple written steps, regular practice, honest conversations, and small rewards for good work.

## Why Training Matters More Than Hiring

Many owners spend weeks looking for the perfect worker, then spend almost no time training the person they finally hire. That is backwards. A willing person with good training will often do better than an experienced person who was never told how your business works.

Good training protects your money and your reputation. When staff know how to record sales, give change, issue receipts, and count stock, mistakes and theft become harder to hide. Customers also remember how they were greeted, how quickly they were served, and whether a complaint was handled politely. One rude reply can lose a customer who would have returned every week.

Training also saves you time and helps you keep good people. If you train someone properly once, you will not need to answer the same question ten times a day. Staff stay longer when they feel they are learning, know what is expected, and feel noticed. Start with the work that affects customers and money first, then add the rest over time.

## Before You Hire: Decide What the Job Really Is

Training starts before the first interview. If you do not know exactly what you want the person to do, you cannot train them to do it well. Write down the job in plain language. A shop assistant in a provision store in Surulere might open the shop by 8:00 am, arrange goods, greet customers, record every sale, give correct change, and close by 8:00 pm.

Be specific about working hours, off days, salary, and any allowance for transport or feeding. Many small disputes start from unclear expectations. If transport is ₦1,000 per day, say so. If salary is paid on the 28th of every month, say so. If the shop closes on Sundays, say so. Write it down and give the worker a copy.

When you interview, look beyond certificates. For most small business roles, attitude, honesty, willingness to learn, and basic communication matter more than a long CV. Ask practical questions: What would you do if a customer says the price is too high? Keep the process simple: a short job description, a one-page form, and a trial of one or two weeks. ## Your First-Day Onboarding Checklist

The first day sets the tone. A new hire who is welcomed, shown around, and given clear instructions will settle faster than one who is left to guess. Walk them through the shop or office. Show them where stock is kept, where the toilet is, where to keep their bag, and where to eat during break time.

Explain the business in simple terms. What do you sell? Who are your customers? What makes your business different? Go through the basic rules on day one: opening and closing times, dress code, phone use, break time, and what happens if they are late. Be firm but respectful, and apply the rules the same way to everyone.

Give the new hire a simple written welcome sheet of one or two pages, with duties, working hours, pay date, who to report to, and the main rules. If the worker cannot read English well, explain it verbally in a language they understand and ask them to repeat the key points back. Assign a buddy for the first week — your most reliable staff member — to show them how work is done and answer small questions.

**First-day checklist:**

- Welcome the new hire and introduce them to the team
- Show them the shop, stockroom, toilet, and break area
- Explain what the business sells and who the customers are
- Go through duties, hours, pay date, and basic rules
- Give them a written welcome sheet and assign a buddy
- Show them how to record a sale and give change
- End the day with a short check-in: what was confusing, and what help do they need?

## Write Simple Standard Procedures

In a small business, most daily work is repeated: opening the shop, serving a customer, taking an order, packing goods, recording a sale, and closing for the day. When those steps live only in your head, every new worker has to learn them by watching and guessing. When you write them down, anyone can follow them.

These written steps are called standard operating procedures, or SOPs. An SOP is simply a short list of steps for doing one task the same way every time. Start with the five tasks that matter most: opening, serving a customer, handling money, closing, and handling a complaint. Write each one in short, numbered steps, using simple language. A procedure for closing a shop in Abuja might be:

1. Count the cash in the drawer and write the total in the sales book.
2. Count the remaining stock for fast-selling items and note any shortages.
3. Switch off fans, lights, and the freezer if required.
4. Lock the back door first, then the front door.
5. Send the day's sales total to the owner on WhatsApp before 9:00 pm.

Keep each procedure to one page. A two-minute phone video showing how to arrange a shelf, pack an order, or use the POS machine can be more useful than a long document. Put procedures where staff can see them: a laminated sheet near the till, a notebook at the counter, or a pinned message in the staff WhatsApp group.

## On-the-Job Training That Actually Works

Most small business training happens on the job, not in a classroom. That is fine, as long as it is deliberate. Watching alone is not training. The best method is simple: show, practise, correct, and repeat.

First, show the task slowly and explain each step as you do it. If you are teaching a new sales girl how to attend to a customer, demonstrate the greeting, how to ask what the customer needs, and how to close the sale politely. Next, let the worker try it while you watch. Let them finish, then give feedback, starting with what they did well.

Then let them repeat the task until it becomes natural. Train in short sessions of fifteen to thirty minutes a day, which is more effective than a three-hour lecture once a month. Pick one skill per session. Use real examples from your own business, and cross-train where possible, so another person can step in when someone is absent.

## Teaching Customer Service the Nigerian Way

Customer service in Nigeria is personal. Customers remember how they were treated more than they remember the price. A customer who feels respected will return, recommend you to friends, and forgive a small mistake. A customer who feels ignored or insulted will not come back, and will tell others not to come either.

Start with greeting, listening, and respect. Teach staff to greet every customer who walks in, even if they are busy: "Good morning, welcome" or "Good afternoon, how can I help you?" Teach them to use polite titles where appropriate — "Sir", "Ma", "Aunty", "Uncle" — especially with older customers. Teach them to listen fully before replying, and never to argue with a customer in public.

Teach staff how to handle prices. Many Nigerian customers will negotiate, especially in markets and fashion stores. Train staff on the lowest price they can accept without calling you, and when they must call you first. You might allow a discount of ₦500 on items above ₦10,000, but no discount on smaller items without your approval. Clear limits prevent staff from giving away your profit to please a customer.

Teach staff how to handle complaints: listen without interrupting, apologise, offer a solution, and follow up. If a customer bought spoiled food, the solution might be a replacement or a refund. Teach staff to use WhatsApp professionally: reply promptly, confirm orders in writing, and avoid slang. Remember regular customers, too. "Welcome back, Sir. The usual?" tells a customer they matter.

## Handling Money and Stock Honestly

Money and stock are where many small businesses lose the most. Training staff to handle both honestly and carefully is not optional. Start with clear rules: every sale must be recorded, every payment must have a receipt where possible, and every expense must be noted. No sale should be done "off the books".

Teach staff how to count cash correctly and give correct change, counting it back out loud: "You gave me ₦10,000, your total is ₦7,500, your change is ₦2,500." Teach them to use the POS machine and to confirm transfer alerts before releasing goods, especially for large amounts. If a transfer is delayed, they should call you first.

Teach basic stock control: how to count stock, record what was sold, and report what is running low. At the end of each day, the cash should match the sales recorded. Do daily and weekly checks yourself. When a mistake happens, handle it fairly: an honest error is a training moment. Pay staff on time, too.

## Giving Feedback Without Quarrels

Many Nigerian business owners avoid feedback until a problem becomes serious, then explode. Others criticise staff in front of customers, which embarrasses the worker and damages the business. Both approaches fail. Feedback works best when it is regular, specific, private, and balanced.

Give feedback quickly, while the event is still fresh. If a worker handled a customer well, say so the same day. If they made a mistake, correct it the same day, privately. Be specific. "You are lazy" is not feedback. "You arrived 30 minutes late on Monday and Tuesday, and the shop opened late" is feedback. "You did well" is vague. "You handled that angry customer calmly and offered a replacement without arguing" is useful praise.

Use a simple structure: what happened, what was good, what needs to change, and what to do next time. For example: "Yesterday, a customer asked for a size 42 shoe and you told them we did not have it. You were polite, which was good. Next time, please check the stockroom first." Correct in private, praise in public.

Hold a short weekly check-in of ten to fifteen minutes. Ask each worker what went well, what was difficult, and what help they need. Listen more than you talk. Keep a simple note of important feedback conversations, especially warnings about money, lateness, or rudeness to customers.

## Keeping Staff Motivated on a Small Budget

You do not need a big salary budget to motivate staff, but you do need to treat people well. Motivation in a small business comes from respect, fairness, learning, and small rewards that show appreciation. Start with respect. Greet your staff by name. Say please and thank you. Do not shout or insult. A worker who feels respected will work harder than one who feels humiliated, even if the pay is the same.

Be fair. Apply rules equally. If one worker is punished for lateness and another is not, the team will notice. If you promise a bonus or an off day, keep the promise. Recognise good work. A simple "Thank you, you did well today" costs nothing and means a lot.

Offer small, practical benefits where you can: a transport allowance, a meal during work hours, or help with a small emergency. If you cannot increase salary now, be honest about it. Help staff learn new skills, celebrate small wins together, and ask staff what motivates them.

## When Staff Leave: Handovers and Lessons

Staff will leave. An apprentice will finish learning and start their own shop. A sales girl will get a better offer. A rider will move to another city. This is normal in small business. What matters is how you handle it.

When a worker tells you they are leaving, stay calm and professional. Thank them for their work. Ask when their last day will be and agree on a handover period, ideally one to two weeks. During that time, the leaving worker should train their replacement, hand over keys, passwords, customer contacts, and any pending orders, and help update the stock records.

Use a simple handover checklist: keys and shop access, cash and POS details, pending customer orders, supplier contacts, stock records, and any passwords for business accounts. Change passwords for business WhatsApp, email, and social media accounts after the worker leaves, especially if they had access to customer data or money.

Do an exit conversation. Ask why they are leaving, what they liked about working with you, and what could be better. Do not burn bridges. A former worker who leaves on good terms may return later or refer a good replacement. After a departure, review your training and keep records of former staff.

## A Simple 30-Day Training Plan

You do not need to do everything in this guide at once. Use this 30-day plan to start small and build up.

**Week 1: Settle in.** Complete the first-day onboarding. Assign a buddy. Teach opening, greeting customers, recording sales, and closing. End each day with a short check-in.

**Week 2: Build core skills.** Train on customer service, handling prices and negotiation, and using the POS machine and transfer confirmations. Practise with role play. Write down the first three procedures: opening, serving a customer, and closing.

**Week 3: Money and stock.** Train on counting cash, giving change, daily stock counting, and reporting shortages. Start daily cash checks and weekly stock checks. Hold your first weekly feedback meeting.

**Week 4: Feedback and motivation.** Review progress with each worker. Praise what has improved. Correct what still needs work. Ask staff what they need to learn next. Set one small team goal for the following month, such as reducing customer complaints or improving on-time opening.

After 30 days, repeat the cycle. Training is not a one-time event. It is a habit. A short training session each week, a clear procedure for each task, and regular feedback will keep your team improving month after month.

**Your starter checklist:**

- Write the job description before you hire
- Prepare a first-day welcome sheet and buddy system
- Write procedures for opening, serving, money, and closing
- Train in short daily sessions: show, practise, correct, repeat
- Teach greeting, listening, price limits, and complaint handling
- Record every sale, count cash daily, and check stock weekly
- Give feedback quickly, privately for corrections and publicly for praise
- Recognise good work and pay on time
- Plan handovers before a worker leaves

## Conclusion

Training your staff in a small Nigerian business does not require a training school, a consultant, or a large budget. It requires clarity, consistency, and care. Tell people what to do, show them how to do it, let them practise, correct them kindly, and recognise them when they do well.

Start with the basics: a good first day, simple written steps, honest money handling, polite customer service, and regular feedback. Add to it week by week. The best time to start training is with your next hire. The second-best time is with the staff you already have, starting tomorrow morning.
