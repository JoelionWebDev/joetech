Chidi is a graphic designer in Enugu. He spends three days designing a logo, delivers beautiful work, and charges ₦15,000 because he is afraid the client will say no to a higher price. The client pays happily, uses the logo everywhere, and tells friends that Chidi is "cheap". Meanwhile, Chidi cannot pay his rent, because after data, electricity, and transport, the ₦15,000 is gone. Chidi is not a bad designer. He is a good designer with a pricing problem.

Pricing is the hardest part of freelancing in Nigeria. Charge too little and you work long hours for money that does not cover your costs. Charge too much and clients walk away, especially when cheaper options are a search away. The answer is not to guess. It is to calculate what your work is worth, understand what your market will pay, and present your price with confidence.

This guide explains how to price your services as a freelancer in Nigeria. You will learn how to work out your minimum rate, how to research what others charge, how to quote for projects, how to handle clients who want discounts, and how to raise your prices over time without losing the clients you already have.

## Why Most Nigerian Freelancers Undercharge

The first reason freelancers undercharge is fear. Many new freelancers believe that a low price is the only way to win a client, especially when they have no reviews or portfolio yet. So they charge whatever the client suggests, or they copy the lowest price they see online. That fear is understandable, but it creates a trap: low prices attract clients who do not value the work, and those clients are the hardest to please and the slowest to pay.

The second reason is that freelancers forget their real costs. A salary earner gets a fixed pay cheque, but a freelancer must pay for everything out of each project: data, electricity, a laptop, software, transport to meet clients, and the days spent looking for work. A designer who charges ₦20,000 for a job that takes three days is earning less than ₦7,000 a day before costs.

The third reason is comparing yourself to the wrong people. A beginner who compares their price to a freelancer with five years of experience and international clients will feel they must charge almost nothing. But the right comparison is with freelancers at your level, serving your market, with your skills.

The fourth reason is the Nigerian market itself. Many local clients are used to low prices and will push hard for a discount. Some will say "my budget is ₦5,000" for work worth ₦50,000. That pressure is real, but it is not a reason to accept every low offer. A freelancer who says yes to every low price ends up with a full diary and an empty bank account.

## How to Calculate Your Minimum Rate

Before you quote any price, work out the lowest rate you can accept without losing money. Start with your monthly living costs: rent, food, transport, data, electricity, and any money you send to family. Add your business costs: software subscriptions, internet, a phone, and repairs or replacement for your laptop. Add them together to get your monthly target. For example, if your living costs are ₦150,000 and your business costs are ₦50,000, your monthly target is ₦200,000.

Next, work out how many hours you can actually bill in a month. You may work eight hours a day, but not all of those hours are paid work. Some hours go to finding clients, replying to messages, learning, and admin. A realistic figure is about 100 billable hours a month, roughly five hours a day for twenty working days. Divide your monthly target by your billable hours. ₦200,000 divided by 100 hours gives a minimum rate of ₦2,000 per hour.

Use that minimum rate as your floor, not your price. Your actual price should be higher, because you also need money for savings, for slow months, and for growth. A good rule is to add at least 30 percent on top of your minimum rate to cover savings and slow periods. In our example, that takes the rate to about ₦2,600 per hour. Write your floor rate down and keep it where you can see it.

## How to Research What Others Charge

Your floor rate tells you what you must charge. Market research tells you what clients will pay. Start by looking at what freelancers with similar skills charge on platforms like Upwork, Fiverr, and LinkedIn. Search for your skill, filter for freelancers in Nigeria or Africa, and note the range of their rates. Do not copy the highest rate or the lowest rate. Look at the middle of the range for someone at your experience level.

Also research your local market. Ask fellow freelancers in Lagos, Abuja, or Port Harcourt what they charge for similar work, and pay attention to what clients in your niche actually pay. A logo designer serving small businesses in Ibadan will charge differently from one serving tech startups in Lekki.

Look at what agencies charge too. A digital agency in Lagos might charge ₦250,000 for a website that a freelancer could build for ₦120,000. That gap is your advantage: you can offer similar quality at a lower price than an agency, while still earning far more than the cheapest freelancer.

Keep a simple note of the rates you find, and update it every few months. Rates change as the naira moves, as demand shifts, and as your skills grow. A freelancer who researched prices a year ago and never checked again is probably undercharging today.

## How to Quote for a Project

There are two main ways to charge: by the hour or by the project. Hourly pricing works well for ongoing work, such as managing a client's social media or doing regular design work. Project pricing works better for defined jobs, such as a logo, a website, or a video. Most Nigerian clients prefer project pricing, because they want to know the total cost before they agree.

To quote a project, break the work into steps and estimate the time each step takes. For a website, the steps might be planning, design, development, content, and testing. Multiply the total hours by your hourly rate, then add the cost of anything you must buy for the job, such as stock photos, a domain name, or a plugin. That total is your project price. Add a small margin for revisions and unexpected problems, because they always happen.

Always put your quote in writing, with a clear list of what is included. For example: "Logo design, ₦60,000. Includes three concepts, two revision rounds, and final files in PNG and PDF. Extra revisions: ₦10,000 each. Payment: 50 percent upfront, 50 percent on delivery." A written quote protects you from scope creep, which is when a client keeps adding work without adding money.

Send the quote quickly. A client who waits three days for a price has usually asked someone else by then. Aim to send a quote within 24 hours of the enquiry, and follow up once if you hear nothing. Never chase a client more than twice. If they do not reply, move on to the next prospect.

## How to Handle Discounts and Difficult Clients

Every freelancer meets the client who says "Give me a discount" or "My budget is small, but there will be more work later". The first rule is to never cut your price without cutting the work. If a client cannot afford your full price, offer a smaller version of the job for a smaller fee. A website with five pages instead of ten, a logo with one concept instead of three, or a video of 30 seconds instead of 60. That way you protect your rate and the client still gets value.

Be polite but firm. A good reply is: "Thank you for your message. My price for this work is ₦80,000, which covers the design, two revision rounds, and the final files. If that is above your budget, I can offer a simpler version for ₦50,000. Let me know which works for you." That reply shows confidence, gives the client a choice, and never apologises for the price. Clients respect freelancers who know their worth.

Beware of the promise of future work. A client who says "Do this one cheap and I will bring you ten more jobs" rarely brings ten more jobs. Judge each job on its own price. If the job is worth doing at a lower rate because it will build your portfolio or teach you a new skill, accept it as an investment. But do not accept a low price because of a promise you cannot enforce.

Finally, know when to walk away. If a client argues about every naira, demands unlimited revisions, or refuses to pay a deposit, that client will cost you more than they pay you. A polite "I am not the right fit for this project, but I wish you well" frees your time for a better client.

## How to Get Paid on Time

A good price means nothing if the money never arrives. The first rule is to always take a deposit before you start work. For most projects, 50 percent upfront is fair. For small jobs, you can ask for full payment upfront. A client who will not pay a deposit is a client who may not pay at all. State your payment terms in your quote and in your invoice, and do not start work until the deposit is in your account.

Use a professional invoice for every job. An invoice should show your business name, the client's name, a description of the work, the amount, your account details, and the payment deadline. You can create one with a free tool or a simple template. A clear invoice makes you look professional, and it gives the client everything they need to pay you without asking questions.

Offer payment methods that are easy for Nigerian clients: bank transfer, Paystack or Flutterwave payment links, and OPay or PalmPay. The easier it is to pay you, the faster you get paid. After you deliver the work, send the final invoice the same day.

Keep a record of every payment: the client, the project, the amount, the date paid, and any balance owed. A simple spreadsheet is enough. That record tells you who your best clients are, who pays late, and how much you actually earned in a month.

## How to Raise Your Prices Over Time

Your prices should grow as your skills and demand grow. A good time to raise your prices is when you have more work than you can handle, when your skills have clearly improved, or when your costs have gone up. If you are turning clients away because you are fully booked, your price is too low. Raising it will reduce the number of enquiries, but the ones that remain will be better clients.

Raise your prices gradually, not all at once. A 10 to 20 percent increase is reasonable, and you can explain it simply: "My rates have increased to reflect the quality of work and the demand for my time." Apply the new rate to new clients first. For existing clients, give them notice: "From next month, my rate for this service will be ₦90,000 instead of ₦75,000. Thank you for your continued business." Most good clients will accept a fair increase, especially if your work has been excellent.

Keep improving the value behind your price. Learn a new skill, speed up your delivery, or add a small extra that costs you little but matters to the client. A writer who also formats the document, a designer who also provides social media sizes, or a developer who also sets up basic SEO is worth more than one who does only the basics. When your value grows, your price can grow with it.

Review your prices every three months. Check your costs, check the market, and check how many enquiries you are getting. If you are getting very few enquiries, your price may be too high for your market. If you are getting many enquiries and closing most of them, your price is probably too low.

## Common Mistakes Freelancers Make with Pricing

The first mistake is charging by guessing instead of calculating. A freelancer who picks a price because it "sounds right" has no idea whether the job will make or lose money. Always calculate your floor rate first, then set your price above it. The second mistake is copying the cheapest freelancer's price. That freelancer may be desperate, inexperienced, or losing money without knowing it. Copy their marketing, not their price.

The third mistake is giving unlimited revisions. Every extra round of changes costs you time, and time is your product. Limit revisions in your quote, usually two rounds, and charge for more. The fourth mistake is working without a deposit. A freelancer who starts work on a promise is lending the client money and time with no guarantee of repayment. No deposit, no work.

The fifth mistake is never raising prices. A freelancer who charges the same rate for three years is earning less every year, because costs rise and the naira loses value. Review your rates every three months and raise them when your skills, demand, or costs justify it. The sixth mistake is apologising for your price. A price stated with confidence sounds fair. The same price stated with "sorry, I hope this is okay" sounds expensive. State your price, explain what it includes, and stop talking.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much should a beginner freelancer charge in Nigeria?

Start with your floor rate, which is your monthly costs divided by your billable hours, then add a margin for savings and slow months. For many beginners, that lands between ₦2,000 and ₦5,000 per hour, depending on the skill and the market. Research what freelancers at your level charge, price near the middle of that range, and raise your rate as your portfolio and reviews grow.

### Should I charge per hour or per project?

Charge per project for defined jobs like a logo, a website, or a video, because Nigerian clients prefer to know the total cost upfront. Charge per hour for ongoing work like social media management or regular design, where the amount of work changes each month. Whichever you choose, calculate the project price from your hourly rate so you know the job is profitable.

### What should I do when a client says my price is too high?

Stay calm and explain what the price includes. Offer a smaller version of the job for a lower fee, rather than cutting your price for the same work. If the client still cannot afford you, thank them politely and move on. A client who cannot afford your rate today may return later, and a client who respects your price is more likely to pay on time.

### How do I price work for international clients?

International clients usually pay in dollars, and their budgets are often higher than local budgets. Research the going rate for your skill on platforms like Upwork and Fiverr, and quote in dollars. Use a payment method like Payoneer or a domiciliary account to receive the money, and confirm the exchange rate and any fees before you agree on a price.

### How often should I raise my freelance rates?

Review your rates every three months and raise them when your skills improve, your demand grows, or your costs rise. A 10 to 20 percent increase at a time is reasonable. Apply the new rate to new clients first, and give existing clients notice before you change their price. If you are fully booked and turning work away, that is a clear sign your price is too low.

## What to Do Next

1. Add up your monthly living and business costs to find your monthly target.
2. Divide that target by your realistic billable hours to find your minimum hourly rate.
3. Research what freelancers at your level charge on Upwork, Fiverr, and in your local market.
4. Set your rates above your floor rate, and write them down where you can see them.
5. Create a simple quote template that lists what is included, the price, and your payment terms.
6. Start asking for a 50 percent deposit before every project, without exception.
7. Review your rates every three months, and raise them by 10 to 20 percent when your skills or demand grow.
8. Practise saying your price out loud with confidence, and stop apologising for it.
