Walk into any busy street in Lagos, Onitsha, Kano, or Port Harcourt and you will see the same thing: shops, kiosks, restaurants, bars, and event centres selling cold soft drinks all day long. Nigerians drink a lot of soft drinks, and somebody has to move those drinks from the bottling plants to the thousands of small retailers who sell them one bottle at a time. That somebody is the distributor — and it is a business you can build with a depot, a truck, and a reliable supply arrangement.

Soft drinks distribution is a volume business. The profit on a single crate is small, so you make your money by moving many crates every day, keeping your costs tight, and building a loyal network of retailers who order from you again and again. This guide walks you through the whole process, step by step, with realistic Nigerian figures and the practical details most guides leave out.

## 1. Understand How the Business Actually Works

A soft drinks distributor sits in the middle of the supply chain. You buy drinks in bulk from a bottling company or a major dealer, store them in a depot, and sell them in crates to retailers — supermarkets, provision stores, kiosks, bars, restaurants, hotels, and event planners. The retailer sells to the final consumer, bottle by bottle.

There are two common ways to enter the business:

**Become an authorised dealer or sub-dealer.** You apply to a bottling company such as Nigerian Bottling Company (Coca-Cola), Seven-Up Bottling Company (Pepsi, Mirinda, 7Up), or a local producer, and they supply you directly at dealer prices. Authorised dealers get the best prices and sometimes support like branded fridges or signage, but the requirements are higher: a registered business, a suitable warehouse, and the ability to buy a minimum number of crates regularly.

**Buy from a major dealer and resell.** If you cannot yet meet a bottler's requirements, you buy from an established dealer at a slightly higher price and sell to retailers in your area. Your margins are thinner, but the entry barrier is much lower, and many successful distributors started this way before graduating to direct dealership.

Either way, the daily work is the same: receive stock, store it properly, take orders from retailers, load trucks, deliver, collect payment, and repeat. The business rewards consistency more than cleverness. Retailers want a supplier who shows up on time, every time, with the drinks they asked for.

## 2. Research Your Area Before Spending a Kobo

Before you rent a shop or buy a single crate, spend a week or two studying the area where you plan to operate. This research costs nothing but saves you from expensive mistakes.

Walk around your target neighbourhood and count the potential customers: provision stores, supermarkets, kiosks, restaurants, fast-food joints, bars, hotels, schools, churches, mosques, and event centres. Talk to shop owners. Ask them who supplies their drinks, how often they order, which brands sell fastest, and what frustrates them about their current supplier. You will hear the same complaints often: late deliveries, out-of-stock popular flavours, damaged crates, and suppliers who disappear during festive periods when demand is highest.

Check the competition. How many distributors already serve the area? What are their prices per crate? Do they deliver or do retailers come to collect? A neighbourhood with three strong distributors is a harder place to start than a growing area with one unreliable supplier.

Write down your findings in a simple notebook or spreadsheet: number of retailers, their average weekly orders, the brands they stock, and the prices they pay. This becomes the foundation of your business plan, and it tells you honestly whether the area can support another distributor.

## 3. Register Your Business and Sort Out the Paperwork

Soft drinks distribution is a business that suppliers, landlords, and banks will take more seriously if it is properly registered. At minimum, register a business name with the Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC). A business name registration is affordable and gives you a certificate you can show to bottling companies and banks. If you plan to grow big, consider registering a limited liability company instead, but a business name is enough to start.

Open a business bank account in the business name. Suppliers and serious retailers prefer bank transfers to cash, and a business account keeps your money organised. Get a Tax Identification Number (TIN) from the Federal Inland Revenue Service — it is free, and you will need it for formal dealings.

If you want a direct dealership, the bottling company will ask for your CAC certificate, a verifiable business address, and sometimes bank statements showing you can handle regular bulk purchases. Keep your documents in a folder, both physical and digital, so you are ready when the opportunity comes.

## 4. Work Out Your Startup Capital Honestly

How much you need depends on your scale, but here is a realistic breakdown for a modest start in a city like Lagos, Ibadan, or Enugu:

- **Depot rent:** A warehouse or large shop in a accessible area costs roughly ₦500,000 to ₦1,500,000 per year depending on location. You will usually pay a year in advance.
- **Initial stock:** A starting stock of 500 to 1,000 crates across popular brands can cost between ₦1,500,000 and ₦4,000,000, depending on the brands and sizes. A crate of 35cl PET bottles typically costs a dealer somewhere in the range of ₦3,000 to ₦5,000, and prices change with the economy, so confirm current prices with suppliers before you budget.
- **Truck or van:** A fairly used delivery truck costs roughly ₦3,000,000 to ₦8,000,000. A smaller van is cheaper but carries fewer crates. If buying outright is too much, many distributors start by hiring a truck per trip or buying on hire purchase.
- **Crates, pallets, and handling equipment:** Budget ₦100,000 to ₦300,000 for extra crates, a hand trolley, and stacking pallets.
- **Running costs:** Fuel, driver salary, loaders' wages, and phone/communication can run ₦150,000 to ₦400,000 a month.

All together, a modest start is realistically in the range of ₦5,000,000 to ₦12,000,000. If that sounds like a lot, remember you can start smaller: a rented shop, stock from a major dealer, and a hired truck, with total capital of ₦2,000,000 to ₦3,500,000. What matters is starting with enough stock to serve your retailers reliably. Running out of the fast-selling brands in your first month is the fastest way to lose customers.

## 5. Get Your Supply: Dealership or Major Dealer

Your supply arrangement decides your profit margin, so treat it as the most important decision in the business.

To apply for a dealership, visit the nearest depot or regional office of the bottling company and ask for the trade or sales department. They will explain their requirements, which usually include a registered business, a warehouse of a minimum size, a delivery vehicle, and a commitment to buy a minimum quantity each month. Some companies also require a refundable deposit for crates and bottles. Be honest about your capacity; it is better to start as a sub-dealer with a smaller commitment than to promise volumes you cannot move and lose the dealership.

If you are buying from a major dealer, negotiate like your profit depends on it — because it does. Ask for the best price per crate, whether there are discounts for bulk purchases, and what the payment terms are. Some dealers give a few days of credit to reliable buyers, but never build your business on credit you cannot repay. Pay promptly, and your supplier will prioritise you when stock is scarce.

Stock the brands that sell in your area. In most Nigerian markets, Coca-Cola, Pepsi, Fanta, Sprite, Mirinda, 7Up, and Chivita or Hollandia juices are the staples. Add energy drinks and bottled water if your retailers ask for them — water is a high-volume, low-margin product that keeps your trucks busy and your retailers loyal. Do not overstock slow-moving flavours; they tie up your cash.

## 6. Set Up Your Depot Properly

Your depot is more than a store — it is the engine of the business. Location matters more than size. Choose a place that trucks can enter and turn around easily, close to your retailers, with good road access even in the rainy season. A depot on a narrow street where a truck cannot pass will cost you deliveries every day.

Inside, organise the space so that stock moves fast. Keep fast-selling brands near the loading area. Stack crates neatly on pallets, not directly on the floor, to avoid damage from water. Label sections by brand and size so loaders can find stock quickly. Keep a clear path for the truck to load and unload.

Security is essential. Drinks are easy to steal and easy to sell. Use a solid gate, good locks, and, if possible, a security guard or a trusted staff member who stays at the depot. Do a daily stock count: count what came in, what went out, and what remains. Any gap between your records and the physical stock is a problem to investigate immediately, not next week.

Keep the depot clean and dry. Crates stored in a damp place grow mould, and retailers will reject dirty crates. Sweep daily, fix roof leaks immediately, and never store drinks in direct sunlight for long periods.

## 7. Price Your Crates and Protect Your Margin

Your selling price per crate is your buying price plus your margin, minus any costs you absorb. A typical distributor margin is slim — often ₦100 to ₦400 per crate depending on the brand and the competition in your area. That sounds small until you multiply it by 200 crates a day.

Set your prices by checking what other distributors charge in your area, then position yourself carefully. Undercutting everyone may win customers quickly, but it also destroys your margin and starts a price war you cannot win.

Decide your payment policy early and stick to it. Cash on delivery is the safest. If you give credit to retailers, limit it to a few trusted customers, set a clear repayment day, and track every naira. Many distributors have collapsed because of unpaid retailer debts, not because of low sales. A retailer who owes you for three weeks is not a customer — they are a risk.

Watch your costs weekly. Fuel prices change, and a delivery route that was profitable last month may lose money this month if you are driving too far for too few crates. Group deliveries by area: serve all the retailers in one neighbourhood on the same trip. This is called route planning, and it is one of the biggest differences between a distributor who makes money and one who burns it on diesel.

## 8. Win Retailers and Keep Them Ordering

Your first customers are the hardest to get. Start with retailers near your depot — the provision stores, kiosks, and bars within a short drive. Visit them in person, introduce yourself, and leave your phone number. Offer a fair price and, most importantly, deliver on time for the first few orders. A retailer who orders 10 crates on Monday and gets them on Monday will order 20 crates next week.

Give new retailers a reason to switch. That could be free delivery for orders above a certain number of crates, a small discount on their first order, or simply being available when their current supplier fails them. Festive periods are your best opening: when other suppliers run out of stock, a distributor who has stock wins customers for life.

Use WhatsApp to take orders. Most retailers already use it, and it is faster than phone calls. Ask them to send their orders the evening before delivery day so you can plan your loading and routes. Send a short message each morning confirming the day's deliveries. Over time, build a simple list of your regular customers with their usual orders — this helps you predict demand and stock up before they even call.

Handle complaints fast. A damaged crate, a wrong flavour, or a late delivery should be fixed the same day. Apologise, replace the stock, and move on. Retailers forgive mistakes; they do not forgive being ignored.

## 9. Run the Daily Operation Like a System

A distribution business lives or dies on its daily routine. Build a simple one and follow it every day:

**Morning (6:00–8:00 AM):** Check the orders that came in on WhatsApp overnight. Confirm stock levels. Load the truck according to the delivery route — the last stop's crates go in first, the first stop's crates go in last.

**Midday (8:00 AM–2:00 PM):** Deliveries. The driver follows the route while a loader or sales rep handles the crates and collects payment. Record every delivery and every payment immediately, not from memory later.

**Afternoon (2:00–5:00 PM):** Receive new stock from your supplier if a delivery is due. Count it, record it, and stack it. Take orders for the next day. Follow up on any retailer who is owing.

Keep a simple record book or a spreadsheet: date, retailer, crates delivered, amount, payment received, balance. If you can afford it, use a basic inventory app on your phone. The format does not matter; the habit does. When you know your numbers, you know whether the business is growing or quietly bleeding money.

## 10. Hire the Right People and Manage Them Well

You cannot run this business alone for long. At minimum, you will need a driver and one or two loaders. As you grow, add a sales representative who visits retailers and takes orders, and a storekeeper who manages the depot.

Hire people you can trust, and verify them. A driver handles your truck and your cash, so check references and agree on clear rules: no unauthorised stops, no personal use of the truck, and all money collected must be handed over the same day. Pay fairly and on time. A driver who is paid late will find other ways to make money from your business.

As the owner, your job is to manage the money, the supplier relationship, and the big customers. Visit retailers yourself regularly, even after you have staff. It keeps you close to the market and lets you hear problems before they become losses.

## 11. Common Problems and How to Handle Them

**Price increases from bottlers.** They happen regularly. When your cost goes up, inform your retailers early and adjust your price. Retailers accept increases better when they hear it from you first, not when they discover it at delivery.

**Truck breakdowns.** A truck that breaks down on a delivery day costs you money and reputation. Service it regularly, keep spare tyres and basic parts, and have the phone number of a reliable mechanic. Budget for maintenance monthly, not only when something breaks.

**Theft and pilfering.** Count stock daily, limit the number of people who handle cash, and do surprise checks. If you suspect theft, investigate quietly and act on evidence, not rumours.

**Cash flow pressure.** Suppliers want payment before delivery, while retailers sometimes want credit. This gap can squeeze you. Keep your prices realistic, collect debts aggressively, and avoid borrowing at high interest to buy stock unless you have done the maths and the margin clearly covers the cost.

## 12. Growing Beyond Your First Depot

Once your depot runs smoothly and you are moving a steady volume of crates every day, you can grow in a few directions. Add more brands or products — bottled water, energy drinks, and juices — to the same retailers. Open a second depot in a neighbouring area once the first one is profitable and well managed. Or negotiate a direct dealership with a bottler, which improves your margin and your status in the market.

## Conclusion

Soft drinks distribution is not a get-rich-quick business, but it is a real one. It works in every Nigerian city, it serves a demand that never disappears, and it rewards the distributor who is reliable, organised, and honest about money. Start with research, secure your supply, set up a depot you can manage, and win retailers one delivery at a time.

Your first month will teach you more than any guide can. Track your numbers, protect your margins, collect your debts, and let your reputation do the marketing. Do that consistently, and the crates will keep moving — and so will your business.
