Adaeze sells skincare from a small shop in Lekki Phase 1. Her vitamin C serum costs ₦18,500. One evening, after a long day of walk-ins and WhatsApp messages, she opened ChatGPT and typed: "write a caption for my cream."

What came back was fine, and useless. "Glow like never before!" "Your skin deserves the best!" Lines that could belong to any cream, sold by anyone, anywhere. Adaeze posted one anyway. It got eleven likes, nine of them from her friends.

Three weeks later she tried again, but this time she wrote a different kind of prompt. She told the AI who it was writing as, what the product was, who buys it, what the caption had to do, how it should sound, and she pasted in a caption she had written herself that had actually sold bottles. The result read like her shop talked. That post brought four orders before noon.

## The jump most people never make

Almost everyone who uses AI for business in Nigeria starts the same way. A short instruction, typed in a hurry, between customers. "Write a product description for Ankara dress." "Reply to this customer." "Give me ten business ideas." The answers come back fast, they read smoothly, and they feel like progress. For a week or two, they are.

Then the sameness sets in. The descriptions could describe any dress. The replies sound like a call centre in another country. The business owner concludes that AI is good for rough drafts and not much else, and goes back to doing everything by hand at midnight.

That conclusion is wrong, but it is understandable. A one-line prompt hands the AI a job with most of the information missing, and the AI does the only honest thing it can: it guesses. It guesses your customer, your price, your tone, your market. The way out is not a better tool or a paid plan. It is giving the AI the information it is missing, in layers, before it starts writing.

## The five layers, built on one real job

A layered prompt has five parts. Role, task, context, format, examples. You do not need to memorise the names. You need to see what each one does, so here they are, built up one at a time around a single real job: a product description for an Ankara two-piece that Chiamaka, a fashion designer in Yaba, sells for ₦38,000.

The role tells the AI whose brain to borrow. "You are a fashion copywriter who writes product descriptions for Nigerian ready-to-wear brands." Without it, the AI writes like a textbook.

The task is the job itself, stated precisely. "Write a product description for an Ankara two-piece set." The task is the one line most people already write. It is necessary, but on its own it is only about a fifth of the prompt.

The context is everything the AI cannot know unless you tell it. The fabric is Ankara from a Balogun market supplier, the set is a cropped top and wide-leg trousers, sizes run 8 to 16, it suits owambe and office Fridays, delivery goes nationwide, the buyer is a working woman in Lagos aged 25 to 40 who wants to look sharp without spending ₦80,000. Context is where most weak prompts die. People assume the AI knows their business. It does not.

The format is the shape of the answer. "Three short paragraphs, then a bullet list of four details: fabric, fit, sizes, delivery. Under 150 words. End with a one-line call to action that mentions the ₦38,000 price." Format is what turns a draft into something you can paste.

The examples layer is the one almost nobody uses, and it is the most powerful. Paste in a description you wrote yourself that sold well, or one from a brand you admire, and add one sentence: "Match this energy and this level of detail, but write new copy." Examples teach the AI your taste faster than any adjective. "Warm and confident" means ten different things to ten people. A sample removes the argument entirely.

Put together, Chiamaka's prompt reads like a proper brief, because that is what it is:

> "You are a fashion copywriter for Nigerian ready-to-wear brands. Write a product description for an Ankara two-piece set: cropped top and wide-leg trousers, sizes 8 to 16, ₦38,000, delivery nationwide. The buyer is a working woman in Lagos, 25 to 40, who wants to look sharp at owambe and office Fridays without spending ₦80,000. Three short paragraphs, then four bullets covering fabric, fit, sizes and delivery. Under 150 words, plain language, no hype words. Here is a description I wrote that sold well — match this energy, write new copy: [paste]."

## Before and after: three business jobs

Layering is easier to trust once you have seen it work on jobs you actually do. Here are three, each with the prompt most people write and the layered version that replaces it.

**A product description.** Tunde sells refurbished laptops from a shop in Computer Village, Ikeja. His weak prompt: "Write a product description for HP EliteBook." The strong version names the role (a tech salesperson writing for Nigerian buyers), the task, the context (HP EliteBook 840 G8, 11th-gen i5, 16GB RAM, 512GB SSD, ₦485,000, 3-month warranty, buyer is a final-year student or young professional who needs a machine that will survive four years of heavy use), the format (a short hook, five bullet specs, one line on warranty, one line on delivery to Lagos and Abuja), and an example (a description of his that moved ten units in a week). The first version produces a spec sheet any site could carry. The second answers the exact fear of the buyer standing in Computer Village with ₦485,000 in his account: will this laptop last?

**A customer reply.** Mrs. Okafor runs a catering business in Surulere. A bride's mother messages on WhatsApp at 9pm: the small chops for Saturday's engagement arrived two hours late last week, and she wants to know what will be different this time. The weak prompt: "Help me reply to an angry customer." The layered version sets the role (a customer service lead for a Lagos catering company), the task (draft a WhatsApp reply), the context (the delay was a Third Mainland Bridge hold-up, the order was 500 pieces of small chops for 120 guests, the client has booked three events this year and is worth about ₦900,000 in repeat business), the format (under 70 words, warm, no excuses that sound like blame, one concrete fix, one goodwill offer), and an example (a reply Mrs. Okafor sent in June that kept a client). The weak prompt gives you a corporate apology. The layered one gives you a message that keeps a ₦900,000 relationship.

**A caption.** A boutique hotel in Lekki Phase 1 wants to fill rooms on slow Sunday nights. The weak prompt: "Write an Instagram caption for a hotel." The layered version sets the role (a social media copywriter for Lagos hospitality), the task (a caption for a Sunday staycation offer), the context (₦85,000 per night instead of ₦120,000, pool and gym included, target is couples in Lekki and Ikoyi who want a night away without leaving Lagos), the format (a hook in the first line, two sentences about the offer, a call to action that says "DM us to book"), and an example (the hotel's best-performing caption from July). One of these captions could advertise any hotel on earth. The other could only advertise this one, on this Sunday, at this price.

## The layer people skip, and why it costs them

If you take one thing from this article, take the examples layer. Role, task, context and format are common sense once someone points them out. Examples feel optional, like extra homework. They are not. They are the difference between telling the AI what good looks like and hoping it guesses.

The practical rule is simple. Before you ask the AI to write anything that carries your brand — a description, a reply, a caption, a proposal — find one example of that thing done the way you like it. It can be your own past work, a competitor's post you admired, or a message a customer complimented. Paste it in, add one line about what to copy from it, and watch the output change.

## Mistakes even advanced users still make

Layered prompts fix most problems, but a few mistakes survive the upgrade. They are worth naming, because they are the mistakes people make after they already think they are good at this.

The first is overloading. Once someone learns the five layers, the temptation is to load every job into a single message: a product description, five captions and a pricing strategy, all at once. The AI handles the first item carefully and rushes the rest. One prompt, one job.

The second is copying prompts from social media without adapting them. A prompt that went viral on X was written for someone else's business, audience and price. Paste it as-is and the output will be shaped around a stranger's context. Borrow the structure, then replace every fact with your own.

The third is letting the AI invent your numbers. This one costs real money. An AI asked to write a price list, a quote or a proposal will fill in prices that sound plausible. A "₦150,000" it invented can end up in front of a client before you notice. The fix is a line in every prompt that touches money: "Use only the prices I give you. Do not invent figures."

The fourth is never testing the prompt twice. A layered prompt is a small system, and systems need a trial run. Run it once, read the output with a cold eye, and fix the layer that failed. If the tone is wrong, the role or the example needs work. If the facts are wrong, the context is thin. If the shape is wrong, the format is vague. Each failure points at exactly one layer, which is what makes layered prompts so fixable.

Save the prompts that produced work you actually used, in one document, with a line each about what they are for. Thirty proven prompts in a shared folder is a small operations manual, and it is worth more than any single clever line you typed once and forgot.

## A two-week way to actually learn this

Reading about layering will not change your prompts. Using it on real work will. The fastest way is a small daily habit, not a course.

In the first week, pick one real job each day and write the layered version before you send it. Monday is a product description. Tuesday is a customer reply. Wednesday is a caption. Thursday is an email to a supplier. Friday is a price quote. Do not aim for perfect. Aim for all five layers present, even roughly. By Friday you will feel the difference in the output, and you will have five saved prompts.

In the second week, take the best three of those five and push them further — a sharper example, a tighter format — and keep the better version.
