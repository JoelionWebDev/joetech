Every day in Nigeria, millions of people wear shoes to work, to school, to church, to weddings, and to the market. Shoes wear out. Soles come loose, heels break, zips fail, leather cracks, and stitching comes apart. Most people do not throw those shoes away. They repair them. That is why shoe repair, the work of the cobbler, remains a steady business in every Nigerian town, even as fashion and technology change.

A shoe repair business is one of the lowest-cost businesses you can start in Nigeria. You do not need a shop in a big plaza to begin. You do not need a university degree. You need a skill, a small set of tools, a good location, and the discipline to do honest work and keep your customers' shoes safe. Many cobblers in Lagos, Abuja, Enugu, and Ibadan started with less than ₦100,000 and built a steady daily income.

This guide explains how to start a shoe repair business in Nigeria step by step. You will learn the skills you need, the tools to buy, how much capital to prepare, where to set up, how to price your work, and how to find and keep customers. If you are looking for a practical business you can start this month, this is a strong option.

## Why Shoe Repair Is a Good Business in Nigeria

The demand for shoe repair is constant. Nigerians love good shoes, and good shoes are expensive. A quality pair of leather shoes can cost ₦25,000 to ₦80,000 or more. A pair of school shoes for a child costs ₦8,000 to ₦20,000. When a ₦40,000 shoe develops a loose sole, the owner will gladly pay ₦2,000 to ₦5,000 to repair it rather than buy a new pair. That simple maths keeps cobblers busy all year.

Shoe repair is also a business that does not depend on the season alone. There are busy periods, such as December before Christmas, Easter, wedding season, and the start of a new school term, when parents repair school shoes and adults repair party shoes. But there is work every week, because people wear shoes every day. A cobbler near a busy market, a bus stop, an office area, or a residential estate will see customers daily.

Another advantage is low competition from big companies. No large company dominates shoe repair in Nigeria. It is a trade run by individuals and small workshops. That means a skilled, honest, and reliable cobbler can build a strong local reputation quickly. Customers return to the cobbler they trust, and they recommend that cobbler to friends, family, and colleagues.

The business also has room to grow. You can start under a tree or in a small kiosk, then move to a proper shop. You can add shoe cleaning, shoe polishing, bag repair, belt repair, and sales of shoe polish, laces, insoles, and socks. You can later train apprentices, open a second spot, or offer pickup and delivery on WhatsApp. The starting point is small, but the path forward is real.

## Learn the Skill First

Do not start a shoe repair business without the skill. Customers will judge you by the first shoe you repair for them. If the sole comes off again in two days, they will not return, and they will warn others. If the repair is neat, strong, and clean, they will return and bring more shoes.

If you do not already know how to repair shoes, learn from a master cobbler. In most Nigerian towns, you can find an experienced cobbler who takes apprentices. Apprenticeship usually lasts from six months to two years, depending on how fast you learn and how often you practise. You may pay a fee, or you may work for the master while you learn. Agree the terms clearly before you start: how long, what you will learn, what you will pay, and what hours you will work.

If you already have basic skills, keep improving. Watch experienced cobblers at work in markets like Balogun, Onitsha Main Market, or Wuse Market. Watch videos on your phone when data allows, but practise with real shoes. Buy old shoes from the market for ₦500 or ₦1,000 and practise repairs on them until your work is neat. Your hands must learn the work, not only your eyes.

## Tools, Materials, and Startup Capital

You do not need expensive machines to start, but you need the right tools. Poor tools produce poor work and waste your time. Buy good quality tools, even if you buy them gradually.

Your basic tool kit should include a cobbler's hammer, a sharp cutting knife, a pair of pliers, needles and strong thread, an awl for making holes in leather, a rasp or file for smoothing soles, scissors, a measuring tape, brushes for polish, and a sturdy workbench or table and a chair. You will also need a clamp or vice to hold shoes firmly while glue dries. A small sewing machine for heavy leather is useful later, but it is not compulsory at the start. Many successful cobblers in Nigeria work by hand for years.

Your materials will include strong shoe glue, polish, thread, replacement soles, heel tips, insoles, laces, and zips. Buy from leather traders in markets such as Idumota in Lagos or the large leather markets in Onitsha and Aba, and compare prices from two or three suppliers.

For startup capital, a roadside start with basic tools and materials can cost ₦50,000 to ₦120,000. A small kiosk can cost ₦150,000 to ₦350,000, and a proper small shop with a sewing machine can cost ₦400,000 to ₦800,000. Do not borrow heavily to start. Begin with what you can afford, earn, and reinvest. Buy tools first, materials second, and decoration last.

You will also need a phone with WhatsApp. Most of your customers will contact you on WhatsApp, send photos of damaged shoes, and ask for prices. Keep your phone charged. In areas with unstable power, a small power bank is a wise investment. Save customers' numbers, keep a simple record of jobs, and reply quickly.

## Choose a Good Location

Location can make or break a shoe repair business. The best location is where many people pass daily with shoes on their feet. Look for busy markets, bus stops, school gates, office areas, residential estates, and shopping areas.

A spot near a market entrance is often excellent. Traders, buyers, and workers pass there all day. A spot near a bus stop in places like Ikeja, Surulere, Wuse, or GRA Enugu can also work well, because commuters notice the cobbler on their way home and bring shoes the next day. A location inside or near a residential estate is good for repeat customers, because families will bring school shoes, work shoes, and party shoes regularly.

Before you settle, observe the area for a few days. Count how many people pass in the morning and evening. Check whether another cobbler already works there. If one cobbler already serves a small area, you may need to choose a different spot or offer something extra, such as faster service or home pickup. Do not set up directly beside another cobbler and try to take his customers by cutting prices. That creates conflict and a price war that hurts both of you.

If you rent a shop or kiosk, agree the rent and terms in writing, even if it is a simple note signed by both sides. Know how much rent is, when it is due, and whether you can put up a signboard. In Lagos, a small kiosk in a busy area can cost ₦10,000 to ₦50,000 a month, while a shop in a plaza can cost much more. Start with a rent you can pay from your weekly income, not from hope. If you work from a roadside spot, confirm with the local council or market association whether you need a permit or a small daily fee, so you are not harassed later.

## How to Price Your Work in Nigeria

Pricing is where many new cobblers make mistakes. Price too low, and you work hard for little money and cannot buy materials. Price too high, and customers will go elsewhere. The goal is a fair price that covers your materials, your time, and a profit, and that customers accept happily.

Start by knowing your costs. For each job, add the cost of materials, such as glue, thread, sole, or heel tip, and the time it takes. A simple sole glue job that takes 20 minutes and uses ₦300 of glue should not be priced the same as a full sole replacement that takes two hours and uses ₦2,500 of materials.

As a guide for 2026, many Nigerian cobblers charge roughly these amounts, though prices vary by city and by the quality of the shoe. Simple gluing of a loose sole: ₦1,000 to ₦2,500. Stitching a sole: ₦1,500 to ₦3,500. Full sole replacement: ₦4,000 to ₦10,000, depending on the sole type. Heel replacement or heel tip: ₦1,500 to ₦4,000. Zip replacement on boots: ₦2,500 to ₦6,000. Shoe polishing and cleaning: ₦500 to ₦2,000. Leather dyeing and restoration: ₦3,000 to ₦8,000. School shoe repair: ₦1,000 to ₦3,000.

Adjust these prices to your area. Prices in Lekki or Abuja may be higher than in a smaller town, because rent and customer expectations are higher. Do not copy another cobbler's price blindly. Know your costs, watch what customers in your area accept, and set a price you can defend.

Always agree the price before you start work. Tell the customer the price, what it covers, and when the shoe will be ready. If the shoe needs more work than you first thought, call the customer before you do the extra work and agree a new price. Surprising a customer with a higher price at collection is one of the fastest ways to lose trust.

Give a simple receipt or a ticket for every shoe you collect. Write the customer's name, phone number, the shoe description, the price agreed, and the collection date. Keep a copy for yourself. This protects you if a customer forgets their shoe, and it protects the customer if there is a dispute. A small exercise book can serve as your job record. Write neatly and keep it safe.

## Daily Operations and Customer Service

A shoe repair business succeeds on reliability. Customers leave their shoes with you and trust you to return them in good condition, on time. That trust is your most valuable asset.

Set clear working hours and keep them. If you open at 8 a.m., open at 8 a.m. If you say a shoe will be ready on Friday, have it ready on Friday. Many customers need their shoes for work on Monday, a wedding on Saturday, or school on Monday morning. A late repair can cause real problems for them. If a delay happens, call or message the customer early and explain. Do not wait for them to come and find the shoe is not ready.

Keep customers' shoes safe and organised. Use tags, shelves, or bags labelled with the customer's name and job number. Never mix up shoes, and never lose a pair. If you work in an open roadside spot, have a secure box or bag for shoes you are keeping overnight. Losing a customer's shoe can cost you far more than the repair fee, because you may have to replace it.

Be polite and professional, even when a customer is difficult. Greet customers, listen to what they want, and explain what you will do. If a customer brings a shoe that is very dirty, do not complain. Clean it as part of the service or explain the cleaning fee. If a customer bargains hard, stay calm and explain your price clearly. You will learn more about handling bargaining in other guides, but the basic rule is to stay respectful and firm.

Ask for feedback when the customer collects the shoe. A simple "Please check it well, sir. If anything is not right, tell me and I will fix it" shows confidence and care. If the customer is happy, ask them to tell their friends or to bring their next pair. Word of mouth is the cheapest and strongest marketing for a cobbler.

## How to Find Customers and Grow

In the first weeks, you need to let people know you exist. Start with your immediate area. Tell neighbours, traders, and shop owners that you repair shoes. Give them your phone number. Offer to repair one pair for a nearby shop owner at a fair price so they can see your quality and recommend you.

Use WhatsApp status to show your work. Take clear before-and-after photos of shoes you repair, with the customer's permission. Post one or two photos a day, not ten. Show a torn sole before, and the same shoe neat and strong after. That visual proof is more convincing than any advert. Add your location and phone number to your WhatsApp Business profile so new customers can find you.

Offer pickup and delivery for busy customers. Many office workers and families would rather send a rider or have you collect shoes from their estate gate than carry shoes to the market. You can charge a small delivery fee, such as ₦500 to ₦1,500, depending on distance. Start with customers near you, and expand as you learn the routes.

Build relationships with people who handle many shoes. School owners, church ushers, hotel staff, security companies, and corporate offices often need regular shoe repairs. Introduce yourself, leave your card or number, and offer a fair rate for regular work. One school that sends 20 pairs of school shoes every term can give you steady income.

Consider adding related services once your main work is steady. Shoe cleaning and polishing for busy professionals, sales of polish, laces, and insoles, and simple bag and belt repairs can add daily income. Do not add too many services at once. Master shoe repair first, then add one service at a time and do it well.

Keep simple records of your income and expenses each evening. Write down what you earned, what you spent on materials, and how many jobs you completed. This shows which jobs earn the most and whether your prices need to change.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

New cobblers often make the same mistakes. Avoid them and you will move faster.

The first mistake is using poor glue or cheap materials to save money. The repair will fail, the customer will return angry, and you will have to redo the work for free. Good materials cost more, but they protect your reputation.

The second mistake is taking too many jobs at once and missing deadlines. It is better to accept five jobs and deliver five on time than to accept fifteen and deliver only five. If you are full, tell the customer honestly and give a later date. They will respect your honesty.

The third mistake is not keeping records. Without a job ticket, you may forget who owns which shoe, or you may forget a price you agreed. A simple book prevents these problems.

## Your First 30 Days: A Simple Plan

Here is a practical plan for your first month. In week one, finish your learning or practise, buy your basic tools and materials, and choose your location. Set up your workspace, make a signboard, and prepare your job book and price list. Tell people around you that you are open.

In week two, focus on your first customers. Do every job carefully, agree prices before work, and deliver on time. Take before-and-after photos with permission, and post them on WhatsApp status. Ask every happy customer to tell one person about you.

In week three, review your prices and your busiest days. Note which repairs customers ask for most. Buy more of the materials you use most. If school shoes are common, stock more school-shoe soles. If office shoes are common, stock more heel tips and polish.

In week four, set a simple goal for the next month. It could be to earn a set amount per week, to gain ten repeat customers, or to add shoe cleaning as a service. Write the goal down and track your progress daily. A small, clear goal keeps you focused.

Starting a shoe repair business in Nigeria does not require a big office or a large loan. It requires a skill you can trust, tools you can rely on, a location people can find, fair prices, and the discipline to deliver on time, every time. Start small, do honest work, keep good records, and let your customers' shoes speak for you. The shoes you repair well today will bring the customers who keep your business alive tomorrow.
