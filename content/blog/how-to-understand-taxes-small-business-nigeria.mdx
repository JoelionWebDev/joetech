Tax is one of those subjects most Nigerian small business owners would rather avoid. You started your business to sell, serve customers and earn a living, not to study tax law. Yet sooner or later, tax catches up with every business: a customer asks for an invoice with VAT, a bank asks for your Tax Identification Number, a state revenue officer visits your shop, or a big client refuses to pay you until you show a tax clearance certificate.

When that moment arrives, many owners panic. Some pay whatever figure they are told without asking questions. Others ignore tax completely and hope nobody notices. Both approaches are expensive: the first wastes money, and the second builds up penalties and interest that can shut a small business down. The good news is that understanding tax is simpler than it looks. You do not need a degree in accounting. You need to know which taxes apply to your type of business, how to register, how to keep records, and when to file and pay.

## Why Tax Matters Even for a Very Small Business

Some owners believe tax is only for big companies. That is not true. In Nigeria, once you earn income from a trade, profession or business, you are inside the tax system, whether you sell clothes in Balogun Market, run a salon in Lekki, bake from home in Ikeja, or sell shoes online from Port Harcourt.

Tax matters for practical reasons beyond avoiding trouble. A registered, tax-compliant business can open a corporate bank account more easily, bid for contracts, and work with large companies and government agencies that demand a tax clearance certificate before they pay. Many corporate clients will not sign a supplier agreement with a business that cannot produce one.

Tax compliance also protects your money. When your records are in order, you can prove what you actually earned instead of accepting an inflated assessment, and you avoid penalties that often cost far more than the original tax. Think of tax as a business cost, like rent or electricity: plan for it, keep records for it, and pay it on time.

## The Two Levels of Tax in Nigeria

Nigeria runs a federal system, so taxes are collected at two main levels: federal and state. A small business owner usually deals with both, but for different taxes.

Federal taxes are administered by the Federal Inland Revenue Service, known as FIRS. The main federal taxes that touch small businesses are Companies Income Tax, Value Added Tax, and withholding tax. If your business is registered as a limited liability company, FIRS is your main tax authority for income tax.

State taxes are administered by your State Internal Revenue Service. In Lagos, that is the Lagos State Internal Revenue Service, LIRS. In other states, the name follows the same pattern. State authorities handle Personal Income Tax for individuals and sole traders, and they also collect certain levies and fees.

Local government areas also collect some levies, such as shop rates and signage fees, but these are usually small and specific to your location. If a local government officer demands a payment, ask for the official demand notice and the legal basis, and keep the receipt.

The important point is this: your business structure decides who taxes you. A registered company pays Companies Income Tax to FIRS. A sole trader or partnership pays Personal Income Tax to the state where the owner lives. Many beginners get confused here, so let us look at each tax one by one.

## Companies Income Tax: The Tax on Your Profit

Companies Income Tax, often shortened to CIT, is the tax a limited liability company pays on its profit. It is charged on the profit your company makes in a year, not on your total sales. That distinction matters. If your company sold goods worth ten million naira in a year but spent nine million naira running the business, the tax is worked out on the profit, not the ten million.

For many years, small companies in Nigeria have benefited from a lower rate. Companies with an annual turnover below a set threshold pay a lower rate than large companies, and very small companies may pay nothing at all on their profit if their turnover falls below the exemption threshold set by law. The exact thresholds and rates change from time to time when the government amends the Finance Act, so always confirm the current figures on the FIRS website or with your accountant before you file.

What you need to remember is the routine. Every year, your company must prepare its accounts, compute its profit, and file a tax return with FIRS. Even in a year when you made a loss, you still file. Filing a nil or loss return on time is far better than filing nothing, because late filing attracts penalties even when no tax is due.

Keep company money separate from personal spending, and pay yourself through the books. Clean separation makes your profit figure honest and defensible.

## Personal Income Tax: The Tax for Sole Traders and Individuals

If you run your business as a sole trader, or you are a freelancer or self-employed professional, your business income is taxed as personal income. In Lagos, you pay through LIRS. In Abuja, you pay through the FCT Internal Revenue Service. In Rivers State, you pay through the Rivers State Internal Revenue Service, and so on.

Personal Income Tax is charged on your total income from all sources, after allowed deductions. The rate rises with income, so higher earners pay a higher percentage on the upper part of their income. As with companies, the exact bands and reliefs are set by law and can change, so confirm the current schedule before you calculate.

Registration is straightforward. You visit your state tax office or register online where the state offers it, provide your National Identification Number and your business details, and you receive a Tax Identification Number. In Lagos, many people register through the LIRS eTax portal. Other states have their own portals or offices. Ask at your state tax office if you are unsure.

Once registered, you file an annual return, usually early in the year, declaring the income you earned in the previous year. You also pay any tax due. Some states allow you to pay in instalments. The key is to file every year, even when business was slow. A filed return is your proof of compliance, and it is what produces your tax clearance certificate.

## Value Added Tax: The Tax Your Customers Pay Through You

Value Added Tax, known as VAT, confuses many small business owners because it is not really your money. VAT is a consumption tax. When you sell a taxable good or service, you add VAT to the price, collect it from the customer, and later pay it to FIRS, after subtracting the VAT you paid on your own business purchases.

In Nigeria, the standard VAT rate is 7.5 percent. That means if you sell a product for one hundred thousand naira before VAT, you add seven thousand five hundred naira as VAT, and the customer pays one hundred and seven thousand five hundred naira in total.

Not every business must register for VAT. There is a turnover threshold: businesses whose annual sales fall below a set amount are not required to register or charge VAT. If your sales are above that threshold, registration is compulsory, and you must charge VAT, file monthly VAT returns, and pay what you owe.

Some goods and services are exempt from VAT, including many basic food items, medical products and educational services. If you sell exempt items, you do not charge VAT on them. If you sell a mix of taxable and exempt items, keep them separate in your records.

Once you are registered for VAT, file your monthly return every month, even in a month with no taxable sales. A missed month attracts a penalty, and penalties accumulate quickly.

## Withholding Tax: The Tax Deducted Before You Are Paid

Withholding tax is another tax that surprises small business owners. When a company pays you for a service, such as a supply contract, a consultancy job, or rent, it may be required to deduct a percentage of the payment and send it to the tax authority on your behalf.

For example, if a company hires your business for a one million naira job and the withholding tax rate for that type of service is 5 percent, the company pays you nine hundred and fifty thousand naira and sends fifty thousand naira to FIRS or the state tax authority. You receive a withholding tax credit note as proof.

That deducted amount is not lost. It is a credit against your final tax bill for the year, so when you file your annual return you present your credit notes and subtract the tax already withheld. Always collect your withholding tax credit notes and keep them safe: without the note, you cannot claim the credit, and you may end up paying the same tax twice.

## Other Taxes and Levies You May Meet

Beyond the big three, a small business may meet other obligations depending on what it does. If you employ staff, you must deduct Pay As You Earn tax, known as PAYE, from their salaries and send it to the state tax authority every month, and think about pension contributions for your employees. If you import goods, you will pay customs duties at the port. Your local government may also charge a tenement rate on your premises, a signage fee, or a trade licence fee. Pay only against an official demand notice, and always collect a receipt.

## How to Register for Tax: Step by Step

Registration is the first real step, and it is easier than most people expect. Here is a practical order to follow.

First, decide your business structure. If you have not registered your business at all, register it with the Corporate Affairs Commission, CAC. A business name registration is enough for a sole trader. A limited liability company suits a business that wants a separate legal identity. Your structure decides which tax authority handles your income tax, so get this right first.

Second, get your Tax Identification Number. For a company, you can obtain a TIN through the FIRS portal or at a FIRS office, using your CAC registration documents. For an individual, your state tax service issues your TIN, usually linked to your National Identification Number. Keep your TIN safe. You will use it on invoices, bank forms and contracts.

Third, register for VAT if your turnover reaches the threshold. You do this with FIRS. If you are below the threshold, you do not need to register yet, but monitor your sales so you register promptly when you cross it.

Fourth, register as an employer if you have staff. This means registering for PAYE with your state tax service so you can deduct and remit your employees' tax correctly.

Fifth, set up a simple record-keeping system from day one. A spreadsheet or a basic accounting app works well. Record every sale, every expense, every bank transfer and every receipt. Tax is mostly about records.

## Keeping Records That Protect You

Tax officers do not ask for your memory. They ask for documents. The businesses that suffer during a tax audit are usually the ones with no records, not the ones with small profits.

Keep these records as a minimum. Keep all sales invoices and receipts, including electronic receipts from transfers and point-of-sale machines. Keep all purchase invoices and expense receipts, from rent and electricity to transport and internet data. Keep your bank statements. Keep contracts and agreements with clients and suppliers. Keep your withholding tax credit notes. Keep your payroll records if you employ anyone.

Store them in an organised way: one folder per month, with income in one section and expenses in another. Photograph paper receipts and save the photos in a backed-up folder, because paper receipts fade. Reconcile your bank statement with your sales record every month. Keep records for at least six years, because tax authorities can look back several years and old records are your defence if an old assessment is questioned.

If your business uses Paystack, Flutterwave, Opay or PalmPay to receive payments, download your transaction statements every month and save them. These statements are part of your sales record, and they help you reconcile what came in against what you recorded.

## Filing and Paying: The Annual Routine

Every tax has its own filing rhythm, and learning the rhythm is half the battle. Companies file their annual income tax return with FIRS within a set period after the end of their financial year. Individuals file their personal income tax return with their state tax service early in the year for the previous year. VAT returns are filed monthly. PAYE is remitted monthly.

Mark these dates in your calendar at the start of every year, and set a reminder two weeks before each one. Late filing attracts penalties, and penalties on small amounts can grow into large amounts if ignored.

FIRS and most state tax services accept payment online through their portals, through bank transfer, or through approved payment channels. Always pay through an official channel and keep the receipt. Never hand cash to an individual without an official demand notice. If you cannot pay the full amount at once, visit the tax office, explain your situation, and ask about a payment plan. Silence is what turns a small debt into a big problem.

## Common Mistakes Small Business Owners Make

The first mistake is not registering at all. An unregistered business cannot get a tax clearance certificate, and it will lose corporate clients and contracts. Registration is cheap compared with the opportunities it opens.

The second mistake is not filing returns in a bad year. Some owners think that if they made no profit, there is nothing to file. The opposite is true. A nil return proves you had no profit. No return at all looks like evasion.

The third mistake is spending business money as if it were personal money. When you take cash from the till for personal expenses without recording it, your books no longer reflect reality. Your profit figure becomes unreliable, and you cannot defend it.

The fourth mistake is ignoring letters from the tax office. A letter is usually a request for information or a notice of assessment. Reply promptly, even if only to ask for more time or clarification. Ignored letters escalate into enforcement.

The fifth mistake is trusting an agent blindly. Some owners hand everything to a so-called tax consultant and never check what was filed or paid. Choose your adviser carefully, ask for copies of every return and receipt, and verify payments through the official portal where possible.

## When to Get Professional Help

You can handle basic tax compliance yourself when your business is small and simple. But there are times when professional help pays for itself. Get an accountant or tax consultant if your turnover is growing fast, if you are registering for VAT, if you employ several staff, if you are bidding for a large contract that requires a tax clearance certificate, or if you receive a tax audit notice.

A good accountant does more than file returns. They help you plan which expenses are deductible, when to register for VAT, and how to use withholding tax credits. Their fee is usually far smaller than the penalties they help you avoid. When choosing an adviser, ask for references, confirm their qualifications, and agree the fee in writing. Avoid anyone who promises to make your tax disappear. FIRS and state tax services also run taxpayer education sessions, and the Small and Medium Enterprises Development Agency of Nigeria, SMEDAN, offers training on tax and record-keeping. Use these resources, especially when you are starting out.

## Your Tax Checklist for This Month

Work through this checklist in the next thirty days: confirm your CAC registration, confirm you have a TIN, check whether your turnover requires VAT registration, organise this year's sales and expense records by month, put your next filing date in your calendar with a reminder, collect any withholding tax credit notes you have received, and confirm PAYE is correct if you employ staff. You do not need to finish everything in a day, but each item makes your business stronger and safer.

Tax is not a punishment for doing business. Once you understand which taxes apply to you, register properly, keep good records, and file on time, tax becomes a routine task instead of a source of fear. Start with registration and record-keeping this week.
