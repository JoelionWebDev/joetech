## What Upselling Really Means

Most Nigerian business owners have heard the word upselling, and many dislike it. It brings to mind a pushy shop attendant who will not let you leave, or a WhatsApp seller who keeps adding items to your order after you have already said yes. Nobody wants to be that seller. Nobody wants to be sold to by that seller either.

Real upselling is different. It is not about tricking a customer into spending more. It is about noticing what the customer truly needs and offering a better, more complete, or more convenient option. When a customer buys a phone in Ikeja and you suggest a screen protector and a case, you are not being pushy — you are helping them protect an expensive purchase. When a customer orders a cake in Lekki and you suggest a matching set of cupcakes for the children's table, you are helping them host a better party.

Done well, upselling feels like helpful advice. Done badly, it feels like pressure. The difference is intent, timing and honesty. This guide shows you how to upsell in a way that increases your revenue, improves the customer's experience, and keeps them coming back.

## Start With the Customer's Need, Not Your Target

The first rule of gentle upselling is simple: listen before you suggest. A customer who came in for a basic hair cream does not need a lecture about your premium range. A customer who is buying a gift for a friend does not need the most expensive item in your shop. They need the right item for their situation.

Ask a few short questions before you recommend anything. Who is it for? What is the occasion? What is your budget? How will you use it? Those questions take less than a minute on WhatsApp or in person, and they change everything. They show the customer you care about their need, and they give you the information to make a useful suggestion instead of a random one.

### A Worked Example: A Skincare Seller in Abuja

A customer messages a skincare vendor in Abuja: "I need a face cream, my budget is ₦15,000." A pushy seller replies with a ₦45,000 premium cream and a long list of other products. A helpful seller asks: "What is your skin type, and is this for daily use or for a specific concern like dark spots?" The customer says she has oily skin and wants something light for daily use. The seller recommends a ₦18,000 lightweight moisturiser that suits oily skin, explains why it works better than the heavier ₦12,000 option, and offers a small ₦5,000 cleanser that pairs well with it. The total is ₦23,000 — more than the original budget, but the customer understands why, and she is more likely to be happy with the result.

Notice what happened. The seller did not ignore the budget. She acknowledged it, explained the trade-off, and gave the customer a choice. The customer felt advised, not pressured. That is the heart of good upselling.

## Offer a Better Option, Not Just a More Expensive One

The simplest form of upselling is to offer a better version of what the customer already wants. This works in almost every Nigerian business, from fashion to food to electronics.

Present two or three options, with the differences explained clearly. For example, a tailor in Surulere might say: "The standard fabric is ₦25,000 and it will look good. The premium fabric is ₦38,000, it holds its shape better after washing, and the colour stays brighter for longer." The customer can then decide whether the extra ₦13,000 is worth it to them. Some will choose the standard option, and that is fine. Others will choose the premium one, and they will feel good about the decision because they understood the benefit.

The key is to explain the benefit in the customer's language, not in technical terms. Do not say "this has a higher thread count". Say "this one will still look new after ten washes". Do not say "this phone has more RAM". Say "this one will not hang when you open WhatsApp, Instagram and your banking app at the same time". Benefits sell. Features alone do not.

### The Three-Option Method

A practical way to do this is to offer three options: good, better and best. The middle option is often the one customers choose, because it feels like a sensible balance. A photographer in Port Harcourt might offer a basic package at ₦150,000, a standard package at ₦250,000 with more edited photos and a photo book, and a premium package at ₦400,000 with a second photographer and a short video. Many clients will choose the middle package, and they will feel they made a smart choice rather than being pushed to the top.

Do not make the cheapest option deliberately poor just to force an upgrade. Customers notice, and they lose trust. Each option should be a genuine, good choice on its own. The more expensive options should simply offer more value — more time, more quality, more convenience, or a longer-lasting result.

## Bundle Products That Belong Together

Bundling is one of the gentlest forms of upselling, because it solves a problem the customer already has. Instead of selling one item at a time, you group items that are naturally used together and offer them at a slightly better total price than buying each separately.

A hair vendor in Lagos might bundle a wig, a wig cap and a bottle of hair serum. A food vendor might bundle a tray of small chops with a bottle of drink for a party. A phone accessories seller might bundle a charger, a case and a screen guard. In each case, the customer saves a little money and avoids the hassle of buying the extras elsewhere. You increase the value of the order and move more stock.

### How to Build a Good Bundle

A good bundle follows three rules. First, the items must genuinely belong together. A random collection of unrelated products feels like a sales trick. Second, the bundle price should be clearly better than buying the items separately, even if only by a small amount. If a wig is ₦85,000, a cap is ₦5,000 and serum is ₦8,000, the separate total is ₦98,000. A bundle price of ₦90,000 is a real saving, and it is easy to explain. Third, make the bundle optional. "You can buy the wig on its own for ₦85,000, or the full set for ₦90,000" gives the customer control. Control is what makes upselling feel comfortable.

Bundles work especially well on WhatsApp and Instagram, where you can show a photo of the full set and list the savings in one message. They also work well at the point of delivery. When a dispatch rider is about to leave with a customer's order, a short message — "We have a matching scarf for ₦7,500 if you would like to add it before dispatch" — can add a sale without any pressure, because the customer can simply say no.

## Time Your Suggestion Well

Timing is everything in upselling. The same suggestion can feel helpful at one moment and annoying at another. The best time to upsell is after the customer has already decided to buy, when they are happy and engaged, not before they have made up their mind.

In a shop in Lekki, that means waiting until the customer has chosen their main item, then suggesting the extra as they head to the counter. Online, it means suggesting the extra after the customer has confirmed the main order, not in your first message. A first message that lists ten products is overwhelming. A follow-up message that says "Your order is confirmed. Would you like to add a matching belt for ₦6,000 before we dispatch tomorrow?" is helpful and easy to answer.

Avoid upselling when a customer is upset, in a hurry, or has already said no. If a customer declines an extra, accept it gracefully and move on. "No problem at all, your order is ready" is the right response. Arguing, repeating the offer, or making the customer feel guilty will cost you far more than the extra sale was worth. A customer who feels respected will return. A customer who feels pressured will not.

## Use Social Proof and Honest Recommendations

Customers in Nigeria trust other customers. If many people buy a particular combination, say so. "Most of our customers who buy this dress also pick the matching scarf — it completes the look" is more persuasive than "You should buy the scarf too". It tells the customer that other people made the same choice and were happy with it.

Be honest about what you recommend and why. If the premium option is not right for a particular customer, say so. A good seller in Onitsha might tell a customer: "For your daily use, the standard blender at ₦65,000 is enough. The ₦120,000 one is better if you blend frozen fruits every day, but for normal soups and smoothies, you do not need it." That honesty builds trust, and trust brings repeat business. The customer who was told not to overspend today is the customer who will come back tomorrow for a bigger purchase, and who will recommend you to friends.

### The Power of "Most Customers Choose"

Use simple, honest phrases that help the customer decide:

- "Most customers who buy this laptop choose the 512GB version because the 256GB fills up quickly."
- "For a party of 50 people, most of our customers order the large tray — the medium one usually runs out."
- "If you travel often, the waterproof case is worth it. If the bag stays at home, the standard one is fine."

Each of these phrases gives the customer useful information. They are not tricks. They are the kind of advice a knowledgeable friend would give, and that is exactly how upselling should feel.

## Upsell After the Sale, Not Just During It

Upselling does not end when the customer pays. The period after delivery is a good time to offer something useful, provided you do it with care. A customer who bought a wig last month might be interested in a maintenance kit. A customer who bought a set of kitchen items might need a refill of a consumable. A customer who booked a photoshoot might want printed albums of the photos.

The key is to base the follow-up on what you know about the customer. Do not send the same generic message to everyone. A short, personal WhatsApp message works well: "Good afternoon, Mrs Eze. I hope the wig is sitting well. We now have the serum that keeps it soft for longer — ₦8,000, and I can add it to your next delivery if you like." That message is personal, relevant and easy to ignore if she is not interested. It does not demand a reply.

Keep a simple record of what each customer bought and when. That record lets you suggest the right product at the right time, instead of guessing. A customer who bought baby clothes three months ago may now need the next size. A customer who bought a phone in January may need a new charger by June. Timing your suggestion to the customer's real life makes it feel thoughtful rather than salesy.

## Train Your Team to Upsell Gently

If you have staff — a shop attendant, a sales assistant, or someone who replies to your WhatsApp — they need to understand gentle upselling too. A staff member who pressures customers can damage your reputation faster than you can repair it.

Teach your team three principles. First, listen before suggesting. Second, explain the benefit, not just the price. Third, accept "no" gracefully. Give them examples of what to say, and examples of what not to say. "Would you like to add a screen guard for ₦4,000? It protects the phone you just bought" is good. "You must buy the screen guard, otherwise the phone will spoil" is not.

Reward good behaviour, not just high sales. If a staff member makes a customer happy and that customer returns, that is worth more than a one-off upsell that made the customer uncomfortable. Review customer feedback regularly, and ask your team what customers respond well to. They are on the front line, and they often know what works.

## Measure What Works and Stop What Does Not

Upselling is a skill you can improve by paying attention to results. Keep simple notes for a month: which suggestions did customers accept, and which did they decline? Which bundles sold well? Which phrases seemed to help? You do not need a complex system. A notebook or a notes app is enough.

For example, a cosmetics seller in Ikeja might notice that customers accept a cleanser suggestion seven times out of ten, but rarely accept a suggestion for a second cream. That tells her to focus on the cleanser and stop pushing the second cream. A food vendor might notice that party bundles sell well on Fridays, when customers are planning weekend events, but not on Mondays. That tells him when to promote bundles and when to leave customers alone.

Set a simple goal, such as increasing your average order value by 10% over the next month. If your average order is currently ₦25,000, a 10% increase means ₦27,500. That is a modest, realistic target. You can reach it by suggesting one relevant extra to every third customer, not by pressuring every customer to buy more. Small, consistent improvements add up. An extra ₦2,500 on 100 orders a month is ₦250,000 of additional revenue, without a single new customer.

## Mistakes That Make Upselling Feel Pushy

Avoid these common mistakes, and your upselling will stay on the right side of helpful:

**Suggesting too many extras at once.** One relevant suggestion is helpful. Four suggestions at once is overwhelming. Pick the single best extra and offer that.

**Upselling before the customer has decided.** If the customer has not chosen the main product yet, adding extras only creates confusion. Wait until the main decision is made.

**Hiding the price.** Never suggest an extra without stating its price. A customer who discovers the cost only at checkout will feel tricked. State the price upfront, every time.

**Making the customer feel cheap for saying no.** Never say "It is only ₦5,000" in a tone that suggests the customer is stingy. Respect every budget.

**Recommending something you would not buy yourself.** If you do not believe in the product, do not recommend it. Customers can sense insincerity.

**Following up too aggressively.** One follow-up message about an extra is enough. Sending the same offer three times is pressure, not service.

## Conclusion: Help First, Sell Second

Upselling without being pushy comes down to a simple idea: help first, sell second. Listen to what the customer needs. Offer a better option and explain why it is better. Bundle items that belong together. Time your suggestion well, accept "no" gracefully, and follow up only when it is genuinely useful. When you do that, customers do not feel sold to — they feel looked after. They spend a little more, they enjoy what they bought more, and they come back.

Start with one change this week. Pick your best-selling product and think of the single most useful extra that goes with it. Offer it, with its price, to your next ten customers, and note how many say yes. That small experiment will teach you more about your customers than any theory. Then build from there, one helpful suggestion at a time.

## Checklist: Gentle Upselling

- Listen and ask questions before suggesting anything.
- Offer a better option and explain the benefit in plain language.
- Present good, better and best options and let the customer choose.
- Bundle items that belong together at a clear saving.
- Suggest extras after the main purchase is decided, not before.
- State the price of every extra upfront.
- Accept "no" gracefully and never repeat the offer aggressively.
- Follow up after delivery only with relevant, personal suggestions.
- Train your team to explain benefits and respect a "no".
- Track which suggestions work and focus on those.
