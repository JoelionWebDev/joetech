A small business owner in Lekki needs a photo for a flyer. She searches Google Images, downloads a picture of a smiling woman with a laptop, and posts it on Instagram. A week later, she gets a message demanding payment for using the image without permission. The photo was not free. It belonged to a photographer, and Google had only shown it — Google did not own it.

This happens to Nigerian businesses more often than most owners realise. Photos make websites, flyers, WhatsApp statuses, and social media posts look professional. But every photo has an owner, and using it without the right permission is copyright infringement, even if you found it on Google, even if you did not make money from it, and even if you did not know it was protected.

The good news is that millions of genuinely free photos are available legally. This guide explains how their licences work, which sites are safe, what you may and may not do, and how to build a simple system so your business never faces a copyright claim.

## "Free" does not always mean free to use

Many business owners confuse three different things: free to view, free to download, and free to use for business. A photo can be free to view on a website and still be illegal to use in your advert. A photo can be free to download for personal use and still require payment for commercial use.

Nigerian copyright law, like copyright law in most countries, protects photographs automatically from the moment they are taken. The photographer owns the copyright unless they have sold it or given it away under a licence. You do not need to see a copyright symbol or a watermark for the photo to be protected. If you did not take the photo, did not buy it, and do not have a licence to use it, assume you may not use it.

That rule applies even to images you find in these common places:

- **Google Images.** Google is a search engine, not a photo library. Most images it shows belong to someone else. Google has a usage-rights filter, but it is not a guarantee. Never assume a Google result is free.
- **Another business's website or Instagram page.** Downloading a competitor's product photo or a hotel's room photo and reusing it is infringement, even if you credit them. Credit is not permission.
- **WhatsApp forwards and Facebook groups.** A photo shared in a group is still owned by its photographer. Forwarding does not transfer the licence.
- **Screenshots from films, music videos, or TV.** Those images belong to the production company. Using a celebrity photo from a film still for your advert can create both copyright and personality-rights problems.
- **AI-generated images.** If you generate an image with an AI tool, check the tool's terms. Some tools allow commercial use, others do not, and some require a paid plan for business use. Keep a record of which tool you used and its terms on the day you generated the image.

The safest rule is simple: only use photos you took yourself, photos you paid for, or photos from a trusted free stock site under a clear licence you have read.

## The licences, in plain English

You do not need a law degree to use stock photos safely. Understand four licence types that cover almost every free photo you will meet.

**1. Public domain (CC0).** The photographer has given up all rights. You can use the photo for any purpose, including commercial use, without attribution and without paying. You may edit it, crop it, and use it in adverts. This is the freest licence, and the safest for business use.

**2. Free for commercial use, no attribution required.** Many stock sites use their own licence that works like CC0 but is not technically CC0. Unsplash and Pexels are the best-known examples. You can use the photo commercially without paying or crediting the photographer, with a few limits — usually you may not sell the unedited photo itself, claim you took it, or use it in a way that implies the person in the photo endorses your product.

**3. Creative Commons Attribution (CC BY).** You may use the photo commercially, but you must credit the photographer, usually with their name and a link to the licence. If you forget the credit, you are breaking the licence. This licence is common on Flickr and Wikimedia Commons.

**4. Creative Commons Non-Commercial (CC BY-NC).** You may use the photo only for non-commercial purposes, with attribution. A business advert, a product page, or a flyer that promotes your services is commercial use, so this licence does NOT cover your business. Avoid CC BY-NC photos for marketing, even if they are free.

Other Creative Commons variants add conditions such as "no derivatives" (you may not edit the photo) or "share alike" (you must share your work under the same licence). For business use, stick to CC0 or the free commercial licences from trusted stock sites. They are simpler, and they avoid attribution mistakes.

One important warning: licences can change, and a photo that was free on one site may be protected on another. Always download from the original stock site, not from a repost, and keep a note of the site, the photographer, and the date you downloaded it.

## Safe free stock photo sites for Nigerian businesses

These sites are widely used, have clear licences, and offer photos you can use for commercial purposes without paying. Bookmark them and use them instead of Google Images.

**Unsplash (unsplash.com).** Large library, high-quality photos, free for commercial use without attribution, although crediting the photographer is appreciated. Search by topic, such as "Lagos market", "African businesswoman", or "Nigerian food". Unsplash photos are popular, so the same image may appear on other websites. That is normal for stock photos, and it is not a problem for most small businesses.

**Pexels (pexels.com).** Similar to Unsplash, with photos and free videos. Free for commercial use without attribution. Pexels has a good selection of African and Nigerian lifestyle images if you search carefully. Its video library is useful for website headers and social media reels.

**Pixabay (pixabay.com).** Photos, illustrations, and vectors, free for commercial use. Pixabay also hosts illustrations and icons, which are useful for flyers and blog graphics. Check each item, because a small number are marked as sponsored content from paid stock sites.

**Burst by Shopify (burst.shopify.com).** Free photos aimed at online sellers, including product, fashion, food, and business images. Useful if you run an e-commerce store.

**Wikimedia Commons (commons.wikimedia.org).** A huge library, but licences vary by photo. Many are CC BY, so you must credit the photographer. It is excellent for photos of Nigerian landmarks, historical images, and places, but check the licence on each photo before you use it.

**Flickr Creative Commons search.** Flickr has millions of photos, but most are fully protected. Use the licence filter and choose only CC0 or CC BY images. If in doubt, do not use it.

For Nigerian and African representation, search with specific terms: "African woman entrepreneur", "Lagos street", "Nigerian market", "African family", "black businessman", "Abuja city". Stock libraries are improving their African content every year, but generic searches still return mostly Western images. Specific searches give you photos that look like your customers, which builds trust.

Avoid random "free photo" sites you have never heard of. Some repost protected photos and claim they are free. If a site has no clear licence page, no photographer names, and no contact details, do not trust it. Stick to the sites above.

## What you may and may not do with a free stock photo

Even a free licence has limits. These are the rules that apply on Unsplash, Pexels, and Pixabay, and they are similar across most free stock sites:

**You may:**

- Use the photo on your website, blog, Instagram, Facebook, WhatsApp Business, flyers, and adverts.
- Edit, crop, resize, and add text or your logo over the photo.
- Use it in a paid advert on Meta or Google, as long as the advert is for your product or service, not for selling the photo itself.
- Use it in a product mock-up, a presentation, or a YouTube thumbnail.

**You may not:**

- Sell the photo as it is, or sell it as a print, a wallpaper, or a stock photo on another site.
- Claim you took the photo, or remove a photographer's credit where credit is required.
- Use a photo of a recognisable person to imply they endorse your product, use your service, or hold an opinion they do not hold. A photo of a woman smiling does not mean she endorses your skin cream.
- Use a photo in a defamatory, misleading, or illegal context — for example, using a stranger's photo to advertise a dating service, a medical treatment, or a loan company without permission.
- Use a photo containing a trademark, such as a Coca-Cola bottle or a Nike logo, in a way that suggests your business is connected to that brand. Trademark rules are separate from copyright, and a free photo licence does not give you trademark rights.
- Download a photo from Unsplash and then upload it to another stock site as your own work. That is a breach of the licence and of the site's terms.

Photos of people deserve extra care. Stock sites usually have a model release for the people in their photos, which means the person agreed to be photographed for stock use. That release covers normal commercial use, but it does not cover sensitive uses — for example, using a photo of a real person to advertise a hospital, a lawyer, or a political campaign can be risky. If your advert is sensitive, use a photo where the person's face is not identifiable, or use a model you hired yourself with a signed release.

For children, be especially careful. Only use photos of children from trusted stock sites, in a normal, respectful context, and never in an advert for adult products, loans, or medical treatments. If you run a school or a children's business in Lagos or Abuja, the safest photos are of your own pupils, with written permission from their parents.

## How to find the right photo, step by step

Good photos are not found by luck. Use this process each time you need one:

1. **Write down what the photo must show.** Not "something nice", but "a Nigerian woman in her thirties, smiling, holding a shopping bag, bright daylight". A clear brief makes the search faster and the result better.
2. **Search two stock sites, not one.** Start with Unsplash, then try Pexels with the same words. Different sites return different results, and the second site often has the better photo.
3. **Check the photo at full size.** A photo that looks sharp as a small thumbnail may be blurry when enlarged for a flyer or a website banner. Download the large size, and zoom in before you use it.
4. **Check the licence on the photo page.** Confirm it says free for commercial use, and note whether attribution is required. If the page says "editorial use only", do not use it in an advert.
5. **Save the source.** Create a simple spreadsheet or a notes file with four columns: photo name, site, photographer, and date downloaded. It takes one minute and it is your evidence if anyone ever questions you.
6. **Edit for your brand.** Crop to the right size for Instagram (square or 4:5), for a WhatsApp status (9:16), or for a website banner (wide). Add your logo in a corner, but do not cover a person's face with text. Keep the colours consistent with your brand.
7. **Compress before you upload.** A large photo slows down a website, especially for visitors on MTN or Airtel data in Nigeria. Use a free compressor such as TinyPNG or Squoosh to reduce the file size without losing visible quality. Aim for under 200KB for a blog image and under 500KB for a website banner.

If you cannot find a suitable free photo, consider taking your own. A modern phone in daylight, with a clean background, produces photos that are often better for a Nigerian business than a generic stock image, because they show your real shop, your real products, and your real customers. Ask customers for permission before photographing them, and keep that permission in writing on WhatsApp or on paper.

## Attribution and what to do if you receive a claim

On Unsplash, Pexels, and Pixabay, attribution is appreciated but not required. On CC BY photos it is required: include the photographer's name, a link to the photo or their profile, and a link to the licence. On a website put the credit under the photo; on Instagram put it in the caption. Writing "Photo: Google" or "Source: Internet" is not a credit and does not make an infringing use legal. If you cannot name the photographer and the licence, do not use the photo.

Copyright claims against Nigerian small businesses are becoming more common, especially from international agencies that scan the internet for their images. If you receive one, stop using the photo immediately, read the claim carefully, and check your records — if you downloaded it from Unsplash or Pexels, reply with that evidence, as many claims are withdrawn on proof. Do not pay immediately: some claims are genuine and some are inflated, so if the amount is large or you are unsure, speak to an intellectual property lawyer in Lagos before paying. Reply privately and in writing, keep every message, then audit your site and social media for other photos you cannot trace. Prevention is the best protection: a business that only uses photos with a clear licence, and keeps a record of each one, rarely faces a claim.

## Paid photos, a simple system, and mistakes to avoid

Free stock covers most small business needs, but consider paying — Shutterstock, Adobe Stock, Getty Images, or iStock, typically $10 to $50 for a standard single-image licence — when you need a very specific image no free photo matches. Read the licence first: a standard licence usually covers websites and social media, while an extended licence is needed for items you sell. Never download paid stock from a "free download" site; those are piracy sites. If you hire a designer, ask in writing where they get photos, and add to your agreement: "All images must be original, licensed, or from a named free stock site, with a record kept." You are responsible for what appears on your business page, even if someone else posted it.

Set up a simple system in an afternoon: a photo folder on Google Drive by year and month, a licence log in Google Sheets (photo name, source site, photographer, licence, date downloaded, where you used it), and a quarterly audit of your homepage, top five blog posts, and last 20 Instagram posts, replacing any photo you cannot trace. Use your own photos where you can — your real shop, products, and customers, with permission — and train any staff who post for you to use only the safe sites in this guide.

Common mistakes to avoid: using Google Images as a photo library; assuming credit makes an illegal use legal; using a celebrity photo to imply endorsement; copying a photo because "everyone else uses it"; ignoring the licence on Wikimedia and Flickr; using a stock photo to sell the photo itself, such as printing it on a T-shirt; and keeping no records. If you cannot prove where a photo came from, you cannot defend its use.

## Final word: beautiful and legal can go together

You do not need to steal photos to have a beautiful website or a professional Instagram page. Unsplash, Pexels, and Pixabay offer millions of high-quality images that are free for business use, and your own phone can produce excellent photos of your real products, your real shop, and your real customers.

Use this rule every time you need an image: if you did not take it, did not buy it, and cannot name the site and licence that allow you to use it, do not post it. Find a free alternative, or take the photo yourself.

Start today: replace one risky photo with one from Unsplash or Pexels, save the source in your licence log, and repeat weekly. In a month your business will look just as good — and you will know every image is yours to use.
