A provision store owner in Abeokuta starts every year saying, "This year, I will grow my business." By June, nothing has changed. She still sells the same products, to the same customers, in the same way. She is not lazy. She works long hours. But her goal was too vague to guide her daily decisions. "Grow my business" does not tell you what to do on Monday morning.

Goals work only when they are clear, specific, and broken into small actions. A good goal tells you what to do, how much to do, and by when. This guide will show you how to set goals that actually work for a Nigerian small business, with real examples you can copy.

## Why Most Business Goals Fail

Most goals fail for four reasons. First, they are too vague. "Make more money" and "get more customers" sound good, but they do not tell you what to change. Second, they are too big. "Open five branches this year" is exciting, but impossible for a business that struggles to pay one shop rent. Third, they have no deadline. A goal without a date is a wish. Fourth, they are never written down or reviewed. A goal you keep in your head disappears when life gets busy.

In Nigeria, there is another reason goals fail: they ignore reality. A goal that assumes stable power, stable prices, and stable fuel costs will break the first time the price of fuel rises or the naira changes. Good goals are realistic about Nigerian conditions. They leave room for slow months, price increases, and unexpected costs.

The good news is that goal setting is a skill, not a talent. Anyone can learn it. You do not need a business degree or a fancy app. You need a notebook, an honest look at your business, and a simple system you will actually follow.

## Step 1: Start With Where You Are Now

Before you set a goal for the future, write down where you are today. You cannot plan a journey if you do not know your starting point.

Answer these questions honestly, in your notebook. How much do you sell in a good month, and in a bad month? How many customers do you serve in a week? What is your profit after all costs? Which products sell best, and which sell worst? How much money do you owe, and how much are you owed? How many hours do you work each week?

If you do not know some of these numbers, that is your first goal: track them for one month. Write down every sale, every expense, and every customer. At the end of the month, you will have real numbers to plan with. Guessing is not planning.

Also write down what is working and what is not. Perhaps your WhatsApp sales are growing, but your shop sales are falling. Perhaps your cakes sell well, but your small chops do not. Perhaps your customers love your service, but complain about delivery. This honest review shows you where to focus your goals.

Be kind to yourself in this review. The point is not to feel bad about the past. It is to see clearly so you can plan well. A business owner in Onitsha who discovers she makes only 5,000 naira profit a week has not failed. She has found the truth, and now she can set a goal to raise it to 15,000 naira a week.

## Step 2: Make Your Goal Specific and Measurable

A good goal answers three questions: what exactly will change, by how much, and by when? Use numbers and dates.

Compare these two goals. Vague: "I want more customers." Specific: "I want 50 new customers by the end of March, so that I serve 200 customers a month instead of 150." The second goal is clear. You will know whether you reached it, and you can plan how to get there.

Here are examples of good goals for Nigerian small businesses. "Increase monthly sales from 500,000 naira to 700,000 naira by June 2027." "Save 300,000 naira for a new freezer by September." "Get 20 Google reviews by the end of this quarter." "Sell 100 pieces of a new product in the first month." "Reduce waste in my food business by 20 percent in three months."

Each of these goals has a number and a date. Each one can be checked. Each one is big enough to matter, but small enough to be possible.

Use the SMART test, in plain language. Specific: what exactly will happen? Measurable: how will you know you did it? Achievable: can you really do it with the money, time, and help you have? Relevant: will it actually help your business? Time-bound: by what date?

If your goal fails this test, rewrite it. "Grow my business" becomes "Increase my monthly profit from 80,000 naira to 120,000 naira by the end of June." Now you have something to work towards.

## Step 3: Choose Only Three Goals at a Time

Many business owners set ten goals at once and achieve none of them. Focus is more powerful than ambition. Choose three goals at most: one for money, one for customers, and one for the business itself.

A money goal could be about sales, profit, or savings. A customer goal could be about new customers, repeat customers, or reviews. A business goal could be about a new product, better service, or a system that saves you time.

For example, a hair salon in Lekki could set these three goals for the next three months. Money: increase monthly profit from 150,000 naira to 200,000 naira. Customers: get 30 new customers through referrals. Business: start taking bookings on WhatsApp Business with a simple price list, so customers can book without calling.

Three goals are enough to make real progress without feeling overwhelmed. When you finish one, replace it with a new one. Over a year, that is twelve goals completed, which is far more than most businesses achieve.

Write your three goals on a card and put it where you will see it every day: on your shop wall, on your fridge, or as the wallpaper on your phone. A goal you see daily is a goal you remember. A goal hidden in a notebook is a goal you forget.

## Step 4: Break Each Goal Into Weekly Actions

A goal without weekly actions is just a dream. The secret of goal setting is to break a big goal into small steps you can do this week.

Take the goal: "Increase monthly sales from 500,000 naira to 700,000 naira in three months." That is an extra 200,000 naira a month. How will you get it? Break it down. Perhaps you need 20 extra customers a month, each spending 10,000 naira. To get 20 extra customers, you need to message 100 old customers, post on WhatsApp Status every day, and ask every happy customer for a referral.

Now turn that into a weekly plan. Week one: message 25 old customers. Post your products on Status every morning. Ask five customers for referrals. Week two: repeat, and add a small offer for new customers. Week three: check your numbers. Did you get new customers? If not, change your approach.

Use a simple weekly check. Every Sunday evening, or every Monday morning, spend thirty minutes reviewing the week. What did you do? What worked? What did not work? What will you do next week? Write the answers in your notebook. This weekly habit is what separates businesses that grow from businesses that stay the same.

If a weekly action is too big, make it smaller. "Post every day" is hard. "Post three times this week" is easier. "Message 100 customers" is hard. "Message 10 customers today" is easy. Small actions done every week add up to big results.

## Step 5: Set Goals for Money, Not Just Sales

Many Nigerian business owners focus only on sales. Sales are important, but profit is what keeps your business alive. A business can sell a lot and still lose money if costs are too high.

Set a profit goal, not just a sales goal. Know your costs: stock, rent, transport, light, staff, and data. Subtract them from your sales to find your real profit. Then set a goal to increase that profit, either by selling more, by reducing waste, or by raising prices carefully.

Set a savings goal too. Decide how much you will save each month, even in slow months. Start small: 10,000 naira a month is better than nothing. Savings give you freedom. With savings, you can buy stock when prices are low, fix a broken freezer without borrowing, and survive a slow month without panic.

Set a debt goal if you owe money. Write down every debt, the amount, and the date you will pay it. A goal like "Pay off my 100,000 naira supplier debt by March" is clear and motivating. Paying off debt frees your profit for growth.

Also set a price goal. Many small businesses in Nigeria undercharge because they are afraid customers will leave. Check your prices against your costs and against competitors. If your costs have risen, your prices must rise too, or your profit will disappear. A goal to review your prices every three months will protect your business.

## Step 6: Review Your Goals Every Month

Goals are not set once and forgotten. They need regular review. At the end of each month, sit down with your notebook and ask four questions.

Did I reach my goal, or am I on track? If yes, celebrate, even in a small way. Buy yourself something small, or take a day off. Celebration keeps you motivated.

If not, why not? Was the goal too big? Did something unexpected happen, like a price increase or a family emergency? Did you forget to do the weekly actions? Be honest. The reason matters, because it tells you what to change.

What will I change next month? Perhaps you need a smaller goal, a different approach, or help from someone else. Perhaps you need to post more often, or change your product range. Change one thing, not everything.

Is this goal still important? Sometimes life changes, and a goal no longer makes sense. A business owner who planned to open a second shop may realise her first shop needs fixing first. It is okay to change a goal. It is not okay to abandon all goals because one did not work.

Keep your review simple and short. Thirty minutes a month is enough. The habit of reviewing is more important than the length of the review.

## Step 7: Track Your Progress With a Simple System

You do not need expensive software to track your goals. A notebook, a Google Sheet on your phone, or a simple wall chart will work. The best system is the one you will actually use every week.

Create one page for each goal. At the top, write the goal and the deadline. Below it, make a simple table with four columns: week, action taken, result, and next step. Every Sunday evening, fill in one row. After four weeks, you will see a clear picture of what is working and what is not.

For a sales goal, track daily sales in a notebook or a sheet. Add them up at the end of each week and compare the total with your target. If you are behind, you will know early, while there is still time to fix it. If you wait until the end of the month to check, it is too late to change anything.

For a customer goal, keep a list of new customers with the date they first bought from you and how they found you. This tells you which marketing works best. If most new customers come from referrals, spend more time asking for referrals. If they come from Instagram, spend more time posting there. Numbers stop you from guessing.

Keep your tracking visible. A wall chart in your shop, with a line that rises as you get closer to your goal, motivates you and your staff. When your staff see the chart moving, they understand the goal and work towards it with you. Share the goal with your team, celebrate small wins together, and ask for their ideas when progress is slow. A goal shared with your team is stronger than a goal kept to yourself.

## Common Goal-Setting Mistakes to Avoid

Avoid these mistakes that many Nigerian small business owners make.

Do not copy another business owner's goals. A goal that works for a big shop in Ikeja may not work for a small shop in a village. Your goals must fit your business, your money, and your customers.

Do not set goals based on what you wish was true. If you sell 200,000 naira a month now, a goal to sell 2,000,000 naira next month is not a goal. It is a fantasy. Set a goal that stretches you, but does not break you. A 20 to 30 percent increase is a good stretch for most small businesses.

Do not set a goal and then do nothing different. If you want different results, you must do different things. A goal to get more customers means you must message more people, post more often, or offer something new. The goal alone does nothing. The actions do the work.

Do not keep your goals secret from everyone. Tell a trusted friend, a family member, or a business partner. Ask them to check on your progress each month. Accountability helps. When someone else knows your goal, you are more likely to work towards it.

Do not give up after one bad month. Business in Nigeria is unpredictable. A bad month does not mean your goal is impossible. It means you need to adjust your plan and keep going. The businesses that succeed are the ones that keep trying after a setback.

## A Real Example: A Tailor in Abuja Sets Three Goals

A tailor in Garki, Abuja, makes clothes for women and children. For years, she worked hard but never planned. Her income went up and down, and she never knew why.

In January, she wrote down her numbers. She served about 40 customers a month and made about 120,000 naira profit. She set three goals for the next three months. Money: increase profit to 180,000 naira a month. Customers: get 20 new customers through referrals and Instagram. Business: create a simple price list and a booking system on WhatsApp, so customers could see prices and book a fitting without calling many times.

She broke each goal into weekly actions. Every Monday, she posted her work on Instagram and WhatsApp Status. Every Wednesday, she messaged five old customers. Every Friday, she asked two happy customers for a referral. She tracked her numbers every Sunday evening.

After three months, she had not reached all her goals, but she had made real progress. Her profit rose to 160,000 naira a month. She gained 15 new customers. Her price list saved her time, and customers liked knowing the price before they called. She reviewed her goals, set new ones, and kept going. Her business was growing, slowly and steadily, because she had a plan and she followed it.

## Your Goal-Setting Worksheet

Use this simple worksheet to set your own goals today. You can copy it into a notebook.

My business today: write your monthly sales, profit, and number of customers.

My three goals for the next three months: write one money goal, one customer goal, and one business goal. Each goal must have a number and a date.

My weekly actions: for each goal, write two or three small actions you will do every week.

My review date: choose one day each month to review your progress. Write it in your calendar or your phone.

My accountability partner: write the name of one person you will tell about your goals.

Setting goals is not about writing beautiful plans. It is about choosing a clear direction, breaking it into small weekly steps, and checking your progress every month. Start with three goals, write them down, and do one small action today. A business with clear goals and steady weekly action will grow, even in a difficult economy. Your future business starts with the goal you set today.
