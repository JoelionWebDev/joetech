A graphic designer in Ikeja charges ₦5,000 for a logo that takes her six hours. A writer in Enugu charges ₦10,000 for an article that takes a full day of research and writing. A web developer in Lekki builds a five-page website for ₦50,000 and spends three weeks on it, including revisions, meetings and data costs. At the end of the month, all three are busy, all three are tired, and none of them can pay their rent from freelancing alone.

This is the undercharging trap, and it catches almost every Nigerian freelancer at some point. You start low to win clients. The low price becomes your reputation. Clients refer you to their friends as "the cheap one." You take on more work to make up the money, your quality drops, and you burn out — still broke. The way out is not working harder. It is pricing properly.

This guide shows you how to calculate what your work is really worth, set a rate you can live on, communicate it confidently, and raise it over time without losing the clients who matter.

## Why Nigerian freelancers undercharge

Undercharging is rarely about laziness or lack of skill. It usually comes from four pressures that are very real in Nigeria.

The first is fear. You worry that if you quote ₦150,000, the client will go to someone who quoted ₦40,000. That fear is not irrational — there is always someone cheaper. But the client who chooses purely on price is also the client who will haggle over every revision, pay late, and disappear when a cheaper option appears. Competing on price is a race to the bottom, and the bottom has no profit in it.

The second is the "exposure" trap. A client promises that this job will lead to bigger jobs, or that working for their big brand will bring you more clients. Sometimes that is true. Most of the time it is a polite way of asking you to work for free. Exposure does not pay rent, and a client who will not pay you fairly now rarely pays fairly later.

The third is not knowing your own costs. If you have never added up your data, your transport, your electricity, your software subscriptions and your time, then any price feels like profit. A ₦30,000 job feels like ₦30,000 in your pocket — until you realise you spent ₦12,000 on data and transport to deliver it and 20 hours of your time.

The fourth is comparison with the wrong people. You see another freelancer charging ₦10,000 for the same service and assume that is the market rate. But you do not know their costs, their quality, their experience, or whether they are even making money. Your price should come from your costs and your value, not from a stranger's Instagram post.

## Step one: know your monthly survival number

Before you price a single job, work out what it costs you to live and work for a month. This is your survival number, and every price you quote must help you cover it.

Write down your monthly living costs: rent share, food, transport, airtime and data, electricity, and personal needs. Be honest. If you spend ₦80,000 on food and transport in a month, write ₦80,000, not the ₦40,000 you wish you spent.

Then add your monthly business costs: data for work, software subscriptions, transport to client meetings, phone repairs, internet, and any tools you pay for. A designer might pay ₦5,000 a month for Canva Pro and ₦15,000 for data. A developer might pay for hosting, a domain, and a code editor subscription. Add it all.

Then add savings and tax. Even a small monthly savings target — ₦20,000 — matters, because freelancing has dry months and you need a buffer. If you earn enough to pay tax, set aside a share for it rather than discovering the bill when you cannot pay it.

Add the three numbers together. Suppose your living costs are ₦150,000, your business costs are ₦40,000, and your savings target is ₦20,000. Your survival number is ₦210,000 a month. That is the minimum your freelancing must bring in, on average, for you to survive without going backwards.

Now divide that by the number of hours you can realistically bill in a month. You cannot bill 40 hours a week — you also need time to find clients, reply to messages, learn, rest, and handle admin. A realistic number for a full-time freelancer is 80 to 100 billable hours a month. Divide ₦210,000 by 90 hours and you get about ₦2,333 per hour. That is your floor: your absolute minimum hourly rate. Any work priced below that rate is work that loses you money over time, however busy it keeps you.

## Step two: choose a pricing model that fits the work

There are three main ways to price freelance work, and each fits different kinds of jobs.

Hourly pricing is simple: you charge for the hours you work. It suits open-ended work like ongoing social media management, virtual assistance, or maintenance, where the scope is hard to pin down. If your floor is ₦2,500 an hour, a 20-hour project costs ₦50,000. The risk is that clients worry the hours will balloon, and efficient freelancers get punished for working fast. Track your hours honestly with a simple timer app or a notebook, and agree on an estimate before you start.

Per-project pricing is the most common model for Nigerian freelancers, and usually the best. You quote a fixed price for a defined deliverable: a logo, a website, a set of ten social media designs, a 1,500-word article. The client knows the cost upfront, and you are rewarded for working efficiently. The key is defining the scope clearly in writing: how many pages, how many revisions, what is included and what costs extra. A website priced at ₦150,000 for five pages and two revision rounds is a clear offer. A website priced at ₦150,000 with unlimited revisions is an invitation to months of free work.

Value-based pricing is the most profitable model, and the one experienced freelancers grow into. Here you price based on the value of the result, not the hours it takes. A sales page that helps a business sell ₦2 million worth of products is worth far more than the eight hours it took to write. A brand identity for a company launching a new product line is worth more than the cost of the design files. Value-based pricing requires confidence and a track record, but it is how you move from charging for your time to charging for your impact.

Most freelancers use a mix: per-project pricing for defined jobs, hourly for ongoing work, and value-based pricing for high-impact projects with clients who understand what the work is worth.

## Step three: research the market — then price above the bottom

Once you know your floor, look at what others charge — not to copy the cheapest, but to understand the range. Check freelance platforms, ask colleagues, look at what agencies charge, and notice what clients in Lagos, Abuja and Port Harcourt are actually paying.

You will find a wide range. A logo might go for ₦10,000 from a beginner and ₦250,000 from an experienced brand designer. A basic business website might range from ₦80,000 to ₦800,000 depending on who builds it and what it includes. Your job is not to sit at the bottom of that range. Your job is to find the point in the range where your skill, your costs and your target clients meet.

A useful rule: if every client accepts your price immediately, your price is too low. If no client accepts it, your price is too high — or you are talking to the wrong clients. A healthy rate gets accepted by roughly six or seven out of ten serious enquiries. The other three are price shoppers, and losing them is fine.

Remember that clients who pay more also tend to behave better. A client who pays ₦200,000 for a website respects your time, replies to your messages, and gives clear feedback. A client who haggled you down to ₦30,000 often demands the most revisions, pays late, and refers you to friends who also want ₦30,000. Your price filters your clients. Price low and you attract the hardest clients. Price fairly and you attract the best ones.

## How to quote a price with confidence

Quoting is a skill, and it gets easier with practice. The structure matters more than the number.

Always quote in writing — WhatsApp message or email — never only by voice call. A written quote can be referred back to, and it protects you when the client "forgets" what was agreed. State the price, what it includes, how many revisions are covered, the timeline, and the payment terms. For example: "Logo design: ₦75,000. Includes three concepts, two revision rounds, and final files in PNG, JPG and PDF. Delivery in five working days. 50 percent upfront to start, 50 percent on delivery."

Ask for a deposit before you start. Fifty percent upfront is standard for Nigerian freelancers, and it is non-negotiable for new clients. A deposit does three things: it proves the client is serious, it covers your early costs, and it protects you if the client disappears halfway. A client who will not pay a deposit is telling you they may not pay at all. Believe them.

Never apologise for your price. Do not write "I know it is a bit high but..." or "I can reduce it if you want." State the price calmly, explain what the client gets, and let the value speak. If the client says it is too expensive, ask which part of the scope they would like to reduce — fewer pages, fewer revisions, a simpler design — rather than cutting your rate for the same work. Reducing scope preserves your value. Discounting your rate teaches the client that your price was never real.

## Handling negotiation without giving your work away

Negotiation is normal in Nigeria, and you should expect it. The trick is to negotiate on scope, not on your rate.

When a client says "Can you do it for ₦40,000 instead of ₦60,000?" you can reply: "For ₦40,000 I can deliver the logo with one concept and one revision round, delivered in seven days. The full package with three concepts and two revisions is ₦60,000. Which would you prefer?" You have respected their budget without devaluing your work, and you have given them a real choice.

You can also trade, not discount. If a client wants a lower price, ask for something in return: faster payment, a testimonial, a referral to two other businesses, or a longer-term commitment. "I can do ₦50,000 if you commit to three months of monthly designs and pay the first month upfront." That is a negotiation that benefits both sides.

Know your walk-away point before the conversation starts. Decide in advance the lowest price you will accept for this job, and if the client goes below it, decline politely: "Thank you for considering me. I cannot deliver this quality at that price, but I would be happy to work together when the budget allows." A polite no costs you nothing and often brings the client back later with a real budget. A desperate yes costs you weeks of underpaid work and a client who will never pay more.

## Raising your prices over time

Your prices should rise as your skill, speed and reputation grow. A freelancer who charges the same rate for three years is earning less every year, because data, transport and food all cost more than they did.

Raise your prices in small, regular steps rather than one dramatic jump. A 10 to 20 percent increase once or twice a year is easy for clients to accept, especially if you announce it in advance: "From January, my rate for a logo moves from ₦60,000 to ₦70,000, reflecting the new brand guidelines and faster delivery I now offer." Existing clients appreciate the warning, and new clients simply meet the new rate.

Tie each increase to a visible improvement: a new skill, a better tool, faster delivery, a stronger portfolio, or a new service. When you can show a client five logos you designed this year that are clearly better than last year's, the higher price explains itself.

Do not wait for permission to raise your prices. Your costs rise without asking you. Your rates should too. The clients who value your work will stay. The ones who leave over a fair increase were never going to sustain your business anyway.

## Getting paid: the part nobody teaches

A good price means nothing if the money never arrives. Protect yourself with simple habits.

Always agree payment terms in writing before you start. State the total, the deposit, the balance, and when the balance is due — usually on delivery, before final files are handed over. Never send final files, final designs, or live website access before the balance is paid. Send a watermarked preview or a screenshot, and release the final work when the transfer lands. This is not distrust. It is professional practice, and serious clients expect it.

Use transfers, not cash, wherever possible, so there is a record. When a client pays, send a short receipt by WhatsApp: "Received ₦75,000 for logo design. Balance: ₦75,000 due on delivery. Thank you." That message prevents almost every payment dispute.

For international clients, use a reliable payment channel you have tested with a small amount first — your Payoneer account, a domiciliary account, or a platform's own payment system. Agree who bears the transfer fees before you start, and quote in dollars where the client pays in dollars, so a naira swing does not eat your fee.

Chase late payments politely but firmly. A message three days after the due date — "Good morning, just checking on the balance for the website project" — is professional. A client who ignores two reminders needs a firmer message with a clear date. And a client who never pays should not get more work until the old bill is settled, however friendly they are.

## Your pricing checklist

Before your next quote, run through this list. Do I know my monthly survival number? Have I calculated my minimum hourly rate? Is the scope written down, with revisions limited? Is a 50 percent deposit required before I start? Is the price at or above my floor? Have I quoted in writing? If the answer to all five is yes, you are pricing like a professional.

Freelancing in Nigeria can pay well — but only if you charge like someone who intends to make a living from it. Your skill has value. Your time has value. The data, the transport, the late nights, the revisions, the years you spent learning — all of it has value. Price for it, say the price clearly, and let the right clients find you. The cheap clients will find the cheap freelancers. Your clients are the ones who understand that good work is worth paying for, and they are out there waiting for a freelancer confident enough to charge what the work is worth.
