If you design websites in Lekki, write content in Abuja, edit videos in Ibadan, or build apps from your home in Enugu, your clients do not have to be in Nigeria. Thousands of Nigerian freelancers now work for clients in the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada and Europe, and they get paid in dollars, pounds or euros for that work.

Getting paid is often the hardest part. A client agrees to pay you $500 for a project, but then you have to work out how the money will reach you. Will it come by bank transfer? Will your Nigerian bank accept it? How much will you lose in fees? How long will it take? What exchange rate will you get when you convert it to naira?

Many freelancers lose money simply because they chose the wrong payment method, or because they had no written agreement and the client delayed payment. Others fall for scams, or they accept payment through a method that freezes their account.

This guide explains the main ways Nigerian freelancers can receive dollars safely, how to set each one up, what it costs, and how to protect yourself. It covers Payoneer, Wise, PayPal, direct bank transfer and other options, and it gives you a practical checklist for invoicing clients and withdrawing your money in naira.

## Why Getting Paid in Dollars Matters

Earning in dollars is attractive for a simple reason: the naira has weakened against the dollar over time, so the same $500 is worth more in naira today than it was a few years ago. For a freelancer paying rent, buying data and feeding a family in naira, that difference matters.

It also opens up a much bigger market. A client in Lagos might pay ₦150,000 for a website. A client in London or New York might pay $500 or $800 for the same work, because that is a normal price in their market. You do not need to charge foreign clients unfair prices — you only need to charge what the work is worth in their market.

## Step 1: Set Yourself Up Properly Before You Ask for Payment

Before a client pays you, make sure your identity and your accounts are ready. Most payment platforms will ask you to verify who you are before they let you receive money. That usually means a government ID, such as a National ID card, international passport or driver's licence, and proof of your address, such as a utility bill or bank statement.

Use your real name everywhere, and make sure the name on your payment account matches the name on your ID and your bank account. If the names do not match, your payment can be delayed or rejected. If you work under a business name, such as Joetech, keep your personal details consistent as well, and keep your business registration documents ready if a platform asks for them.

Open a domiciliary account if you can. A domiciliary account is a Nigerian bank account that can hold dollars, pounds or euros. Banks such as GTBank, Access Bank, Zenith Bank and First Bank offer them. With a domiciliary account, a client or a platform can send dollars directly to your bank, and you can choose when to convert the money to naira. You will usually need your ID, a utility bill, two passport photographs and a small opening amount. Ask your bank for its current requirements, because they change from time to time.

Keep a separate record for your freelance income. Use a simple spreadsheet or notebook to record every job: the client name, the amount agreed, the date, the payment method, and whether you have been paid. This helps you at tax time, and it helps you chase late payments with facts instead of guesswork.

## Step 2: Choose the Right Payment Method

There is no single best payment method for every freelancer. The right choice depends on where your client is, how much they are paying, and how quickly you need the money. Here are the main options used by Nigerian freelancers.

### Payoneer

Payoneer is the most widely used option for Nigerian freelancers, and for good reason. Many freelance platforms, including Upwork and Fiverr, pay through Payoneer. Many direct clients also use it.

With Payoneer, you get a virtual receiving account in dollars, pounds or euros. You give the client your Payoneer account details, they send the money, and it arrives in your Payoneer balance. From there, you can withdraw it to your Nigerian bank account in naira, or to your domiciliary account in dollars where that is supported.

To set it up, create an account on Payoneer with your real name and email address, verify your identity with your ID, and link your Nigerian bank account. The verification can take a few days, so do it before you need to be paid, not after a client is already waiting to send money.

Payoneer charges fees. There is usually a fee when you receive money from a client, and a fee or a conversion charge when you withdraw to your bank. The exact fees depend on how the money arrives and which currency you withdraw in. Check the fee page in your Payoneer account before you quote a price, and add the fee to your thinking when you set your rate. If a client pays you $200 and Payoneer takes a percentage, you should know in advance how much will actually reach your bank.

One important rule: never create a second Payoneer account if you already have one. Payoneer allows one account per person, and a second account can be closed. If you already have a verified account, use it. If you have a problem with your account, contact Payoneer support instead of opening a new one.

### Wise

Wise, formerly TransferWise, is popular for direct payments from clients in the UK, Europe and the United States. It is known for fair exchange rates and clear fees. You can see the fee and the rate before you send or receive money, which makes it easier to plan.

Wise works well when a client wants to pay you directly by bank transfer. The client sends money through Wise to your Nigerian bank account, and Wise converts it at a rate close to the real market rate. The fee is usually lower than a traditional bank transfer.

Setting up Wise requires identity verification, just like Payoneer. You will need your ID and, in some cases, proof of address. Not every Nigerian freelancer can open a full Wise account with a balance in dollars, so check what is available to you in Nigeria at the time you sign up. Even if you cannot hold a dollar balance, your client can still use Wise to send money to your Nigerian bank.

### PayPal

PayPal is the most famous name in online payments, and many foreign clients will ask if you accept it. The position in Nigeria is more complicated. Nigerians can send money through PayPal, but receiving money into a Nigerian PayPal account has long been restricted. That means a client may not be able to pay you directly into a Nigerian PayPal account.

Some freelancers use a PayPal account registered in another country, or they receive PayPal payments through a third party. That is risky. It can lead to frozen funds, closed accounts and lost money. Do not build your freelance income on a workaround that PayPal can close at any time.

If a client insists on PayPal, explain the position politely and offer Payoneer or Wise instead. Most serious clients will accept another method if you explain it clearly. If the work is through a platform such as Upwork, the platform itself handles payment, so PayPal is not needed.

### Direct Bank Transfer and SWIFT

A client can send dollars directly to your Nigerian domiciliary account by SWIFT transfer. This is a normal bank-to-bank transfer. The client needs your bank name, your account number, the SWIFT code of your bank, and sometimes your bank's address.

SWIFT transfers are reliable, but they can be slow and expensive. A transfer can take three to five working days, and both the sending bank and your bank may charge fees. For a large payment, such as $1,000 or more, SWIFT can still be worth it. For a small payment of $50, the fees can take a large share, so a platform such as Payoneer is usually better.

Always confirm the details with your bank before you give them to a client. A wrong digit in the account number or SWIFT code can delay the payment for weeks. Ask your bank for its exact SWIFT instructions in writing, and copy them exactly when you send them to the client.

### Other Options: Crypto and Payment Links

Some clients offer to pay in cryptocurrency, such as USDT. Crypto can be fast, and it avoids some bank delays. It also carries risk. Prices can move quickly, some exchanges are not reliable, and you need to understand how to convert crypto to naira safely through a trusted exchange or peer-to-peer platform. Only accept crypto if you understand it well, and only for clients you trust. Never accept crypto from a stranger who found you online and wants to pay you before you have done any work — that is a common scam pattern.

You can also use payment links from Paystack or Flutterwave to collect payment in naira from Nigerian clients, and in some cases from foreign clients who pay by card. For a foreign client paying in dollars, Payoneer or Wise is usually simpler and cheaper.

## Step 3: Agree Payment Terms in Writing Before You Start Work

The best payment method will not help you if the client never pays, or pays late. Agree the terms in writing before you start the work, even if the client is friendly and the project is small.

Your written agreement does not need to be a long legal document. A clear email or WhatsApp message that the client confirms is enough for most freelance jobs. It should state:

- Exactly what you will deliver, and by when
- The total fee, in dollars, and what it includes
- How and when you will be paid — for example, 50 percent before you start, and 50 percent when the work is delivered
- What happens if the client asks for extra work beyond what was agreed
- The payment method you will use, and who pays the transfer fees

Asking for a deposit is normal and professional. For a new client, ask for 50 percent upfront. For a large project, you can split payment into three parts: a deposit, a payment at the halfway point, and a final payment on delivery. A client who refuses to pay any deposit is a warning sign. Serious clients expect to pay a deposit.

Send a simple invoice for every job. Your invoice should show your name, your email, the client name, a description of the work, the amount, the payment method and your account details. You can create one in Google Docs, Canva or a free invoice tool, and save it as a PDF. An invoice makes you look professional, and it gives the client everything they need to pay you without asking extra questions.

Keep your messages polite but firm when a payment is late. A short message such as "Hello, just checking in on the invoice for $300 sent on 10 November. Please let me know when I can expect payment" is enough. If the client does not reply after two reminders, stop work on any new tasks for that client until the old invoice is paid.

## Step 4: Protect Yourself from Scams and Lost Money

Freelancers are a target for scams because they work online and they expect payments from strangers. Learn the warning signs.

Be careful if a client wants to pay you far more than your rate without discussion, if they ask you to pay a fee to receive your payment, if they send you a cheque and ask you to send part of it back, or if they ask for your bank login, your card details or your one-time password. No real client needs your password. No real payment platform asks you to pay a fee before you can receive money you have already earned.

Do not start work until you have a written agreement and, for a new client, a deposit. Do not send the final files, the full website login, or the complete design until the final payment has arrived. You can send a preview, a watermarked copy or a screenshot, but keep the final deliverable until you are paid.

Keep records of everything: your agreement, your invoice, your messages with the client, and proof of payment. If a platform such as Upwork is involved, keep all communication inside the platform so there is a record if there is a dispute.

Use strong passwords and two-factor authentication on your Payoneer, Wise, email and bank accounts. If someone gets into your email, they can try to change your payment details and steal your money. Never share a one-time code with anyone, even if they claim to be from your bank or from Payoneer.

## Step 5: Withdraw Your Money and Manage It Well

When the money arrives in Payoneer or Wise, you need to get it into your hands in naira, at a good rate and without losing too much to fees.

Compare the rate before you withdraw. Payoneer and Wise show you the rate they will use. Your bank will use its own rate if you withdraw dollars to a domiciliary account and then convert them. The best option changes from time to time, so check both before you move a large amount. For a small amount, the difference may be small. For $1,000, a small difference in the rate can mean tens of thousands of naira.

Withdraw to the bank account in your own name. Do not withdraw to a friend's account or to an account with a different name. That can cause delays and can look suspicious to the bank.

Keep some of your earnings as savings. Freelance income goes up and down. A good habit is to save at least 20 percent of every payment in a separate savings account, such as a Kuda or Opay savings pocket, or a fixed deposit with your bank. That savings will carry you through a slow month, pay for a new laptop when yours fails, or cover data and transport when work is quiet.

## What to Do If a Payment Is Delayed or Missing

If a payment does not arrive when expected, do not panic. First, check your own account carefully. Look in your Payoneer or Wise account for pending transactions. Check your email, including your spam folder, for a message from the platform or the client. Check that you gave the client the correct account details.

Next, contact the client politely. Ask for proof of payment, such as a receipt or a transaction reference number. A real payment will have a reference you can check with your bank or with the platform.

If the payment was sent through a platform such as Upwork, check the platform's payment status. It may be in a holding period, or it may be waiting for the client to approve the work. If it is still missing after the stated time, contact the platform's support with the job details and the transaction reference.

## Freelancer Payment Checklist

- [ ] Verify your identity on Payoneer and Wise, and link your Nigerian bank account
- [ ] Open a domiciliary account with GTBank, Access Bank, Zenith Bank or First Bank if you can
- [ ] Agree the fee, the deliverables and the payment schedule in writing before you start
- [ ] Ask for a 50 percent deposit from new clients
- [ ] Send a clear invoice with your payment details for every job
- [ ] Compare fees and exchange rates before you withdraw a large amount
- [ ] Save at least 20 percent of every payment for slow months
- [ ] Turn on two-factor authentication on your payment and email accounts
- [ ] Never share your password or one-time code with anyone

Getting paid in dollars as a freelancer in Nigeria is not complicated once you have the right setup. Choose a reliable payment method, agree your terms in writing, ask for a deposit, and keep good records. Do that for every client, and your freelance work will pay you fairly, on time, and in a currency that holds its value.
