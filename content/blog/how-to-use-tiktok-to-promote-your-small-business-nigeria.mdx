Funke sells Ankara dresses from a small shop in Yaba. Every morning she films the same kind of video: she holds up a dress, says the price — ₦28,000 — and tells people to WhatsApp her. She posts seven days a week. Her views hover around 300. Her WhatsApp stays quiet.

Three stalls down, Blessing sells the same kind of dresses. Most days she films herself haggling with fabric sellers at Tejuosho market, or laughing with her tailor when a zip bursts, or showing a customer twirling in a dress that fits perfectly. Once a week, sometimes twice, she posts a dress with a price and says, "Four left in this fabric, WhatsApp me before Friday." Her videos pull 4,000, 9,000 views. Her dresses sell out.

Same market. Same product. Same phone. The difference is not effort. Funke is working harder. The difference is that Blessing knows which of her videos are doing which job — and Funke treats every video like a sales pitch.

Every TikTok video your business posts is doing one of two jobs. It is either building your name, so that strangers remember you and start to trust you, or it is asking for an order, right now, from people who already trust you enough to buy. Both jobs matter. The trouble starts when you confuse them.

## The Two Jobs, Side by Side

Think of your TikTok page the way you think of a physical shop. Awareness is the signboard, the smell of fresh food from the doorway, the owner who greets passers-by by name. Selling is the cashier. Nobody walks into a shop because the cashier shouted at them from the pavement. They walk in because something outside made them curious, then the cashier does her work.

An awareness video answers a question in the viewer's head: who is this person, and why should I care? A selling video answers a different question: what exactly do I get, how much is it, and how do I pay?

Here is a quick way to tell them apart. Cover the price and the "WhatsApp me" line with your thumb. If the video still makes sense — still entertains, still teaches, still shows something real — it is an awareness video. If covering the price leaves nothing behind, it is a selling video. Neither is wrong. A page full of only one kind is.

Awareness videos do slow work. A woman in Abuja watches your video of a botched cake rescue at 11 p.m. She does not order. Two weeks later she sees you again, packing orders at dawn, and she follows you. A month later her sister's birthday comes up and your name is the first one she remembers. That is awareness paying off, and it looks like nothing is happening right up until the day it does.

Selling videos do fast work. They go to people who already know you — your followers, the people who watched three of your videos last week, the woman who saved your last post. They give those people a reason to act today: a price, a limited quantity, a deadline, a way to pay.

## What Awareness Videos Actually Look Like

You do not need to be funny, and you do not need to dance. You need to be specific. General videos get scrolled past; specific ones get watched.

Film the story behind the product. A hair vendor in Onitsha, Chiamaka, filmed herself unboxing a shipment of wigs that arrived with the wrong colours — and what she did about it. The video got 38,000 views, not because wigs are exciting, but because a real problem, handled honestly, is interesting. People who watched it did not buy that day. Plenty of them came back when she posted the corrected stock a week later.

Show your personality and your process. A suya seller in Surulere, Musa, films the fire, the slicing, the pepper mix he refuses to reveal. He never says a price in those videos. He does not need to. By the time someone in Surulere wants suya on a Friday night, his face is already in their head.

Show your customers. A makeup artist in Lekki, Adaeze, films her clients' reactions when they first see themselves in the mirror. No price, no "book now". The reaction does the selling later, silently, every time the video is shared into a group chat.

Notice what these videos have in common: no price, no deadline, no order instruction. They earn attention, and attention is the raw material selling videos need.

## What Selling Videos Actually Look Like

A selling video is short, specific, and bossy in a good way. It tells the viewer exactly what to do, and makes it easy.

It names the product, the price, the quantity, and the deadline in the first few seconds. "This two-piece is ₦35,000. I have six left, sizes 10 to 16. WhatsApp number is in my bio — once they finish, that fabric is gone." That is a selling video. It is not trying to be liked. It is trying to be acted on.

It shows the product clearly, in real light, on a real person, with nothing hiding. A tailor in Aba, Emeka, films every new shirt on his own body before he lists it, turns around slowly, and states the price out loud. Customers trust him because they can see exactly what ₦18,000 buys.

It answers the objection before it is asked. Delivery to Port Harcourt is ₦4,500 and takes three days. Sizes run small, order one up. Every objection you answer in the video is one fewer WhatsApp conversation that ends in silence.

It ends with one instruction, not four. "WhatsApp me" beats "WhatsApp me or DM me or check my website or call this number". One door, clearly marked.

## What Happens When You Mix Them Up

The mix-up runs in both directions, and both cost money.

The first mix-up is shouting prices at strangers. Funke's daily price videos go to people who have never heard of her. They have no reason to trust her ₦28,000 dress over the ten other ₦28,000 dresses on their feed. So they scroll. TikTok reads the scroll as boredom and shows her next video to even fewer people. After a month of this, Funke concludes TikTok does not work. TikTok worked fine. She was asking cold strangers to marry her on the first date.

The second mix-up is the opposite, and it is sneakier: a page full of lovely, warm, funny videos that never once asks for the sale. A baker in Ibadan, Tolu, had 22,000 followers who adored her decorating videos. Her order book was empty. People loved watching her. Nobody knew she took orders, because she never said so. Her videos were doing the awareness job beautifully, and the selling job never showed up. Followers are not customers until you give them a way to become one.

The third mix-up is the half-and-half video: a nice story that ends with a clumsy price shout, or a price video dressed up with a trending sound that has nothing to do with the product. The viewer came for the story and got ambushed by a sales pitch, or came to buy and got a joke instead. Either way they leave slightly annoyed, and slightly annoyed people do not follow and do not buy.

## A Weekly Rhythm That Keeps Both Jobs Done

You do not need a content calendar with colour codes. You need a rough rhythm you can keep while running a real business.

A workable split for most small businesses is five awareness videos to two selling videos each week. Post the awareness videos on the days people are browsing, and put your selling videos where buyers are already in buying mode, typically Friday and Sunday evening, when people are planning the weekend or the week ahead. By Friday, the people seeing the price have already seen your market run, your tailor, and your laughing customer all week. The price is not a cold shout. It is the next step in a story they have been following.

New businesses should lean even further toward awareness — six or seven awareness videos to one selling video — until people know who you are. A brand nobody recognises cannot sell, no matter how good the price is. Once you have a few thousand followers who watch regularly, shift the balance toward selling, because now there is an audience ready to act.

Festive seasons flip the rhythm the other way. In December, or the week before Sallah, people are already looking to buy. That is when selling videos earn their keep: three or four a week, tied to real deadlines — "last dispatch before Christmas is the 20th", "Eid orders close Thursday". When the buying season passes, slide back to awareness. The rhythm follows the customer's mood, not yours.

## Reading the Numbers Without Fooling Yourself

Awareness and selling videos are judged by different numbers, and judging one by the other's scorecard is how businesses make bad decisions.

An awareness video is working when people save it, share it, follow you after watching it, and watch it to the end. Shares matter most here. A share means someone thought of a specific friend while watching — that is a personal recommendation, and it is worth more than a thousand passive views.

A selling video is working when it brings WhatsApp messages, orders, and money. Views barely matter. A selling video with 800 views and nine orders beat a selling video with 40,000 views and one order. The 40,000-view video entertained strangers. The 800-view video reached the right 800 people.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many selling posts is too many?

If more than half your videos in a week carry a price and an order instruction, you are probably overdoing it. Your followers will start to feel like a mailing list, and mailing lists get ignored. Pull back to one or two selling videos a week and watch your views recover.

### Can one video do both jobs?

It can try, and it usually does both badly. A video that tells a story and then tacks on a price at the end keeps neither audience happy. Do the jobs separately and each video gets to be good at its one thing.

### What if my awareness videos get views but no sales?

Give it time, and check the bridge. Are your selling videos actually appearing — with a price, a deadline, and one clear instruction? Are your WhatsApp links working? Awareness without a selling step is a crowd with no till. The views were never the problem.

## Start With This Week

Look at your last ten videos and sort them into two piles: the ones that built your name, and the ones that asked for an order. Most small businesses find the pile is lopsided — usually all selling, sometimes all awareness. That lopsided pile is your answer.

This week, post five videos that show who you are — your market run, your process, a customer, a lesson, a mistake you fixed — and two that ask for the order, with a price, a quantity, and a deadline. Write down what each one brings in. By Sunday you will know, from your own numbers rather than anyone's advice, which job your page has been neglecting.
