Every business owner who has hired a designer knows the frustration: you explain what you want, the design comes back, and it is not what you had in mind. Then come the revisions, the misunderstandings, and the quiet suspicion that the designer did not listen. In most cases, the designer did listen. The problem is that the brief was vague, and a vague brief leaves the designer guessing.

A good brief is not a long document. It is a clear set of answers to the questions a designer needs before they start. This guide walks through those questions, with examples from Nigerian small businesses, so your next design project comes back closer to what you imagined, with fewer revisions and less wasted time.

## Start with the goal, not the design

The most common briefing mistake is starting with the deliverable instead of the goal. "I need a flyer" is not a brief. A flyer for what, for whom, and to achieve what? A flyer to announce a new branch opening is different from a flyer to sell tickets for an event, even if both are the same size and printed on the same paper.

Begin your brief by writing one sentence that states the goal. For example: "We want more people in Ikeja to know our pharmacy now offers home delivery." Or: "We want visitors at our trade fair stand to remember our catering brand and contact us afterwards." Or: "We want our new skincare line to look premium enough for a supermarket shelf."

That sentence does two things. It tells the designer what success looks like, and it helps you judge the finished work. If the design does not serve that goal, it does not matter how attractive it is. Share the goal in writing, not just in conversation, so both sides can refer back to it.

## Describe your audience in plain terms

A designer cannot design for "everyone." A design aimed at young professionals in Lagos will look different from one aimed at parents choosing a school for their children, or at corporate clients choosing a training provider. Your brief should describe your audience as specifically as you can.

Useful details include age range, location, and what the audience cares about. "Busy mums in Lekki who order food on WhatsApp" is a useful description. "Women aged 25 to 45 in Abuja who value natural ingredients" is useful. "People who want quality" is not, because every business says that, and it tells the designer nothing.

## Say what to say, and what not to say

Designers are not mind readers, and they should not have to guess your message. Your brief should include the exact words that must appear in the design, and the main message in one short sentence.

List the must-have text separately from the nice-to-have text. Must-have text includes your business name, the offer, the price if you want it shown, the date of an event, and your contact details. Nice-to-have text includes slogans, extra benefits, and secondary information. When space is tight, the designer needs to know what can be cut and what cannot.

Provide the text in final form, not as rough notes. If you write "put something about quality here," the designer will invent wording that may not match your brand. If you write the exact headline you want, the designer can focus on making it look good. The same applies to contact details: give the exact phone number, WhatsApp link, website address, and social media handle you want shown, and double-check them before sending the brief.

Also list anything that must not appear. That might include an old phone number, a discontinued product, a partner logo you no longer use, or a claim you cannot support. A short "do not include" list prevents embarrassing mistakes that are expensive to fix after printing.

## Show examples, and explain what you like about them

Examples are the fastest way to communicate taste. If you have designs you admire, share them, but do not just send pictures with no explanation. Tell the designer what you like about each one: the colours, the clean layout, the bold headline, or the friendly tone. That explanation turns a picture into guidance.

If you already have brand materials, such as a logo, brand colours, or fonts, share them at the start, in the best quality you have. A logo sent as a small, blurry image from WhatsApp cannot be used well in print. If you do not have brand guidelines yet, say so, and ask the designer to propose colours and fonts as part of the work. That is a normal request, and it is better than pretending guidelines exist when they do not. For help on what brand guidelines should contain, see our guide on [a brand guidelines document for a small business](/blog/brand-guidelines-document-small-business).

## Agree on deliverables, formats, and timing

Many design disputes are not about taste at all. They are about what was supposed to be delivered, in what format, and by when. Your brief should settle these questions before work begins.

List every item you need. A logo project might need a main logo, a simplified version for small sizes, and versions for light and dark backgrounds. A social media project might need a set number of post designs in a specific size. A print project needs to state the size, such as A5 flyer or a roll-up banner. Vague requests such as "some designs for Instagram" lead to mismatched expectations.

State the file formats you need. For print, designers usually supply high-resolution files suitable for a printer. For social media, you need correctly sized images. For your website, you may need versions that load quickly. If you are unsure what formats you need, say where each item will be used, and ask the designer to recommend the right formats.

Agree on timing and revisions in advance. State your deadline, and ask how many revision rounds are included. Two or three rounds is common. A revision round means a set of changes based on your feedback, not a completely new design each time. If you expect unlimited revisions, say so before the project starts, because that affects the price and the designer's schedule.

## A checklist you can use today

Before you send your next brief, run through this list. If you can answer every item, your designer has what they need.

- What is the goal of this design, in one sentence?
- Who is it for, described in plain terms?
- Where will it appear: print, social media, website, packaging, or somewhere else?
- What is the main message, and what exact text must appear?
- What must not appear?
- What examples do you like, and what specifically do you like about them?
- What brand materials can you share: logo, colours, fonts?
- What items do you need, in what sizes and formats?
- What is the deadline, and how many revision rounds are included?
- Who will give feedback, and how will it be collected?

## FAQ

### How long should a design brief be?

One or two pages is plenty for most small business projects. A brief that is too long gets skimmed, and a brief that is too short leaves questions unanswered. Focus on the checklist above, and write short, direct answers rather than long paragraphs.

### What if I do not know what style I want?

That is normal, and a good designer can help you discover it. Share examples of designs you like and dislike, describe your audience and your goal, and ask the designer to present two or three different directions at the start. Choosing between directions is much easier than describing a style from nothing.

### Should I tell the designer my budget?

Yes, if you can. Knowing the budget helps the designer propose a realistic scope, such as how many items and revision rounds are included. A designer who knows the budget can also tell you honestly if what you want costs more, so you can adjust the scope before work begins rather than halfway through.

### What do I do if the first design still is not right?

Give specific feedback tied to your brief. Instead of "I don't like it," say what is not working: the headline is hard to read, the colours feel too dark for our audience, or the contact details are missing. Specific feedback tied to the goal gives the designer something they can actually fix.

Good design is a partnership. Your job is to bring clarity about your business, your audience, and your goal. The designer's job is to turn that clarity into work that catches attention and moves people to act. Write the brief well, and you give that partnership the best possible start.
