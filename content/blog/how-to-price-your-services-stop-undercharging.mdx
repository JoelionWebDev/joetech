You finish a project, the client is delighted, and you send the invoice. Then you look at the amount and feel a small sting — because you know, deep down, that the work was worth more than what you charged. If that feeling is familiar, you are undercharging, and you are not alone. Many Nigerian freelancers, designers, developers, writers, and small business owners charge far too little for the value they deliver.

Undercharging is not humility. It is a slow leak in your business. It means you work longer hours for less money, you cannot afford to invest in better tools or training, and you end up resenting the very clients you worked hard to win. The good news is that pricing is a skill, and like any skill, it can be learned.

## Why So Many of Us Undercharge

Undercharging usually starts with fear. You worry that if you charge more, the client will walk away. You worry that someone else will do it cheaper. You worry that you are not "good enough" yet to charge a proper rate. So you quote low, win the job, and then work twice as hard to make the money add up.

There is another cause that is rarely talked about: many of us never actually calculate what our work costs us. We quote a number that feels right, or we copy what a friend charges, or we accept whatever the client suggests. None of that is pricing. Pricing starts with knowing your costs, and most undercharging happens because the real costs were never counted.

## Start With Your Real Costs

Before you can set a price, you need to know what it costs you to do the work. Sit down and add up your monthly business costs: internet and data, electricity or generator fuel, software subscriptions, transport to meet clients, phone calls, and any help you pay for. Then add your living costs — rent, food, transport, and savings — because your business must pay for your life, not just your tools.

Next, work out how many hours you can realistically bill in a month. If you work eight hours a day, you will not bill all eight. Some hours go to finding clients, answering messages, learning, and admin. A realistic figure for many freelancers is around 80 to 100 billable hours a month. Divide your total monthly costs by your billable hours and you have your minimum hourly rate — the rate below which you are working at a loss. For example, if your monthly costs come to ₦300,000 and you can bill 100 hours, your minimum rate is ₦3,000 per hour. Anything below that means the business is eating into your savings.

This number is your floor, not your price. Your price should sit above it, because a business also needs profit — money to grow, to handle slow months, and to reward you for the risk you take. If your floor is ₦3,000 an hour, charging ₦5,000 or ₦6,000 an hour for skilled work is not greedy. It is what keeps the business alive.

## Three Simple Ways to Set Your Price

There is no single correct way to price a service. These three methods are used by freelancers and agencies everywhere, and you can use whichever fits the job.

### 1. Cost-plus pricing

Start with your costs and add a profit margin. If a website takes you 20 hours and your rate is ₦5,000 an hour, the labour cost is ₦100,000. Add the cost of hosting, a domain, and any stock images, then add a margin — say 20 to 30 percent — for profit. This method is simple and honest, and it guarantees you never work at a loss.

### 2. Market pricing

Look at what others in your field charge for similar work. Ask colleagues, check job boards and freelance platforms, and pay attention to what clients in your market expect. Market pricing helps you stay competitive, but be careful: copying the cheapest price in the market is how undercharging spreads. Use the market as a guide, not as a ceiling.

### 3. Value-based pricing

Price according to the result the client gets, not the hours you spend. A landing page that brings a business new customers every week is worth far more than the hours it took to build. When the value is high, the price should reflect it. This is the hardest method to learn and the most rewarding, because it frees you from the clock entirely.

For most jobs, a mix works best: use your costs to set the floor, check the market to stay realistic, and adjust upward when the value to the client is clearly high.

## Stop Selling Hours, Start Selling Results

Charging by the hour punishes you for being good. The faster and better you get, the less you earn per job. That is backwards. Clients do not really buy your hours; they buy an outcome — a website that brings in orders, a brand that looks trustworthy, a campaign that fills a hall.

Whenever you can, quote a fixed project price instead of an hourly rate. A project price lets you earn more as you get faster, and it gives the client certainty about what they will pay. When you quote, describe what the client gets: the pages, the features, the revisions, the delivery date. A clear scope protects you from endless extra work, and it helps the client see why the price is fair.

## How to Raise Your Prices Without Losing Clients

Raising your prices feels risky, but it is one of the healthiest things a business can do. Here is how to do it calmly.

First, raise prices for new clients before you raise them for existing ones. Quote your new rate on the next enquiry that comes in. That way you practise the new price without risking a current relationship.

Third, raise your prices a little at a time if a big jump feels too much. A 10 or 15 percent increase every few months is easier to accept — for you and for your clients — than doubling overnight.

And accept that some clients will leave. That is normal, and it is often a good thing. The clients who leave over a fair price are usually the ones who were costing you the most time. The space they leave is room for better clients.

## What to Say When a Client Says "Too Expensive"

Almost every freelancer hears this. It is rarely the end of the conversation. Here is how to handle it.

Do not apologise for your price, and do not drop it immediately. Instead, ask a question: "What budget did you have in mind?" or "Which part of the work would you like to adjust?" Often the client has a number in mind, and once you know it, you can adjust the scope — fewer pages, fewer revisions, a simpler design — instead of cutting your rate.

If the budget genuinely cannot stretch, it is fine to say no politely. "I understand, and I would rather be honest than take on work I cannot do well at that price." Saying no protects your time for clients who will pay fairly — and it often brings the client back later, when they are ready.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I know if I am undercharging?

If you are always busy but never have money left at the end of the month, you are undercharging. Other signs: clients never question your price, you feel resentful while doing the work, or you cannot afford to take a day off.

### Should I charge the same as my competitors?

Use competitors as a guide, not a rule. If your work is better, faster, or more reliable, you can charge more. If you are just starting out, you may charge a little less while you build a portfolio — but set a date to review your prices, so a beginner rate does not become a permanent one.

### Is it okay to charge different clients different prices?

Yes, within reason. Prices can vary with the size of the job, the urgency, and the value to the client. A large company that will earn millions from your work can fairly pay more than a small startup. What matters is that every price covers your costs and reflects the value delivered.

### What if I lose clients when I raise my prices?

Some clients will leave, and that is expected. The ones who stay are the ones who value your work. In practice, most freelancers find that a fair price increase loses very few good clients — and the higher income more than makes up for the ones who go.

## Charge What the Work Is Worth

Your price is a message. A price that is too low tells clients your work is not worth much, and it tells you the same thing every time you look at your bank balance. Do the maths, know your floor, price for value, and raise your rates with confidence.
