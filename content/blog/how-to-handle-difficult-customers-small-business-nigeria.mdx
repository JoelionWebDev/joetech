Every Nigerian business owner has a story about a difficult customer. The woman who buys a dress, wears it to a wedding, and returns it the next day demanding a refund. The man who haggles over a price you already reduced twice, then says he will pay "next week" and disappears for a month.

If you run a small business in Nigeria — a shop in Lekki, a food business in Surulere, a fashion brand on Instagram — difficult customers are not a matter of if, but when. One badly handled argument can cost you five customers you never met. The good news is that handling difficult customers is a skill, not a talent. You need a few clear rules, a few practised sentences, and a policy decided in advance.

## Why Difficult Customers Are Part of the Business

It helps to understand why customers become difficult, because the reason is rarely that they woke up wanting to fight you. Many Nigerian customers are under real pressure. Money is tight, prices keep rising, and a purchase that goes wrong can feel like a serious loss. When a purchase they saved for disappoints them, anger is often fear wearing a loud voice.

Some customers are difficult because they have been cheated before. Many buyers have been scammed at least once, so they arrive suspicious. They haggle hard because they assume your first price is inflated. Others are simply used to market culture, where haggling is normal. Understanding this does not mean accepting bad behaviour — it means responding to the situation instead of reacting to the tone.

## Rule One: Stay Calm Before You Say Anything

The first few seconds decide everything. When a customer raises their voice, your body reacts before your brain does, and the words that come to mind are the ones you will regret. The most valuable skill in customer service is the pause.

When a customer confronts you, take a slow breath before you speak. Put your phone down and face them. That pause stops you from saying something in anger that you cannot take back, and it shows the customer you are taking them seriously. Then lower your voice, do not raise it. A calm voice is contagious in the same way shouting is. When you shout back, you turn a complaint into a fight, and the other customers watching decide never to buy from you.

If you feel yourself getting angry, buy time with a sentence: "Let me look into this properly for you." Step away if you can — check your records, drink some water. Train your staff to do the same, with one rule: nobody argues with a customer, ever. If a customer becomes abusive, staff stay polite, listen, and call you.

## Listen First, Even When the Customer Is Wrong

Most difficult customers become much easier once they feel heard. People escalate when they believe nobody is listening, so listen properly — even when you already know the customer is mistaken.

Let the customer finish speaking before you respond. Do not interrupt or correct them mid-sentence. When they pause, repeat back what you heard: "So the dress arrived on Thursday, but the colour was different from the picture, is that correct?" This tells the customer you were listening, and gives them a chance to correct you. Very often, hearing their complaint repeated back calmly halves their anger.

Ask questions instead of making statements. "Can you show me the picture you received?" is better than "That is not what we sent." Questions show you are investigating; statements sound like you have already decided the customer is lying. And when the customer is right, say so quickly: "You are right, we made a mistake, and I am sorry." Customers do not expect perfection. They expect honesty.

## The Customer Who Haggles Over Every Price

Haggling is part of buying and selling in Nigeria, and you should expect it. But there is a difference between normal bargaining and a customer who haggles endlessly or tries to pay less than your cost. You need a clear approach, decided in advance.

First, know your lowest price before the customer arrives — the lowest amount you can accept and still make a profit. Once you know your floor, haggling is simple: be generous down to that line, and firm below it. Owners who suffer most from haggling never decided their floor.

Second, never drop your price without giving a reason. If you reduce the price just because the customer shouted, you have taught them that shouting works. Tie any discount to something real: "I can do ₦45,000 instead of ₦50,000 if you pay in full today." Third, sell value, not just price: "This fabric is original Ankara, not the copy that fades after two washes." Finally, say no with a smile: "I wish I could do that price, but at ₦20,000 I would be selling at a loss." A customer who buys below your cost is not a customer — they are a cost.

## The Customer Who Pays Late or Owes You Money

Late payment is one of the biggest problems for Nigerian small businesses. A customer takes goods on credit, promises to pay "by Friday," and Friday becomes next month — while you need that money to restock, pay rent, and pay staff. Chasing money badly can destroy a relationship, but not chasing it can destroy your business.

Prevent late payment before the sale. State your terms clearly, in writing: "Payment in full before delivery," or "50% deposit, balance on delivery." Put it on the receipt or in the WhatsApp message confirming the order. When terms are written down, there is nothing to argue about later.

Avoid giving credit to new customers — credit to a stranger is a loan with no security. Say: "For first orders, we take full payment before delivery. Once we have done a few orders together, we can discuss terms." When payment is late, follow up the day after the due date: "Good morning, a quick reminder that the balance of ₦35,000 is due." Most late payments are forgetfulness, not dishonesty. If that fails, follow up firmer, with a deadline and a reason — but never shame a debtor publicly. It rarely recovers the money and it damages your reputation.

## The Rude Customer: Setting Boundaries Without Losing the Sale

There is a line between a difficult customer and an abusive one, and you are allowed to draw it. A customer who is frustrated is difficult. A customer who insults your staff, uses abusive language, or threatens you is abusive — and no sale is worth your dignity or your staff's safety.

Set boundaries calmly and early: "I want to help you, but I cannot continue this conversation while you are shouting at my staff. Let us speak calmly so I can fix this." Most people respect a boundary once it is stated. If they continue, repeat it once, then end the interaction: "I am going to step away now. You are welcome to come back when we can talk calmly." Then do it.

Protect your staff. Your sales assistant, rider, or tailor is not paid to be shouted at. When a customer abuses your staff, step in and take over, then tell them they did the right thing by staying calm. Staff who feel protected stay; staff left to face abuse alone leave, and good staff are harder to replace than difficult customers. Watch for patterns, too: one rude outburst is a bad day, but repeated rudeness tells you who this person is.

## Refunds, Returns and Complaints: Have a Policy Before You Need One

Most refund arguments happen because there was no policy — or because it existed only in the owner's head. The customer assumed one thing, the seller another, and the argument began. A written policy, decided before any problem arises, prevents most of these arguments.

Write your policy down and share it before the sale. A few lines on your WhatsApp catalogue, your Instagram bio, your receipt, or a small notice in your shop is enough: "Items can be exchanged within 3 days if unworn and with the receipt. No refunds on custom-made items. Delivery fees are not refundable." Short, clear, and shared before payment — that is all it takes.

Be clear about what is refundable and what is not. Custom-made items — a tailored dress, a banner with the customer's name — generally cannot be resold, so most businesses do not refund them. Say so before you start the work. But a faulty product, a wrong item, or something that does not match what was advertised should be exchanged or refunded without a fight, because that is your mistake, not the customer's.

When a refund request comes in, follow a simple process: listen and find out what went wrong, check your records, then decide based on your policy — not on how loud the customer is. If the policy says refund, refund it, even if the customer was rude. If it says no refund, explain calmly and offer an exchange, a repair, or store credit. ## When the Complaint Goes Public: WhatsApp Status and Instagram

A private complaint is a problem. A public complaint is a reputation event. Many customers post on WhatsApp status or Instagram stories before speaking to you directly. How you respond matters enormously, because your response is for everyone watching, not just the complaining customer.

The first rule is speed. Respond quickly, publicly, and calmly — do not ignore a public complaint and hope it disappears, because it will be screenshotted and shared. A fast, professional reply shows you take complaints seriously: "Thank you for bringing this to our attention. We are sorry. Please send us a DM with your order details so we can resolve this today." You have apologised, shown action, and moved the conversation to a private channel.

Never argue in public. Never post their private messages, phone number, or address. Never call them a liar in a comment, even if they are lying. The audience is not judging who is right — they are judging how you behave under pressure.

Once you have replied publicly, follow up privately. Message the customer, find out what happened, check your records, and fix it if you can. If the complaint was fair, fix it and say so. If it was unfair, explain the facts calmly, with evidence — a delivery photo, a chat record, a receipt.

## Scripts: Exactly What to Say

Knowing what to say in the moment is hard, especially when you are upset. Practise a few sentences in advance.

When a customer is shouting: "I understand you are upset, and I want to fix this. Can you tell me exactly what happened so I can help?" It acknowledges the emotion, promises action, and asks for facts.

When a delivery is late: "I am sorry your order is late. I know you were expecting it today. Let me check with the rider right now and give you an exact time." Apologise first, without blaming the rider or the traffic. The customer does not care whose fault it is — they care when their order will arrive.

When a customer wants a price you cannot accept: "Thank you for your patronage. The lowest I can do is ₦40,000, because at less than that I would be selling at a loss." Short, warm, and final.

When a customer asks for credit: "For first orders, we take payment before delivery. After we have done a few orders together, we can talk about payment terms."

When a customer returns a used item and demands a refund: "Our policy is that worn items cannot be refunded, but I can offer you an exchange for another size or style." You have said no to the refund, yes to a solution.

When a customer insults your staff: "I want to help you, but I cannot continue while you are speaking to my staff this way. Let us talk calmly, or message me on WhatsApp."

Practise these out loud, or write them on a card near your counter. Having the words ready is what separates a business that handles complaints well from one that loses its temper.

## When to Let a Customer Go

Not every customer is worth keeping. This is a hard truth, because every sale feels precious when money is tight. But some customers cost you more than they pay you: they take hours of your time, abuse your staff, demand refunds for items they used, and pay late every time. Keeping them is not loyalty. It is a slow loss.

Here is how to recognise one. They are rude to your staff repeatedly. They haggle every price below your cost and still complain. They take goods on credit and never pay on time. They threaten public complaints to get free products. One of these alone might be manageable. Several together, over and over, is a pattern — and patterns do not change because you were patient one more time.

Letting a customer go does not mean fighting with them. It means quietly changing the terms: no more credit, no more discounts, no more priority over loyal customers. Most will drift away on their own once the special treatment stops. If you do need to say it directly, keep it simple: "Thank you for your patronage. Unfortunately, I do not think we are the right fit for your needs." The businesses that last are not the ones that keep every customer — they are the ones that keep the right customers.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I stay calm when a customer is shouting at me?

Take one slow breath before you speak, lower your voice, and use a sentence that buys you time, such as "Let me look into this properly for you." Focus on the problem, not the tone.

### Should I always give the customer a refund when they complain?

No. Follow your written refund policy, not the volume of the complaint. Refund or exchange quickly when the mistake was yours. You are not obliged to refund custom-made items, worn clothes, or products the customer damaged. When you cannot refund, offer an exchange, a repair, or store credit.

### What do I do when a customer complains about my business publicly?

Respond quickly, publicly, and calmly. Apologise briefly, show you are taking action, and invite the customer to DM you their order details. Never argue in public or post their private messages — everyone watching is judging how you behave under pressure.

### How do I handle a customer who always haggles and never accepts my price?

Know your lowest price before the negotiation starts and never go below it. Tie any discount to something real, like full payment today, and explain the value behind your price. A customer who only buys below your cost is not a customer; they are a loss.

### Is it okay to refuse to serve a customer?

Yes, when a customer is abusive, threatening, or repeatedly disrespectful. Do it calmly: explain you cannot continue while they are speaking abusively, and invite them to return when the conversation can be calm.

### How do I collect money from a customer who keeps promising to pay?

Follow up with a polite written reminder the day after the due date. If that fails, follow up again with a firm deadline and a reason, such as needing to settle your own supplier. Put payment terms in writing before the sale, avoid credit for new customers, and stop offering it to repeat late payers. Never shame a debtor publicly.

## What to Do Next

First, write down your refund and return policy today — a few clear lines about exchanges, refunds, custom orders, and time limits — and share it where customers can see it: your WhatsApp catalogue, Instagram bio, or receipts. A policy decided before a problem arrives prevents most arguments.

Second, practise three scripts out loud: one for a shouting customer, one for a price you cannot accept, and one for a late payment reminder. Share them with your staff and agree on one rule: nobody argues with a customer, ever.

Third, identify one customer who costs you more than they pay you, and change their terms this week: no more credit, no more special discounts, no more priority. That frees your time for customers who pay on time and respect your business.
