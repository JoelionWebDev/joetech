Every day, Nigerians shop online, apply for jobs, log into bank apps and register for services on websites they have never visited before. Most of those sites are genuine. But some are fakes built to steal your password, your card details or your money. The good news is that you do not need to be a tech expert to tell the difference. A few quick checks — ones that take less than a minute — will protect you most of the time.

Fake websites, often called phishing sites, are designed to look exactly like the real thing. They copy the logo, the colours and the layout of a bank, a shopping site or a government portal, then send you a link by SMS, WhatsApp or email. Once you type in your details, the criminals behind the site have them. This guide shows you how to check any website before you enter your details, using simple signs anyone can spot.

## Start With the Address Bar: Look for HTTPS and the Padlock

The first thing to check is the address at the top of your browser. A safe website address starts with "https://" — the "s" stands for secure. It means the connection between your phone or computer and the website is encrypted, so nobody on the same Wi-Fi can easily read what you send. Most browsers also show a small padlock icon beside the address. Tap or click the padlock and it will tell you whether the connection is secure.

But here is the important warning: HTTPS alone does not mean a website is genuine. Criminals can also get HTTPS certificates for their fake sites, and many do. The padlock only tells you the connection is encrypted — it says nothing about who is running the site. So treat HTTPS as a minimum requirement, not a guarantee. A site without HTTPS, showing "Not Secure" in the address bar, is a strong reason to leave immediately, especially if it asks for any personal details.

Next, read the domain name itself very carefully. This is where most fake sites give themselves away. Scammers register addresses that look almost identical to real ones: "gtbank-online.com" instead of the bank's real address, "jumia-deals.ng" instead of jumia.com.ng, or "faceb00k.com" with zeros instead of the letter o. Look for extra words like "login", "secure", "verify" or "support" added to a familiar name, strange spellings, and endings you do not expect. If a link came to you in a message, never trust the text of the link — tap and hold it (or hover over it on a computer) to see the real address it points to before you open it.

## Check the Website Itself for Signs of a Real Business

A genuine business website usually tells you who it is. Scroll to the bottom of the page and look for a footer with a real company name, a physical address, a phone number and an email address. Click on the "About Us" and "Contact Us" pages and read them. A real company will describe what it does and how to reach it. A fake site often has empty or copied contact pages, a contact form with no address, or a phone number that never answers.

Also look at the quality of the site. Poor spelling and broken English across the whole site, images that do not load, buttons that go nowhere, and pages full of copied text are all warning signs. Real businesses invest in their websites. That said, a polished design alone proves nothing — some fake sites look very professional — so use this check together with the others, not on its own.

Check how the site handles payment. A genuine Nigerian online store will usually offer familiar payment options: Paystack, Flutterwave, bank transfer to a company account, or pay on delivery. Be careful if a site only accepts direct transfers to a personal account, asks you to pay with gift cards or cryptocurrency, or rushes you to pay immediately. And never enter your card PIN, your bank's one-time password (OTP), or your BVN on any website — no genuine website will ever ask for those.

## Search the Name and Read What Other People Say

Before you trust a new website, search for it. Type the company name into Google along with words like "review", "scam" or "complaint". If other people have been cheated by the site, you will often find their stories on social media, forums or review platforms. No results at all can also be a warning sign: a business that claims to be big and popular but has no footprint anywhere online is suspicious.

Check the site's social media pages too. A real business usually has a Facebook or Instagram page with real posts, real followers and real comments from customers over a period of time. A page created a few days ago with only a handful of posts and no genuine customer interaction deserves caution. You can also check how old the website itself is by searching for its domain on a free domain lookup tool — a site claiming ten years of experience but registered last week is lying.

If the website belongs to a bank, a government agency or a well-known company, do not use the link that was sent to you at all. Close the message, open your browser, and type the address yourself — or use the official app you already have installed. This one habit defeats most phishing attempts, because the fake link is the whole trick.

## Watch Out for Pressure, Prizes and Too-Good Deals

Scammers rely on urgency. If a website or message tells you your account will be closed in 24 hours, that you have won a prize you never entered for, or that a deal ends in the next ten minutes, slow down. Real banks do not threaten to close your account by SMS link. Real companies do not give away free phones to random phone numbers. Urgency is a tool to stop you thinking — and thinking is exactly what protects you.

The same applies to prices that make no sense. A brand-new iPhone selling for a fraction of its normal price, a car far below market value, or a "work from home" offer promising huge pay for almost no work are all classic traps. If a deal looks too good to be true, it almost always is. Compare the price with two or three other sellers before you pay anything.

Also be careful with downloads. A website that asks you to download an app or a file before you can continue — especially an app outside the Google Play Store or Apple App Store — is a serious red flag. Those downloads can be malware that steals your passwords or locks your phone. Only install apps from the official app stores, and only after checking the developer name and reviews.

## What to Do If You Already Entered Your Details

If you realise you have entered your password or card details on a suspicious site, act fast. First, change your password on the real website immediately, from a device you trust, and change it anywhere else you used the same password. Turn on two-factor authentication wherever it is offered — it means a stolen password alone is not enough to get into your account.

If you entered card details, call your bank at once and ask them to block the card and watch the account for strange transactions. If you sent money to a scammer, report it to your bank immediately and to the police, and keep screenshots of the website, the messages and the payment as evidence. Quick action can limit the damage. And tell people around you what happened — sharing your experience may stop the same scam from catching a friend or family member.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does the padlock icon mean a website is safe?

Not on its own. The padlock only means your connection to the site is encrypted. A fake site can have a padlock too. Use it as a basic check — a site without HTTPS is unsafe — but always check the domain name, the business details and other people's reviews as well.

### How can I check if an online store in Nigeria is genuine?

Check that the domain name matches the real business, look for a physical address and working phone number, search the business name with the word "review" or "scam", and check its social media pages for real customer activity. Prefer stores that use familiar payment providers like Paystack or Flutterwave, or offer pay on delivery.

### What should I never enter on a website?

Never enter your card PIN, your bank OTP, your BVN, or your full online banking password on a site you reached through a link in a message. Your bank will never ask for your PIN or OTP on a website, and no genuine site needs your BVN to sell you something.

### I clicked a suspicious link but did not enter anything. Am I safe?

Probably, but stay careful. Simply visiting a site rarely causes harm on its own, but do not download anything it offers and do not enter any details. If your phone starts behaving strangely afterwards, run a security scan, clear your browser data, and consider changing your important passwords as a precaution.

Staying safe online is mostly a habit, not a skill. Check the address bar, read the domain name carefully, look for real business details, search before you trust, and never let urgency rush you into typing your details. Those five habits will protect you from the vast majority of fake websites.
