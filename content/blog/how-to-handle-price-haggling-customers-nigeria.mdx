"Last price?" Those two words can make a Nigerian seller's heart sink. You have just stated your price — ₦25,000 for a bag, ₦15,000 for a dress, ₦350,000 for a website — and the customer immediately asks you to cut it. Sometimes they offer half. Sometimes they say another seller is selling it cheaper. Sometimes they promise to bring many customers if you reduce the price today.

If you give in every time, your business will slowly die. You will work hard, sell a lot, and have nothing left at the end of the month. If you refuse rudely every time, you will lose customers who would have bought happily at a fair price. The skill is in the middle: stay calm, stay friendly, protect your profit, and still close the sale.

Haggling is not an insult. In Nigeria, bargaining is part of the culture of buying and selling, from Balogun Market to a WhatsApp chat. Many customers haggle out of habit, not because your price is wrong. Some haggle because money is genuinely tight. Some haggle because they want to feel they got a good deal. Your job is to understand which one you are dealing with, and respond in a way that respects them and respects your business.

## Why Customers Haggle — And Why It Is Not Always About Price

Before you learn what to say, it helps to understand why the customer is bargaining. The reason changes the right response.

Some customers haggle because they do not yet see the value. They do not know why your bag costs ₦25,000 when another bag costs ₦12,000. They have not seen the leather, the stitching, the lining, or the warranty. For them, the answer is not a lower price. It is a better explanation.

Some customers are testing you. They want to know if your price is real or if you inflated it expecting a negotiation. If you drop the price by 40 percent in ten seconds, you confirm their suspicion that the first price was a joke. If you hold steady and explain calmly, they learn to trust your prices.

Some customers genuinely cannot afford the full price today. A student, a young mother, or a salary earner waiting for payday may love your product but have a smaller budget. For them, a smaller size, a cheaper option, or a payment plan may be better than a discount.

And some customers will haggle over everything, no matter what. They enjoy the negotiation itself. With them, you need a firm, friendly boundary, or you will spend 30 minutes negotiating a ₦500 discount on a ₦5,000 item.

Knowing the reason helps you stay calm. Haggling is not a personal attack.

## Know Your Lowest Price Before the Customer Asks

The most important rule of haggling is this: decide your lowest price before the conversation starts, not during it. When you are under pressure in a chat, it is easy to agree to a price that loses you money. When you have decided in advance, you can stay calm.

For every product or service, work out three numbers and write them down. First, your cost: what you paid for the item, plus delivery, packaging, data, and any other cost. Second, your listed price: the price you advertise. Third, your floor price: the lowest price you can accept and still make a reasonable profit. Never go below the floor price, no matter how much the customer begs.

Here is an example. You buy a handbag for ₦14,000, pay ₦1,500 for delivery to your shop in Lekki, and ₦500 for packaging. Your total cost is ₦16,000. You list it at ₦25,000. Your profit at full price is ₦9,000. Your floor price might be ₦21,000, which still leaves ₦5,000 profit. If a customer offers ₦18,000, you can say no politely, because you know that price leaves only ₦2,000 for all your time, your rent, your data, and your risk. That is not a good sale.

Build a small margin into your listed price for negotiation, if your market expects it. Listing at ₦25,000 when your floor is ₦21,000 gives you room to offer a small discount and still profit. But do not inflate your price sharply just to allow haggling — customers compare prices, and an inflated price destroys trust.

For services — a website, a logo, catering, makeup — your floor price is even more important, because your time is the cost. A website that takes you two weeks cannot be sold for the price of two days of work. Write down your minimum fee for each service and do not go below it, even if the customer promises future work. Future work that never comes is the most expensive promise in Nigerian business.

## Stay Calm and Friendly — Never Argue About Price

The moment a customer says "That is too expensive," many sellers get defensive. They argue, they raise their voice, or they send a long, angry message. That never works. The customer stops listening, and the sale is lost.

Instead, pause. Take a breath. Then respond with warmth and confidence. Your tone should say: I understand, my price is fair, and I am happy to help you find a way to buy.

Use phrases that keep the conversation friendly. "I understand, and thank you for asking." "That is a fair question." "Let me explain what is included in the price." These phrases buy you time, show respect, and move the conversation from argument to explanation.

Never insult the customer, never say "Go and buy from the cheaper seller then," and never say "You cannot afford it." Even if you are thinking it, saying it ends the relationship and can earn you a bad review in a group chat. A customer you treat well today may return next month with a bigger budget, or refer a friend who can pay full price.

## Explain the Value Before You Discuss a Discount

Most haggling happens because the customer does not yet understand the value. Before you offer any discount, explain what the price includes. This is your strongest tool, and it costs you nothing.

Talk about quality, not just features. Do not say "This is a good bag." Say "This is full-grain leather, the stitching is double, the lining is thick, and I will repair the zip free for six months if it fails." Do not say "This is a good website." Say "Your website will load in under three seconds, work on every phone, rank on Google, and come with a contact form and WhatsApp button."

Talk about what is included. Delivery, installation, training, a warranty, after-sales support, a free gift, or a follow-up call all add value. When a customer compares your ₦25,000 bag with a ₦15,000 bag online, they may not know the cheaper bag has no warranty, thin leather, and delivery that costs ₦5,000 extra. Help them compare fairly.

Show proof. A short video of the product in use, a photo of the label, a customer testimonial, or a before-and-after picture can do more than a long speech. When a customer sees the quality, the price starts to make sense.

Ask a question that shifts the conversation. "What matters most to you — the lowest price, or a bag that will last two years?" Most customers will say they want quality that lasts. Once they say that, you can show them why your price is fair for that quality.

Only after you have explained the value should you discuss a discount. If you discount first, you teach the customer that your price was never real. If you explain first, a small discount feels like a genuine favour, not a confession.

## Smart Ways to Give a Little Without Losing Profit

Sometimes a small concession will close the sale, and it is worth giving. The trick is to give something small, planned and conditional — not a big, panicked price cut.

Offer a smaller discount, not a large one. If your listed price is ₦25,000 and your floor is ₦21,000, offer ₦23,000 first. That is an 8 percent discount, and it feels generous to the customer while protecting your margin. If you jump straight to ₦21,000, the customer will ask for ₦18,000 next, because you have shown that big drops are possible.

Trade a discount for something valuable. "I can do ₦23,000 if you pay today" or "I can do ₦23,000 if you buy two" or "I can do ₦23,000 if you pay by transfer now, so I do not pay a card fee." This way the discount is not free. The customer gets a better price, and you get faster payment, a bigger order, or a saving of your own.

Offer a smaller version instead of a cheaper price. If a customer cannot afford a full hamper at ₦50,000, offer a smaller hamper at ₦30,000. If they cannot afford a full website at ₦350,000, offer a simple landing page at ₦150,000. If they cannot afford a 5-litre soup bowl, offer a 2-litre bowl. The customer still buys, you still profit, and the value of your work is protected.

Add value instead of cutting price. A free delivery within your area, a small free gift, a free extra service, or a longer warranty can feel more generous than a ₦2,000 discount, and it often costs you less. A free ₦1,000 gift that costs you ₦400 feels like a win to the customer and protects your margin.

Use time-limited offers. "This price is valid until Friday" or "I can hold ₦23,000 for you today only." A deadline helps the customer decide, and it stops the negotiation from dragging on for a week. Make sure the deadline is real. If you say "today only" and then offer the same price next week, customers will learn that your deadlines mean nothing.

Set a clear rule for bulk orders. A customer buying 20 packs for an office party or 50 souvenirs for a wedding deserves a better unit price than a customer buying one. Decide your bulk discount in advance — for example, 5 percent off for 10 items, 10 percent off for 20 or more — and publish it. When the rule is clear, the negotiation is short.

## Scripts You Can Copy for Common Haggling Lines

It helps to have words ready before the customer speaks. Here are calm, polite replies you can adapt to your own business. Keep them in your phone notes and copy them when you need them.

When the customer says "Last price?": "Thank you for asking. The last price for this one is ₦23,000. It is original, and it comes with a six-month warranty on the zip. Would you like me to reserve one for you?"

When the customer says "Another seller is selling it for ₦15,000": "Thank you for letting me know. This one is original leather with a warranty and free delivery in Lekki. The cheaper one may be a different quality. I can offer ₦23,000 for you today. Would that work?"

When the customer offers half your price: "I appreciate your offer. Unfortunately, I cannot do ₦12,500, because my cost alone is higher than that. The lowest I can do is ₦21,000. Shall I hold it for you?"

When the customer says "I will bring many customers if you reduce it": "Thank you, I really appreciate that. My price is already fair, and I will give your friends the same good service. For this order, I can offer ₦23,000. When your friends order, I will give them a first-time discount too."

When the customer says "I am a regular, give me a discount": "Thank you for always supporting us. Because you are a regular, I can offer you ₦23,000 today, and I will add a small gift. Thank you for staying with us."

When the customer says "I will think about it": "No problem at all. I will hold the price until Friday for you. Please message me when you are ready, and I will reserve it for you."

Notice the pattern. Each reply thanks the customer, states the price clearly, gives one reason the price is fair, offers a small concession if possible, and ends with a question that moves the sale forward. You are not arguing. You are guiding.

## When to Say No — And How to Say It Politely

Some sales are not worth making. If a customer insists on a price below your floor, the kindest thing you can do is say no politely and let them go. Selling at a loss is not good business. It is a slow way to close your shop.

Saying no does not mean being rude. It means being clear and respectful. "Thank you so much for your interest. Unfortunately, I cannot sell it at ₦15,000, because that is below my cost. The lowest I can do is ₦21,000. If that works for you later, please message me. I will be happy to serve you." That message leaves the door open. Many customers return after checking other sellers and realising your price was fair.

Watch for red flags that tell you to walk away. A customer who insults your product, threatens to damage your reputation if you do not discount, demands free extras worth more than the product, or asks you to deliver before payment with no history of trust is not a customer you need. Protect your peace and your business.

Do not chase a customer who has said no. One polite follow-up is enough. "Hello, just checking if you still need the bag. The ₦23,000 offer is still open until Friday." After that, let it go. Chasing a customer who has decided against you looks desperate and rarely works.

Also, do not lower your price because you feel guilty. Many Nigerian sellers, especially women selling to friends and family, feel guilty charging a fair price to people they know. Remember: your friends should want your business to survive. A friend who insists on a price that loses you money is not supporting you. Offer friends a small, planned discount, such as 5 percent, and hold the line there.

## Mistakes That Make Haggling Worse

- **Dropping your price immediately.** It tells the customer your first price was fake.
- **Quoting a different price to different customers for the same item.** Customers compare notes in group chats, and you will be exposed.
- **Negotiating without knowing your costs.** You cannot protect a profit you have not calculated.
- **Giving a discount with no condition.** A discount for nothing teaches customers to ask every time.
- **Arguing or insulting the customer.** Even if you win the argument, you lose the customer.
- **Promising future discounts you cannot keep.** "Next time I will do it cheaper" creates an expectation you will regret.
- **Selling at a loss to win a customer.** A customer who only buys at a loss is not a customer. They are a cost.
- **Hiding the price until the end.** "DM for price" invites haggling, because the customer has no anchor to trust.

## Build Prices Customers Do Not Want to Haggle Over

The best way to handle haggling is to make it less necessary. Clear, fair pricing, good packaging, and excellent service reduce the urge to bargain.

Publish your prices where customers can see them. A price list on WhatsApp, Instagram, or your website removes the mystery. When the price is public, customers are less likely to ask for a secret discount, because they know everyone pays the same.

Reward loyalty without destroying your price. A loyalty card, a small gift on the fifth order, or a birthday discount shows appreciation without teaching every new customer to demand a discount on the first order.

Keep your quality consistent. A customer who buys a good product at a fair price and comes back to find the same quality will stop haggling, because they know the price is honest. A customer who finds the quality has dropped will haggle harder, because they no longer trust the value.

Haggling will always be part of selling in Nigeria. You cannot stop customers from asking, and you should not try. What you can do is prepare: know your costs, know your floor price, explain your value calmly, offer a small concession only when it makes sense, and say no politely when the price is too low. Do that consistently, and you will close more sales, keep more profit, and earn a reputation as a fair seller — the kind of seller customers return to, and recommend to their friends.
