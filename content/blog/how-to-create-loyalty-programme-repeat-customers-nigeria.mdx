A food vendor in Yaba ran a loyalty card for a year: buy nine plates, get the tenth free. Customers loved it. The cards were always in use, the queue was steady, and she felt proud of it. Then one evening she sat down and did the maths. Each free plate cost her ₦1,800 to make. She was giving away roughly forty free plates a month. That was ₦72,000 a month in free food — and her regular customers, the ones using the cards, were the same people who would have bought from her anyway, card or no card. The programme was not bringing new money in. It was giving away money she already had.

This is the question most small business owners never ask about their loyalty programme: is it making me money, or is it quietly costing me money? A programme can feel successful — full cards, happy customers, busy shop — and still be a leak. This guide shows you how to find out, with real numbers, and how to fix it without angering the customers who love it.

## The one calculation that matters

Forget everything else for a moment. A loyalty programme earns its keep if the extra profit it creates is bigger than the cost of the rewards. That is the whole test. Everything else is decoration.

Work it out with your own numbers. Start with the reward cost. A free plate that costs you ₦1,800 to make is a ₦1,800 reward, even though the customer sees a ₦4,500 plate. A 10 percent discount on a ₦20,000 order costs you ₦2,000. Free delivery to Lekki costs you the rider's fee, say ₦2,500. Write down what each reward actually costs you, not what it is worth to the customer. The gap between those two numbers is where loyalty programmes make sense — a reward that feels like ₦5,000 to the customer but costs you ₦2,000 is a good reward.

Next, work out what a loyal customer must spend to earn it. If the reward is a free plate after nine plates at ₦4,500 each, the customer spends ₦40,500 to earn a reward that costs you ₦1,800. That is 4.4 percent of the revenue. That is fine — as long as the programme is genuinely bringing extra visits. If those nine plates would have been bought anyway, the ₦1,800 is pure loss.

So the real question is: how many of those visits happened because of the programme? You cannot know exactly, but you can estimate. Compare the average visits per customer before the programme and after it. If your regulars came twice a month before and now come three times, the programme created one extra visit per customer per month. Multiply that extra visit by your profit per visit, and compare it with the reward cost. If the extra profit is bigger, the programme pays. If it is smaller, it does not.

A salon in Surulere did this properly. Before her stamp card, her regulars came every three weeks. After it, they came every two weeks, because the eighth haircut was free and they wanted to reach it. Each extra visit was worth about ₦6,000 in profit. The free haircut cost her ₦4,000 in time and products. She gained roughly ₦14,000 in extra profit for every ₦4,000 reward. That programme was working. The food vendor's was not — and the difference was that the salon's reward changed behaviour, while the food vendor's reward paid people for behaviour that was already happening.

## Five numbers to track every month

You do not need software. You need one sheet, checked once a month, with five numbers.

**Members.** How many people have joined? This tells you whether the programme is attractive. If sign-ups stall, the reward is not exciting enough or nobody knows about it.

**Active members.** How many members earned or redeemed something in the last 30 days? This is the number that matters. A programme with 200 members and 15 active ones is a dead programme with a long list. If fewer than one in four members is active, something is wrong — usually the reward is too far away.

**Repeat rate.** Of the customers who bought from you this month, how many had bought before? Compare it with the months before the programme. If the repeat rate is climbing, the programme is doing its job. If it is flat, it is decoration.

**Reward cost.** Add up everything the rewards cost you this month: free products, discounts given, delivery fees covered, cards printed. This is your real monthly bill for loyalty.

**Extra sales.** Estimate the extra sales from members compared with before the programme. Be honest and conservative. If members spent ₦600,000 this month and similar customers spent ₦500,000 a month before the programme, the extra ₦100,000 is your rough gain. Take the profit on that extra ₦100,000 — not the full amount — and compare it with the reward cost. Profit bigger than cost means the programme earns. Cost bigger than profit means it leaks.

Write these five numbers on one page at the end of every month. After three months, you will see the truth clearly, and you will stop guessing.

## Warning signs your programme is losing money

The clearest sign is a reward that is too easy. If customers earn the reward in a week, the reward is not building loyalty, it is a discount with extra steps. A supermarket that gives a free product after three visits is not rewarding loyalty. It is cutting its price for everyone and calling it a programme.

The second sign is staff giving rewards away. A stamp card is only honest if every stamp means a real purchase. If your staff stamp cards for friends, or stamp twice to be nice, the cost quietly doubles. Check your records against your sales. If you gave out forty rewards in a month but only had thirty qualifying purchases, you have a leak, and it is usually a person, not a rule.

The third sign is rewards for customers who would have come anyway. Your oldest, most loyal customers are the worst people to reward with a discount, because they were never going to leave. The programme should change the behaviour of the middle group — the customers who buy sometimes and could buy more often. If all your rewards go to people who were already buying weekly, you are paying for loyalty you already had.

The fourth sign is a reward nobody can reach. This is the opposite problem, and it wastes money differently: you spend on cards, announcements and staff time, and nobody ever redeems anything. The programme costs you the setup and the effort and returns nothing. If fewer than one in ten members has ever redeemed a reward, the target is too far away.

## How to fix a leaking programme without losing customers

The fear that stops most owners from fixing a bad programme is that customers will be angry. They will be — if you change the rules overnight and in silence. They will not be, if you handle it properly.

First, never change the rules for rewards people have already earned. If a customer has eight stamps on a ten-stamp card, let her finish that card under the old rules. Changing the deal halfway is the one thing customers never forgive, and they will tell their friends about it.

Second, give notice. Announce the change a month ahead, in plain words, and give a reason customers can respect: "To keep this programme running for everyone, the free plate now comes after ten plates instead of eight, starting from 1 January. All cards already in use will be honoured under the old rules until they are complete." Most customers accept a change they understand. They reject a change that feels like a trick.

Third, change the reward before you change the threshold. Instead of making the free product harder to reach, swap it for a reward that costs you less but still feels good: free delivery instead of a free product, a free small item instead of a free large one, a discount on a quiet day instead of any day. Customers notice the threshold. They rarely notice the swap, because the new reward still feels generous.

Fourth, add an expiry. Points or stamps that never expire become a growing debt. If a customer has stamps from two years ago, you owe a reward forever. A six-month or twelve-month expiry keeps the programme honest and pushes customers to come back sooner. Announce the expiry clearly when the card is issued, and remind people a month before their points expire — that reminder itself brings them back.

## When to kill a programme

Some programmes should not be fixed. They should be ended. Kill it if the reward cost is more than 10 percent of the revenue from members for three months in a row, and no change to the rules fixes it. Kill it if fewer than one in ten members is active after six months — the programme is not wanted, and keeping it wastes your time. Kill it if it has become a source of arguments: customers disputing stamps, staff accused of favouritism, rewards promised and not delivered. A programme that creates quarrels costs more than money.

Ending it properly matters. Thank the members. Honour every reward already earned. Give a final date, at least a month away, and let everyone redeem what they have. Then replace it with something simpler: a thank-you message, a birthday gift, a first look at new stock. Loyalty does not need a programme. It needs attention. The programme was only ever a way of organising that attention.

## A programme that pays

The food vendor in Yaba did not kill her card. She changed it. The free plate moved from the tenth plate to the twelfth, and she added a second reward: a free drink after five plates, which costs her ₦300 and arrives fast enough to keep customers motivated. She also started stamping only for plates above ₦4,000, so a small side order did not earn the same stamp as a full meal. Three months later, her reward cost fell from ₦72,000 a month to ₦38,000, her regulars still came just as often, and the free drink brought back customers who had drifted to the food seller across the road. Same card. Same customers. Different maths.

That is the whole point. A loyalty programme is not a kindness project. It is a business tool, and like any tool, it should be measured. Cost the rewards. Track the five numbers. Fix what leaks, and keep what earns. Your customers will still feel valued — more so, because the programme will still be there next year, paying for itself instead of quietly draining the business it was meant to grow.
