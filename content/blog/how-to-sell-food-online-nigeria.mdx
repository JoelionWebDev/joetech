Every day in Lagos, Abuja, Port Harcourt and Ibadan, thousands of people scroll through their phones at lunchtime wondering what to eat. They are too busy to cook, too tired to go out, or working from home with no food in the fridge. If your food appears on their screen at that moment, with a clear photo, a clear price, and a delivery option, you have a sale.

Selling food online in Nigeria is one of the most practical businesses you can start from home. You do not need a restaurant, a shop, or a large amount of money. You need a kitchen, a phone, cooking skills, and the discipline to deliver what you promise. Many food vendors in Nigeria started with one pot, a WhatsApp status, and a handful of customers — and grew into businesses that feed hundreds of people every week.

But food is also a business where trust is everything. A customer who orders from you is trusting you with their health and their money. One bad meal, one late delivery, or one dirty pack can end the relationship. A vendor who cooks well, packs neatly, delivers on time, and replies politely will build a loyal customer base that orders again and again.

This guide explains how to sell food online in Nigeria, step by step. You will learn how to choose what to sell, how to price your meals so you make a profit, how to take orders on WhatsApp and other platforms, how to package and deliver food safely, and how to market your food without spending a fortune.

## Why Selling Food Online Works in Nigeria

Food is something everybody needs, every single day. Unlike fashion or gadgets, which people buy occasionally, food sells daily. A customer who loves your jollof rice on Monday may order it again on Wednesday and recommend you to their colleagues on Friday. That repeat demand is what makes a food business stable.

The online market is large and always hungry. Office workers in Lekki, students in Yaba, bankers in Victoria Island, families in Surulere — all of them order food online when they are busy. Social media and WhatsApp have made it easy for a small vendor in a home kitchen to reach these customers without renting a shop on a busy road.

## Choose What to Sell

The first decision is your menu. Do not try to cook everything. A vendor who sells jollof rice, fried rice, ofada, amala, pepper soup, small chops, cakes, and shawarma all at once will struggle to buy ingredients, keep quality high, and remember prices. Start with one or two things you cook very well, and build your name around them.

Pick food that travels well. Some dishes survive delivery better than others. Jollof rice, fried rice, small chops, meat pies, and soups in sealed containers travel well. Dishes that must be eaten hot off the fire, or that go soggy quickly, are harder to deliver. If you sell amala and ewedu, test how it looks and tastes after forty minutes in a pack before you list it.

Think about your customers. Office workers want lunch that is filling, affordable, and easy to eat at a desk. Families want bulk food for the weekend. Party organisers want small chops and finger foods in large quantities. Students want cheap, tasty food in generous portions. Choose a customer group and cook for them.

Finally, think about what makes your food different. Is it your grandmother's recipe? Is it extra spicy? Is it a generous portion? Is it ready in thirty minutes? Customers remember one clear reason to choose you. Find yours and say it in every post.

## Set Up Your Kitchen and Your Business Name

Before you take your first order, set up your kitchen for business, not just for family cooking. You need enough pots, a reliable gas cooker or stove, clean work surfaces, and storage for ingredients. Buy a few extra food flasks, coolers, and serving spoons so you can cook for ten customers as easily as for your family.

Cleanliness is not optional. Your kitchen should be clean enough that you would be proud to show it in a video. Wash your hands, tie your hair back, keep your nails short, and use clean cloths. Customers cannot see your kitchen, but they can taste and smell the difference between a clean cook and a careless one. Some vendors post short videos of their clean cooking process, and that alone wins them customers.

Choose a business name that is easy to remember and easy to spell. "Mama T's Kitchen", "Lagos Food Basket", or "Ada's Small Chops" are better than a long, complicated name nobody can type. Use the same name on WhatsApp, Instagram, and TikTok so customers can find you everywhere.

Set up a WhatsApp Business account with your business name, a clear profile photo of your food or logo, and a short description that says what you sell, your location, and your delivery areas. Add your opening hours, your address or area, and a catalogue of your menu with prices. When a customer messages you, they should see your menu without asking.

Register your business when you can. A CAC business name registration adds trust, helps you open a business bank account, and makes it easier to work with companies that want to order food for their staff. It is not required on day one, but it is worth doing as soon as your sales become regular.

## Price Your Food So You Make a Profit

Pricing is where many food vendors lose money without realising it. They set a price that feels fair, sell many plates, and end the week with nothing left. That usually means they forgot to count a cost. Every plate you sell must cover the ingredients, the gas, the packaging, the delivery, and your time, and still leave a profit.

Start by costing one plate properly. Write down every ingredient in that plate and its cost. If a bag of rice costs ₦55,000 and makes about 40 plates, the rice in one plate costs about ₦1,375. Add the chicken, the pepper, the oil, the seasoning, the gas, and the takeaway pack. If the total cost of one plate is ₦2,800, selling it for ₦3,000 leaves you only ₦200, which is too little. Selling it for ₦4,000 leaves you ₦1,200, which gives you room to grow.

Check what other vendors in your area charge for similar food, but do not copy the cheapest price. A vendor selling jollof rice for ₦2,500 in Ikeja may be using smaller portions or cheaper ingredients. Your price should reflect your portion size, your quality, and your costs, not someone else's guess.

Offer clear portion sizes. A regular plate, a large plate, and a family pack give customers choice and give you higher sales. A family pack of jollof rice for ₦18,000 may be better value for a customer than four separate plates, and it is easier for you to cook and deliver as one order.

Review your prices regularly. Food prices in Nigeria change often, and a price that worked in January may lose you money by June. When the price of rice or chicken rises, adjust your prices or your portion size honestly, and tell your customers why. A small, explained increase is better than quietly reducing the portion and disappointing people.

## Take Orders on WhatsApp and Other Platforms

WhatsApp Business is the most important tool for a food vendor in Nigeria. Most of your customers already use it every day, and it lets you chat, share photos, take payment, and confirm delivery in one place. Set up your catalogue with clear photos, the name of each dish, the price, and the portion size, so a customer can order without asking twenty questions.

Create a simple ordering routine. When a customer messages you, ask for the dish, the quantity, the delivery address, and the time they want it. Confirm the total price including delivery, ask them to pay or pay on delivery, and send a confirmation message. Keep a notebook or a spreadsheet of every order: the customer's name, phone number, order, amount, and delivery status. This record stops confusion and helps you know your regular customers.

Set an order cut-off time. If you cook lunch, tell customers to order before 10:00 a.m. so you have time to cook and deliver. If you cook for dinner, set the cut-off at 4:00 p.m. A clear cut-off protects you from last-minute orders you cannot fulfil and trains customers to order early.

You can also list your food on delivery platforms such as Glovo, Chowdeck, or FoodCourt, which bring you customers you would not find on your own. These platforms charge a commission, so factor that into your price. Use them to reach new customers, and use WhatsApp to keep the regular ones.

Take payment before or on delivery, and be clear about it. Many vendors accept bank transfer or Paystack and Flutterwave payment links, which are safer than cash. If you accept pay on delivery, confirm the customer is available before you send the rider, because a rider who returns with unpaid food costs you money.

## Package and Deliver Food Safely

Packaging is part of your product. A customer judges your food the moment they open the pack. Use clean, sturdy food packs that do not leak, and seal soups in tight containers inside a bag. Put hot food in packs that can handle heat without melting or changing the taste. Add a serviette, a spoon or fork where needed, and a small note with your business name and number.

Label every order. Write the customer's name and the dish on the pack or the bag. When you have five orders going out at once, a label stops you from sending the wrong food to the wrong person. It also looks professional, which matters when a customer orders for their boss or a guest.

Work with reliable dispatch riders. Find one or two riders in your area who know the roads, answer their phones, and handle food carefully. Agree on delivery fees for different areas before the day starts, so you can tell customers the exact delivery cost. For Lagos, a delivery within Lekki may cost ₦1,500, while a delivery from Lekki to Ikeja can cost ₦4,000 or more. Always confirm the delivery fee with the rider before you promise it to the customer.

Deliver on time, and communicate when you cannot. If traffic on the Lekki-Epe Expressway delays a rider, message the customer immediately and give a new time. Customers forgive a delay they were warned about. They rarely forgive a delay they discovered by calling you.

## Market Your Food Without a Big Budget

Your best marketing tool is a good photo. Before you serve a plate, take a clear photo in daylight, with the food neatly arranged and the colours showing. A bright photo of smoky jollof rice with grilled chicken sells more than ten paragraphs of description. Take photos every time you cook, and post your best ones daily.

Post on your WhatsApp status every morning. Show what you are cooking today, the price, and the cut-off time for orders. Many vendors get most of their orders from a status post at 8:00 a.m., when office workers are planning lunch. Post again at midday with a photo of a finished order, and in the evening with tomorrow's menu.

Ask happy customers for reviews and referrals. After a customer enjoys your food, ask them to send a short message you can screenshot and post, or to share your number with their colleagues. A recommendation from a friend is stronger than any advert. Some vendors offer a small discount to customers who bring a new customer, and that keeps orders growing.

Be consistent. Post every day, cook on the days you promised, and keep your quality the same. A customer who orders on Monday and gets a great meal, then orders on Friday and gets a poor one, will not come back. Consistency is what turns a one-time buyer into a regular.

## Common Mistakes Food Vendors Make Online

The first mistake is taking more orders than you can cook. A vendor who accepts fifty orders with one pot will deliver late, run out of food, and disappoint forty people. Know your capacity, set a daily limit, and stop taking orders when you reach it. It is better to serve twenty customers well than fifty badly.

The second mistake is using photos that are not your food. Downloading a beautiful photo of jollof rice from the internet and posting it as your own will bring orders, but the food that arrives will not match the photo, and the customer will feel cheated. Always use photos of food you actually cooked.

The fourth mistake is mixing business money with personal money. When the money from food sales goes into the same account as your personal spending, you will not know whether you made a profit or a loss. Open a separate account or wallet for the business, record every sale and every expense, and pay yourself a fixed amount. That discipline is what turns a hustle into a business.

The fifth mistake is failing to reply to customers. A customer who messages at 9:00 a.m. and gets no reply until 2:00 p.m. has already ordered from someone else. Reply quickly, even if it is just to say the food is sold out for today. A fast reply keeps the customer, even when you cannot serve them that day.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much money do I need to start selling food online in Nigeria?

You can start with as little as ₦20,000 to ₦50,000 for ingredients and packaging if you already have pots and a cooker at home. That is enough to cook a small batch, take photos, and test whether customers order. As orders grow, reinvest your profit into more ingredients, better packaging, and a bigger cooking capacity.

### Do I need a food handler's certificate or a licence to sell food online?

There is no single national licence required to cook from home, but some states and local governments have health and food safety rules, and large companies may ask for basic food safety evidence before they order regularly. Keeping your kitchen clean, handling food safely, and registering your business are good steps. Check your local government requirements if you plan to supply offices or events.

### How do I handle delivery outside my area?

Start with your own neighbourhood and nearby areas where delivery is fast and cheap. As you grow, partner with a dispatch rider or a logistics company that covers more of the city. Always confirm the delivery fee before you promise it to the customer, and be honest if an area is too far or too expensive to deliver to.

### What if a customer complains about my food?

Reply the same day, apologise, and find out exactly what went wrong. If the food was late, cold, or not what they ordered, offer a clear solution, such as a replacement, a discount on the next order, or a refund. Do not argue with the customer in public. A polite, fast response often turns a complaint into a loyal customer.

### Can I sell food online while keeping a full-time job?

Yes, many vendors start part-time, cooking on weekends or in the evenings for parties and bulk orders. Be honest about your availability, set clear order days and cut-off times, and do not accept orders you cannot fulfil. As demand grows, you can decide whether to go full-time.
