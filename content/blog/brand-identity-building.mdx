Funke makes the best small chops in Surulere. That is not a slogan. That is what her neighbours say, what her church members say, and what the people at the office on Adeniran Ogunsanya say every Friday when her puff puff and spring rolls disappear before noon.

For two years she sold from her kitchen with no name at all. Orders came through WhatsApp. People saved her number as "Funke Small Chops" or "Funke Puff Puff" or just "Funke." When a customer wanted to recommend her, they had nothing to forward except a phone number. When she wanted to print a sticker for her packs, there was nothing to print.

In October she decided to fix it. She had ₦150,000 set aside, three weeks before a big Christmas order season, and no idea where to start. What follows is exactly what she did, in the order she did it, with the options she considered and the reasons she chose. If you are starting a business with no name and no look, this is your map.

## Week One: The Name

Funke's first instinct was to call the business "Funke's Kitchen." It is warm and it is honest, but it has a problem: it tells a stranger nothing about what she sells. A kitchen could be anything. A woman scrolling past on Instagram needs to know in two seconds that this is small chops, and that they are good.

She wrote down every name idea she had, twenty-one of them, in a notebook. Then she struck out anything that failed one of three tests. Can a customer spell it after hearing it once? Can they say it without stumbling? Does it tell them what she sells, or how it feels to eat it?

Four names survived, and she tested all four on real people — her sister, two customers, and the woman who sells fabric two shops down.

**Chops by Funke.** Simple, clear, personal. The downside is that it is hard to grow beyond Funke herself. If she ever hires staff or opens a second spot, the name still points at one woman.

**Golden Bites Lagos.** The word golden makes people think of fried food done right — that deep, even colour her puff puff gets. The risk is that "golden" is overused. There is a Golden Bites bakery in Ikeja and a Golden Bites shawarma spot in Lekki, and Funke did not want to spend her first year explaining she is the other one.

**Small Chops Palace.** Fun, confident, and it says exactly what she sells. The problem is the word palace. Her packs cost ₦3,500 for a box of ten pieces. Palace promises something grand and expensive, and her customers are office workers ordering lunch and mothers ordering for birthday parties.

**Funke's Golden Chops.** This is the one she picked. It keeps her name, which is where her reputation lives — people already ask for "Funke" by name. It keeps golden, which describes the food. And it keeps chops, which describes the product. A stranger reading it knows who makes it, what it looks like, and what it is. She checked that nobody else in Lagos was using it — a search on Instagram, a search on Google, and a check with the Corporate Affairs Commission name search, which costs nothing to look at. It was clear.

The lesson is that she picked it by testing, not by feeling. A name you love but nobody can spell costs you customers every single day.

## Week One, Continued: The Colours

Funke's second decision was colour, and she made it in one afternoon at a print shop on Bode Thomas, staring at a colour chart with the printer, Mr. Sunday, who has printed stickers for half the food sellers in Surulere.

She wanted two colours and one helper. Two, because three or four colours turn a small food brand into a carnival. One helper — a warm cream — for backgrounds and boxes, because pure white looks clinical on a food pack and gets dirty fast in a delivery bag.

The main colour is a deep, warm orange. Not the bright orange of a traffic cone, but the orange of properly fried puff puff, the colour of her food at its best. Mr. Sunday matched it to a printed sample: a burnt orange that reads clearly on a white sticker, on a brown paper pack, and on a phone screen. Orange does two jobs for a food business. It makes people hungry, and it stands out on a crowded table at a party where every other pack is plain brown or white.

The second colour is a dark green, the colour of the scent leaf she fries her spring rolls with. Green balances the orange. It says fresh, it says homemade, and it gives her a colour for text that is softer than black.

She wrote the colours down with their codes, and this is the part most new businesses skip. Orange: #E8590C. Dark green: #1B4332. Cream: #FFF8E7. She keeps those codes in her phone notes. When she orders stickers from Mr. Sunday, she reads him the codes. When her nephew designs her Instagram posts, he uses the same codes. A brand colour is only a brand colour when it is the same every time.

The whole colour decision cost her nothing. It cost one afternoon, one conversation, and the discipline to stop at two colours instead of six.

## Week Two: The Voice

A brand voice is just a decision about how your business talks, written down so you talk the same way every time. Funke had never thought about it, because her WhatsApp messages already had a voice. She is warm, she jokes, she calls her customers "my dear" and she never sounds like a bank. The job was to write down what she already did naturally, and stop doing the things that did not sound like her.

She wrote five lines on a page and stuck it to her kitchen wall.

**We sound like a friendly neighbour, not a company.** "Good morning my dear, your chops are ready" — not "Dear valued customer, your order has been processed." If a message sounds like it came from a bank, Funke rewrites it.

**We are proud of the food, not shy about it.** "Our puff puff is the softest in Surulere" is fine. Funke has earned that sentence after two years of Friday sell-outs. False modesty does not sell chops.

**We say prices plainly.** A box of ten is ₦3,500. A box of twenty is ₦6,500. Delivery to Surulere is ₦1,000, to Lekki is ₦2,500. No "DM for price." Funke learned that "DM for price" loses her three customers for every one it keeps, because people assume the worst when the price is hidden.

**We apologise fast and fix it faster.** If an order is late, she says so before the customer asks. "My dear, your order will be thirty minutes late, the oil took long to heat. I have added two extra spring rolls." That sentence has saved her more customers than any discount.

**We never sound angry on the page.** A rude customer gets a calm reply, every time. "I understand, my dear. Let me fix it." The reply is the brand. The argument is not.

She tested the voice the way she tested the name: she wrote three WhatsApp messages for the same situation — a customer asking if she delivers to Ikeja — one in her new voice, one in stiff formal English, one in slang. She sent them to her sister and asked which one sounded like Funke. Her sister picked the right one in two seconds. That is the whole test.

## Week Three: The First Five Assets

With a name, colours and a voice, Funke needed things to exist in the world. Not everything. Five things, in this order, because these are the five things her customers actually touch.

**First, the sticker.** A round sticker, 5 centimetres across, with her name in dark green, the orange line underneath, and her WhatsApp number. Mr. Sunday printed 500 for ₦18,000. The sticker goes on every pack, every box, every bag. It is the cheapest advertising she will ever buy, because every pack travels to a party where forty strangers see it.

**Second, the WhatsApp business profile.** A proper photo of her food, not her face. The business name, Funke's Golden Chops. A short description in her voice: "The softest small chops in Surulere. Puff puff, spring rolls, samosas. Order for your party or your office. We deliver across Lagos." And a catalogue with four items, each with a price. She set it up herself in an evening, and it cost nothing.

**Third, the price list.** One page, printed on cream paper, with the four products, the two box sizes, the prices, and the delivery fees to Surulere, Ikeja, Lekki and the Island. She sends it as a photo on WhatsApp and prints ten copies for the shop counter. A customer who can see the price does not need to ask, and a customer who does not need to ask orders faster.

**Fourth, the Instagram page.** She opened @funkesgoldenchops, used the same sticker as the profile photo, and posted nine photos in one evening — the food, the kitchen, a party table full of her packs, and one short video of puff puff coming out of the oil. Nine posts on day one, so the page does not look empty when the first visitor arrives.

**Fifth, the thank-you card.** A small cream card, the size of a business card, that goes into every order. "Thank you, my dear. You made our day. Order again on WhatsApp." With the orange line and her number. The printer charged ₦12,000 for 500. Customers keep them. Two of them have stuck the card on their fridge, which Funke counts as the best advertising she has ever bought.

Five assets. Total spend on printing: ₦30,000. Total spend on everything else: nothing but time. And now Funke's Golden Chops exists.

## What She Did Not Do

She did not buy a logo from a designer charging ₦150,000, because a sticker with her name in a clean font does the job for now. She will buy a proper logo when the Christmas orders pay for it. She did not print a banner, a flyer or a branded apron, because none of her customers would ever see them. She did not register a trademark, because her name check with the Corporate Affairs Commission was enough for a business her size. And she did not try to sound like a big company, because her customers buy from her precisely because she is not one.

Three weeks after she started, her Christmas orders opened. She sent one WhatsApp broadcast to her 180 saved customers — in her voice, in her colours, with her price list — and booked 34 party orders in two days. The year before, with no name and no list, she had booked eleven.

A brand identity is not a logo. It is a name people can spell, two colours that stay the same, a voice that sounds like you, and five things with your name on them. Build those in three weeks and your business stops being a phone number. It becomes a name people can recommend.
