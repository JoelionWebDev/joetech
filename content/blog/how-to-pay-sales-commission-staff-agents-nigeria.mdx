Nothing kills a small business faster than a quarrel over money with the people who sell for you. A sales agent brings in a big customer, the money lands in your account, and then the argument starts: "You promised me 10 percent." "No, I said 10 percent of profit, not of sales." "When will you pay me?" By the time the argument ends, you have lost a good salesperson, and they have told three other agents not to work with you.

It does not have to be like that. A clear commission plan, written down and agreed before the first sale, protects both sides. Your staff know exactly what they will earn, and you know exactly what each sale costs you. This guide shows you how to design a fair commission system for staff and agents in Nigeria, write a simple agreement, track sales honestly, and pay on time.

## Why Commission Matters More Than Salary Alone

Most small businesses in Nigeria cannot afford the big salaries that banks and telecom companies pay. A shop assistant in Lagos might earn ₦80,000 to ₦120,000 a month. A sales agent working from home might earn nothing at all unless they sell. Commission bridges that gap: it gives your people a reason to sell harder, because their income grows when your business grows.

Commission also aligns interests. A staff member on a flat salary gets paid the same whether they sell one item or fifty. A staff member who earns commission on every sale has a reason to greet customers warmly, follow up on WhatsApp, and close the deal instead of letting the customer walk away.

But commission only works if it is fair and predictable. If your people do not understand the plan, do not trust your sales records, or do not get paid on time, commission becomes a source of resentment instead of motivation. The system you build must be simple enough to explain in five minutes and transparent enough that anybody can check their own earnings.

## Step 1: Decide Who Earns Commission and for What

Not everybody in your business should earn commission on everything. Be specific.

**In-house sales staff:** Your shop assistants, showroom staff and office sales team. They usually earn a base salary plus commission on what they personally sell. Their commission is typically lower than an agent's, because you also pay their salary, their transport and their workspace.

**Field sales agents:** People who find customers for you outside your shop — the agent who brings a corporate client to your printing business, the marketer who sells your real estate plots in Ibeju-Lekki, the freelance rep who sells your insurance or your training course. They usually earn commission only, no salary, and their rate is higher because they bear their own costs.

**Referrers:** Customers, friends or partners who send a buyer your way but do not do the selling. A small one-off referral fee, for example ₦5,000 or 2 percent, is usually enough. Do not confuse a referrer with an agent: the referrer makes an introduction, the agent does the work of closing the sale.

Write down who falls in each group in your business. Then define what counts as a sale. Is it when the customer pays a deposit, or only when they pay in full? Is it when the order is delivered, or when the money enters your account? For most small businesses, the safest rule is this: commission is earned when the money is received and the sale is complete. If a customer pays a 50 percent deposit, commission is paid on the 50 percent received, and the balance follows when the rest is paid. That rule prevents the common problem of paying full commission on a sale that later gets cancelled or refunded.

## Step 2: Choose a Commission Structure That Fits Your Margins

Your commission rate must come from your profit margin, not from what your competitor pays or what an agent demands. If you sell a product for ₦100,000 and your total cost is ₦85,000, your gross profit is ₦15,000. Paying 10 percent of the selling price — ₦10,000 — leaves you with only ₦5,000. Paying 10 percent of profit — ₦1,500 — may be too small to motivate anybody. You need a structure that works for both sides, and the only way to find it is to do the maths first.

### Flat percentage of sales

The simplest structure. The salesperson earns a fixed percentage of the selling price on every sale. For example, 5 percent on every product sold. If they sell ₦500,000 worth of goods in a month, they earn ₦25,000.

This works well when your margins are consistent across products and you want simplicity. It is easy to explain and easy to calculate. The risk is that your people will push the easiest products to sell, even if those are your lowest-margin items.

Typical ranges in Nigeria, as a starting guide: retail and fast-moving goods, 2 to 5 percent; furniture, electronics and services, 5 to 10 percent; real estate and high-value items, 3 to 7 percent; insurance and financial products, 10 to 20 percent on first-year premium, following the provider's rules. These are guides, not laws. Your margin decides your rate.

### Tiered commission

The rate rises as sales rise. For example, 5 percent on the first ₦500,000 sold in a month, 7 percent on sales above ₦500,000, and 10 percent on sales above ₦1,000,000. Tiers motivate your best people to keep pushing after they have hit their basic target, instead of relaxing for the rest of the month.

Tiers work well for staff with monthly targets. They are slightly harder to calculate, so keep the tiers simple — two or three levels at most — and show a worked example in your agreement.

### Commission on profit, not price

The salesperson earns a percentage of the gross profit on each sale. If a product sells for ₦200,000 and costs you ₦150,000, the profit is ₦50,000, and 20 percent of profit is ₦10,000. This protects your margins and encourages your people to sell at full price instead of offering discounts that eat your profit. It only works if your people trust your cost figures, so be prepared to show them how profit is calculated for each product.

### Flat fee per sale

A fixed amount per sale, regardless of price. For example, ₦5,000 for every training course sold, or ₦20,000 for every plot of land sold. This is the simplest structure of all and works well when your products have similar prices. It is also the easiest to explain to agents who work with several companies.

### Combination: salary plus commission

For in-house staff, this is the most common and most stable structure in Nigeria. A base salary that covers basic living costs, plus commission that rewards performance. For example, a sales assistant earns ₦100,000 salary plus 5 percent commission on personal sales. If they sell ₦400,000 in a month, they take home ₦120,000. The salary gives them security; the commission gives them drive.

## Step 3: Set Targets That Are Challenging but Reachable

A target gives commission meaning. Without a target, commission is just a bonus that people stop noticing. With a target, it becomes a goal.

Set a monthly target based on your real numbers, not on hope. Look at what your business sold in the last three months. If your shop averages ₦1.5 million in monthly sales, a target of ₦2 million is a stretch but possible. A target of ₦5 million is a fantasy, and your staff will ignore it.

Break the target down. A monthly target of ₦2 million is about ₦77,000 a day over 26 working days. That daily number is something a salesperson can picture: "I need to sell four items at ₦20,000 today." Big numbers demotivate; small daily numbers motivate.

Tie a reward to the target, not just to the commission itself. For example, hitting the monthly target earns a bonus of ₦20,000, or the top salesperson of the month gets a ₦30,000 bonus or an extra day off. 
Review targets every quarter. If your business grows, raise them gradually. If the market slows down — during a fuel scarcity, or in January when customers are broke after December spending — adjust them fairly. A target that ignores reality is worse than no target.

## Step 4: Write It Down — The Commission Agreement

Verbal agreements cause quarrels. Written agreements prevent them. Your commission agreement does not need a lawyer to be useful, though a lawyer should review it if large sums are involved. It needs to be clear, signed by both sides, and kept where both sides can see it.

A simple commission agreement should cover:

- **Who the agreement is between:** Your business name and the salesperson's full name.
- **What they will sell:** The products or services covered.
- **The commission rate and structure:** The exact percentage or fee, with a worked example.
- **When commission is earned:** When payment is received in full, or as payments arrive.
- **When commission is paid:** The exact payday, for example "on or before the 10th of the following month."
- **How sales are tracked:** Who records the sale, and how the salesperson can verify their own sales.
- **Returns and cancellations:** What happens if a customer returns a product or cancels. The fair rule is that commission on a refunded sale is deducted from the next payment.
- **Discounts:** Whether the salesperson can offer discounts, and how a discount affects commission.
- **Duration and termination:** How long the agreement lasts, and what happens to unpaid commission if either side ends the relationship. Unpaid commission that has been earned must still be paid.
- **Confidentiality:** The salesperson should not share your prices, costs or customer list with competitors.

Give each salesperson a signed copy. Keep a copy yourself. If the plan changes, write the change down, date it, and get both sides to sign again. Never change commission rates in the middle of a month without agreement — that single act destroys trust faster than anything else.

For agents who are not your employees, the same document works as an agency agreement. Add one line: the agent is an independent agent, not an employee, and is responsible for their own tax and transport. That distinction matters under Nigerian labour law.

## Step 5: Track Every Sale Honestly

Trust dies when sales records are unclear. Your salesperson must be able to verify every sale they are paid for, and you must be able to verify every sale they claim.

Use one system for all sales. It can be a notebook, a Google Sheet, or a simple point-of-sale app. What matters is that every sale is recorded the same way, on the same day it happens: date, customer name, product, amount, amount paid, salesperson's name, and commission due.

Give each salesperson a way to check their own numbers. A shared Google Sheet they can view, a weekly summary sent on WhatsApp, or a printed sheet at the end of the week. When people can see their own earnings growing, they sell more. When they cannot see anything, they assume they are being cheated — even when they are not.

Reconcile weekly. Sit down, even for 15 minutes, and compare the sales record with the bank alerts and the cash received. Fix errors immediately, while the sale is still fresh in everyone's memory. A mistake discovered three months later is almost impossible to resolve fairly.

## Step 6: Pay on Time, Every Time

This is the most important rule in this whole guide. Pay commission on the agreed date, every time, without being chased. Late payment is the number one reason good salespeople leave small Nigerian businesses.

Set a fixed commission payday and stick to it. For example, salaries on the 28th, commission on the 10th of the following month, after you have reconciled the sales. Tell your people the date in the agreement, and remind them when it is coming. If you will be late because of a genuine cash problem, tell them before the date, explain why, and give a new date. Silence is what breeds suspicion.

Pay by transfer, not cash, so there is a record. Send a short message with each payment: "Your commission for October is ₦45,000, paid to your account today. Thank you for your hard work." That message costs nothing and builds loyalty.

Never use commission as a punishment. Do not withhold earned commission because a staff member was late to work, or because you are unhappy about something else. Deal with discipline separately. Commission is pay for sales made, and withholding it is both unfair and, in many cases, unlawful.

If cash flow is tight, plan for commission in your monthly budget. Set aside the commission amount as sales come in, in a separate account or a labelled envelope, so the money is there on payday. Paying commission from money you have already spent elsewhere is a planning failure, not a staff problem.

## Common Problems and How to Solve Them

**"My agent brought a customer but another agent closed the sale."** Decide the rule in advance: commission goes to the person who introduced the customer, if the introduction is recorded, or it is split. Record introductions in your sales book on the day they happen.

**"My staff offer big discounts to win sales, and my profit disappears."** Limit discount authority. Staff can offer up to 5 percent without asking you. Anything more needs your approval. And pay commission on the discounted price, not the original price, so the cost of the discount is shared.

**"An agent claims a sale they did not make."** No record, no commission. If the sale is not in the book with the agent's name on the day it happened, it does not count. This rule sounds harsh, but it is the only rule that protects honest agents.

**"Sales are good but I cannot afford the commission."** Your rate is too high for your margin, or your prices are too low. Recalculate from your real costs. It is better to agree a lower rate honestly than to promise a high rate and fail to pay it.

## Tax and Legal Notes for Nigerian Employers

Commission paid to employees is part of their employment income and should be included in their pay records for PAYE tax purposes — ask your accountant how to record it. For independent agents, keep a record of every payment with the agent's name and amount, so your books are clean. Do not use a commission-only arrangement to avoid paying a fair base salary to someone who works full-time in your shop. When large sums are involved, ask a lawyer or accountant for a one-hour consultation before you sign. It is cheap compared to a dispute.

## A Simple Example You Can Copy

Here is a complete, simple plan for a small Lagos business selling home appliances:

- Sales assistant salary: ₦120,000 per month.
- Commission: 5 percent of the selling price on every item they personally sell.
- Target: ₦1,500,000 in monthly sales.
- Target bonus: ₦25,000 if the target is met.
- Commission is earned when the customer pays in full.
- Commission is paid on the 10th of the following month, by transfer.
- Discounts above 5 percent need the manager's approval.
- If a customer returns an item, the commission on that item is deducted from the next month's payment.

If the assistant sells ₦1,800,000 in a month, they earn ₦90,000 in commission plus the ₦25,000 target bonus, on top of their ₦120,000 salary — a total of ₦235,000. The business keeps its margin, the assistant earns well, and everybody knows exactly how the number was calculated.

2. **Tomorrow:** Choose one commission structure from this guide and draft the rates.
3. **Day 3:** Write the one-page commission agreement, with a worked example.

A fair commission plan is not an expense. It is an investment in the people who bring money into your business. Pay them fairly, pay them transparently, and pay them on time — and they will sell for you like the business is their own. Because, in a small way, it will be.
