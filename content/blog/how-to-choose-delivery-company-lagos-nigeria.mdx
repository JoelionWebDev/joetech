For an online seller in Lagos, delivery is not a side issue — it is the product. A customer who orders a dress, a phone, or a food tray will judge your whole business on one thing: did it arrive on time, in good condition, and without drama? A great product delivered late, damaged, or lost creates a refund, a bad review, and a customer who never returns.

Yet many small businesses choose a delivery partner by whoever is closest or cheapest right now. That works until a rider disappears with a ₦85,000 order, a courier takes four days to deliver within Lagos, or a customer in Abuja waits a week for a package promised in two days.

Choosing the right delivery company is a business decision, not an errand. This guide shows you how to work out what your business actually needs, compare the main options in Lagos — bike dispatch riders, courier companies, and bus-park logistics — check costs honestly, test a provider before you commit, and build a delivery system your customers can trust.

## First, Work Out What Your Business Needs

Before you compare any company, write down your own delivery profile. A fashion seller sending ten small parcels a day inside Lekki needs something very different from a furniture maker sending one large item a week to Abuja.

Answer these questions:

**What are you sending?** Small, light items like clothes, cosmetics, and phone accessories are ideal for bike riders. Heavy or bulky items like a standing fan, a bag of rice, or a piece of furniture need a van or a courier with larger vehicles. Fragile items like cakes, glassware, and electronics need careful handling and good packaging, and some riders will refuse them.

**How many deliveries do you make?** One or two a day, ten a day, or fifty a week? Volume changes the maths completely. A rider charging ₦3,000 per delivery is fine for two orders, but at twenty orders a day that is ₦60,000 daily. High-volume sellers need negotiated rates, not per-trip prices.

**Where do your customers live?** If most of your customers are in Lekki, Ajah, and Victoria Island, a local rider who knows those areas is perfect. If your customers are spread across Lagos — Ikeja, Surulere, Yaba, Festac, Ikorodu — you need a company with wide coverage. If you ship to Abuja, Port Harcourt, or other states, you need a courier with an interstate network.

**How fast must it arrive?** Food, cakes, and medicines often need same-day delivery within hours. Fashion and electronics can usually wait until the next day. Your promise to the customer should match what your delivery partner can actually do, every day, not just on a good day.

**Who pays for delivery?** You, the customer, or both? Some businesses include delivery in the product price and advertise "free delivery." Others charge the customer separately. Either way, you need to know the real cost before you set your price.

Write your answers down: that is your brief. Any company that cannot meet it is the wrong partner, however cheap or popular it is.

## The Main Delivery Options in Lagos

### 1. Bike dispatch riders

Bike riders are the backbone of Lagos e-commerce delivery. They move fast through traffic, they can reach estates and side streets that vans cannot, and they usually deliver within a few hours inside one area. A rider in Lekki can collect from your shop at 10am and deliver to a customer in Ajah by noon.

Riders are best for small parcels, documents, food, and urgent same-day deliveries within Lagos. Typical intra-Lagos bike delivery costs between ₦2,000 and ₦5,000, depending on distance — Lekki to Ajah is cheaper than Lekki to Ikeja, and Lekki to Ikorodu costs far more.

The risks are real. Individual riders vary widely in reliability. A rider may accept a job and then switch off his phone. Bikes have limited capacity and no protection from rain, so your packaging must be waterproof. And if a rider has an accident or the goods are damaged, an informal rider may have no insurance and no way to compensate you.

Use riders you know, or riders from a registered dispatch company with an office you can visit. Save the numbers of two or three reliable riders so you are never stranded when one is busy.

### 2. Courier and logistics companies

Companies like GIG Logistics, DHL, Kwik Delivery, and other Nigerian courier brands run structured networks: booking offices, tracking numbers, scheduled pickups, and delivery to other states. They are slower than a local bike rider for a trip across Lekki, but far better for interstate deliveries and for businesses that need records, receipts, and tracking.

A courier is the right choice when you ship to Abuja, Enugu, Kano, or Port Harcourt, when you send heavier items, or when your customer wants a tracking number they can check themselves. Prices vary by weight, size, and destination: a small parcel from Lagos to Abuja typically costs between ₦4,000 and ₦8,000, while Lagos to Port Harcourt is usually a little more.

The trade-off is speed within Lagos. A courier parcel can take 24 to 48 hours even inside the same city, because it passes through a sorting hub. If your customer expects delivery in three hours, a courier will disappoint them. Use couriers for what they are good at — structured, trackable, interstate delivery — and use riders for urgent local jobs.

### 3. Bus-park and waybill services

For large or heavy goods going to other states, many traders use bus-park waybills: your goods travel on a passenger bus or a cargo bus to the destination town, and the customer collects them at the park. This is often the cheapest option for bulky items — a carton of goods to Onitsha or Aba can cost far less by bus than by courier.

The downside is reliability and care. Buses leave on their own schedule, goods can be delayed, and there is little tracking. Waybills work best when you know the driver or the park agent personally, and when the customer is comfortable collecting from the park.

### 4. Your own delivery staff

Some growing businesses hire their own rider or driver. This gives you full control: your rider wears your brand, follows your instructions, and answers to you. It also gives you fixed costs — salary, fuel, bike maintenance, and the risk of a rider falling sick or quitting.

Consider your own rider only when you make at least fifteen to twenty deliveries a day, every day. Before that, outsourcing is cheaper and more flexible. Many businesses use a hybrid: their own rider for Lekki and Ajah, and a courier for the rest of Lagos and other states.


## How to Compare Delivery Companies: The Checklist

When you talk to any delivery company, score them on these points. Do not choose on price alone.

**Coverage.** Which areas of Lagos do they serve, and which states? Ask specifically about the areas your customers live in: Ikorodu, Epe, Badagry, and Festac are often excluded or charged extra. Get the list in writing.

**Speed.** What is the promised delivery time for Lekki to Ikeja? For Lagos to Abuja? And what happens when they miss it? A company that promises "same day" but delivers in two days will damage your reputation.

**Price and how it is calculated.** Is the price per trip, per kilogram, or per zone? Is there a minimum charge? Are there extra fees for fuel, for weekend delivery, or for cash collection? Ask for a written rate card, not a verbal quote.

**Tracking.** Can you and your customer track the parcel? Even a simple WhatsApp update from the rider — "Picked up", "On the way", "Delivered" — is far better than silence.

**Insurance and liability.** If goods are lost or damaged, who pays? A serious company has a clear policy, even if it only covers up to a stated amount. Ask the question directly before the first delivery, not after a loss.

**Payment collection.** Can the rider collect payment from the customer and remit it to you? If so, how quickly, and is there a fee? This matters if you offer payment on delivery.

**Reliability and reputation.** Ask other sellers in your area who they use and why. Check the company's Google Business Profile and social media comments for complaints about lost parcels or rude riders. One bad story may be an exception; ten similar stories are a pattern.

**Customer service.** When something goes wrong — and it will — can you reach a real person quickly? Test this before you commit: call their support line and see how long it takes to get an answer.

**Professionalism.** Do riders wear uniforms or branded shirts? Do they carry proper delivery bags? Are parcels handled with care? Your customer meets the rider, not your office. The rider is your brand in that moment.

## What Delivery Really Costs — Work Out Your True Numbers

Delivery fees look small until you add them up. A seller doing ten deliveries a day at ₦3,000 each spends ₦30,000 a day, ₦180,000 in a six-day week, and over ₦700,000 in a month. That is a major business cost, and it must be built into your prices.

Calculate your delivery cost per order. Add the rider or courier fee, packaging costs, and the cost of failed deliveries — orders where the customer refused, was unreachable, or gave a wrong address, and you still paid for the trip. If one in ten deliveries fails, your real cost per successful delivery is about 11 percent higher than the quoted fee.

Then decide your delivery pricing strategy:

**Charge the customer the exact delivery fee.** This is the simplest and most transparent option. Tell the customer the fee before they pay, and let them choose the speed. Most customers accept a fair delivery charge when it is stated upfront.

**Include delivery in the product price.** "Free delivery within Lekki" sounds attractive, and it can increase orders. It only works if your product margin covers the delivery cost. If your profit on a ₦15,000 dress is ₦4,000 and delivery costs ₦3,000, free delivery leaves you almost nothing.

**Offer free delivery above a minimum order.** For example, free delivery on orders above ₦50,000 within Lagos. This encourages larger orders and protects your margin on small ones.

**Negotiate volume rates.** Once you send ten or more parcels a day with one company, ask for a corporate rate. Many courier companies offer 10 to 20 percent discounts for regular volume, or a flat monthly rate. Get the agreement in writing, with the areas and the delivery times it covers.

Review your delivery costs every month. If a rider's price rises, or a new company offers a better rate for your busiest routes, adjust. Delivery is a cost you can negotiate, and loyal, high-volume customers get better terms.

## Test Before You Commit

Never hand a new delivery company your most valuable order on day one. Test them first.

Start with three to five small, low-value deliveries to different areas: one within your neighbourhood, one across Lagos, and one interstate if that matters to you. Note the pickup time, the delivery time, whether the customer was contacted before arrival, the condition of the parcel, and how the rider behaved.

Ask the customer for honest feedback. Did the rider call before arriving? Was the package intact? Did delivery take as long as promised? Customers will tell you the truth if you ask, and their answers are more valuable than any advert.

Run the test for one to two weeks, keeping your previous option as a backup so a failure does not leave you stranded.

If the test goes well, start with a small commitment — one week, or twenty deliveries — and review again. A good partner earns more of your business gradually. A bad partner should be dropped quickly, before a big loss.

## How to Handle the Problems That Will Happen

Even the best delivery company has bad days. What matters is how you respond.

**Late delivery.** Call the customer before they call you. "Good afternoon, your order is on its way and will arrive by 5pm instead of 2pm. I apologise for the delay." Customers forgive delays they are warned about; they do not forgive silence.

**Damaged goods.** Photograph the damage, contact the delivery company the same day, and make the claim in writing. Replace or refund the customer promptly — then pursue the delivery company for compensation. The customer should never be the one who suffers while you argue with a courier.

**Lost parcel.** This is every seller's nightmare. Stay calm, inform the customer honestly, offer a replacement or a refund immediately, and follow up with the delivery company in writing. If the company has insurance, file the claim with your proof: booking receipt, photos of the item, and the customer's order. Keep copies of everything.

**Customer unreachable or wrong address.** Confirm the address and phone number before dispatch, and ask the customer to share a live location on WhatsApp for difficult addresses. If the rider cannot reach the customer, ask the rider to wait a few minutes, call the customer yourself, and agree a new time. Do not pay for the same delivery twice without asking why the first attempt failed.

**Rider demands extra money.** Agree the price before dispatch, in writing, and tell the customer the price too. If a rider demands more on arrival, the customer should call you, not pay extra. Report riders who do this to their company or stop using them.

## Packaging: The Part Most Sellers Get Wrong

Good packaging prevents most damage claims. Use a sturdy box or a strong nylon bag, not a thin shopping bag. Wrap fragile items in bubble wrap or newspaper. Seal the package properly so it cannot open in transit. Label it clearly with the customer's name, phone number, and address — and your own phone number as the sender.

For food and cakes, use containers that seal tightly, and tell the rider the package is fragile and must stay upright. For liquids like oils and creams, seal the bottle, wrap it in nylon, and pack it upright. For electronics, use the original box if you still have it.

Waterproofing matters in Lagos, especially during the rainy season. A plastic bag inside the delivery bag protects clothes and electronics from a sudden downpour on a bike.

## Setting Delivery Expectations With Your Customers

Most delivery complaints are not about the delivery itself, but about the promise. If you promise "delivery in 2 hours" and it takes 6, the customer is angry. If you promise "delivery tomorrow" and it arrives today, the customer is delighted. Under-promise and over-deliver.

State your delivery times clearly wherever customers order: on your Instagram bio, your WhatsApp Business catalogue, and your website. For example: "Orders before 12 noon are delivered same day within Lekki and Ajah. Other parts of Lagos: next day. Other states: 2 to 3 working days."

Confirm the address and the delivery fee before the customer pays. A short message avoids most disputes: "Your order is ₦25,000 and delivery to Ikeja is ₦3,500. Total is ₦28,500. Please confirm and I will dispatch today."

Send the customer the tracking information or the rider's phone number when the order leaves. A customer who can follow their order does not need to call you every hour. And when the order is delivered, follow up with a short message: "Your order has been delivered. Please confirm you received it in good condition. Thank you for shopping with us."

## Conclusion

The right delivery company for your business in Lagos is not the cheapest one, and not the biggest one. It is the one that matches your products, your volume, your areas, and your speed promise — and that proves it, delivery after delivery.

Work out what you need, compare riders and couriers on coverage, speed, price, tracking, insurance, and reputation, and test before you commit. Build the real cost of delivery into your prices. Package well, set honest expectations, and communicate with your customer at every step.

Delivery is where your business meets your customer face to face. Get it right, and every order becomes an advert for the next one.
