Nothing frustrates a customer more than a shop that is closed when it said it would be open, or a delivery that arrives three hours late with no message. In Nigeria, where traffic in Lagos can turn a thirty-minute trip into three hours and a power cut can stop work without warning, customers understand that things go wrong. What they do not forgive is silence and broken promises. A customer who is told the truth will wait. A customer who is ignored will buy from someone else.

Your opening hours and your delivery times are promises. Every time you keep one, you build trust. Every time you break one without warning, you lose a little of it. For a small business, trust is everything, because you do not have a big brand name to hide behind. This guide shows you how to set hours and delivery times that fit your real life, communicate them clearly, and keep them even on difficult days.

## Why Reliable Hours Matter So Much

Customers plan their day around your business. A mother in Ikeja who wants to pick up her child's birthday cake at 4 pm will leave work early to make it. If she arrives and finds your shop closed, she has wasted her time and her transport fare, and she will not forget it. A customer who orders food for a meeting at noon and receives it at 2 pm has a problem that goes beyond hunger: her meeting was embarrassed. Your hours and your delivery times shape your customers' days, so they take them seriously.

There is also a quiet advantage: consistency makes you look professional. A shop that opens at 9 am every day, including Saturdays, looks like a serious business. A shop that opens at 9 am on Monday, 11 am on Tuesday, and not at all on Wednesday looks like a hobby. Customers pay more, and trust more, when a business looks serious. Your hours are one of the cheapest ways to look professional.

## How to Choose Opening Hours That Fit Your Life

The best opening hours are the ones you can actually keep, every day, even when you are tired or short-staffed. Do not copy a big supermarket's hours. A supermarket has many staff and can stay open from 8 am to 10 pm. You are one person, or a small team, and your hours must fit your real capacity.

Start by looking at when your customers actually come. If you run a physical shop, keep a simple record for two weeks: write down the time of each sale or visit. You will quickly see a pattern. A food vendor near an office in Victoria Island may find that most sales happen between 11 am and 3 pm, with almost nothing after 6 pm. A fashion shop in Lekki may find that evenings and Saturdays are the busiest times. Let the pattern decide your hours, not guesswork.

Consider your own life too. If you are a mother who must take children to school at 7 am, opening at 7:30 am is a promise you will break often. Open at 9 am instead, and be there at 9 am every single day. If you run your business alone and you need one day a week to restock and rest, choose a closing day and announce it. A shop that is closed every Sunday, clearly announced, loses far fewer customers than a shop that is sometimes open on Sunday and sometimes not.

Match your hours to your location. A shop inside a market in Onitsha should follow market hours, because customers come when the market is busy. A shop in a residential estate in Abuja should open when residents return from work. An online business should set hours that match when customers message most: for many Nigerian online vendors, that is between 8 am and 9 pm, with a rush of messages in the evening when people are home from work.

## Writing Your Hours Where Customers Can Find Them

Hours that exist only in your head are useless. Customers must be able to find them in seconds, in every place they look for you.

Put your hours on your WhatsApp Business profile first, because that is where most Nigerian customers will check. WhatsApp Business lets you set business hours, and it can send an automatic reply when a customer messages outside those hours. Set this up properly, with a friendly away message: "Thank you for messaging Ada's Cakes. We are open Monday to Saturday, 9 am to 6 pm. We will reply when we open." That message alone stops many complaints.

Put your hours on your Instagram and Facebook bios. You have very little space in a bio, so keep it short: "Open Mon–Sat, 9am–6pm | Lekki". If your hours change for a public holiday, update the bio that same morning, and post it on your status too.

Put your hours on Google Business Profile if you have one. Many customers search Google for a business near them and check the hours before they leave home. Google shows whether you are open now, which is very useful. Keep this listing updated, because wrong hours on Google send customers to a closed shop, and they will leave a bad review.

Put your hours on your shop sign and your door. A simple sign, "Open Monday to Saturday, 9 am to 6 pm, closed on Sundays", costs little and answers the question for every passer-by. If you change your hours, change the sign the same day. A sign with old hours is worse than no sign at all.

Put your hours on your website and your order pages. If you sell online, your website should show your opening hours and your order cut-off time clearly, near the order button, not hidden on a contact page. A customer who is about to pay wants to know when her order will be processed, and she wants to know it before she pays.

## Setting Delivery Times You Can Honestly Keep

Delivery is where most Nigerian small businesses break their promises, because delivery depends on things outside your control: traffic, rain, fuel scarcity, and riders who take three jobs at once. The answer is not to promise the fastest time. The answer is to promise a time you can keep, and then keep it.

Start by measuring your real delivery times. For two weeks, write down every order: when it was placed, when it left your shop, and when the customer confirmed receipt. Sort the results by area. You may find that deliveries within Lekki take one to two hours, deliveries to Ikeja take three to four hours, and deliveries to the mainland take four to six hours. Those real numbers are your delivery times. Use them, even if they feel slow. A customer who is told four hours and receives her order in three is delighted. A customer who is told one hour and receives it in three is angry. The promise matters more than the speed.

Build buffer time into every promise. If your fastest delivery to Surulere is two hours, promise three. The extra hour covers traffic on the Third Mainland Bridge, a rider who gets lost, or a sudden downpour. If the delivery arrives early, you look excellent. If something goes wrong, you still have time to fix it and keep your promise. Businesses that promise their best-case time break promises constantly. Businesses that promise their realistic time, with buffer, keep them.

Set a daily order cut-off time and announce it. For example: "Order before 12 noon for same-day delivery in Lekki. Orders after 12 noon will be delivered the next day." A cut-off time protects you from the 5 pm order that must be delivered by 6 pm, which is a promise you cannot keep. Customers accept cut-off times easily, because they are clear and fair. Put the cut-off time in your WhatsApp catalogue, your Instagram bio, and your order confirmation message.

Charge properly for delivery, and say the fee upfront. Many businesses lose money on delivery because they charge too little, then rush and cut corners. Calculate your real delivery cost: the rider's fee, your packaging, and your time. In Lagos, a bike delivery within Lekki often costs ₦1,500 to ₦3,000, and a delivery to the mainland can cost ₦3,500 to ₦6,000. Charge the real fee and state it before the customer pays. A customer who knows the fee in advance will not argue. A customer who discovers a hidden fee at the door will be angry, and she will remember.

## Working With Dispatch Riders

For most small businesses in Nigeria, the rider is the delivery. Your rider's reliability is your reliability, so choose riders carefully and manage the relationship well.

Use riders you trust, and keep the number small. It is better to work with two reliable riders who know your business than with ten strangers from an app. A rider who knows your shop, your packaging, and your customers will deliver better and faster. Pay them fairly and on time. A rider who is paid late will take other jobs first, and your deliveries will suffer. Agree on a rate card in advance: how much for Lekki, how much for Ikeja, how much for the mainland, and how much for urgent deliveries. Write it down so there is no argument later.

Give the rider everything needed for a smooth delivery. Write the customer's name, phone number, and full address clearly, including a landmark. "14B Admiralty Way, Lekki Phase 1, opposite the Ebeano supermarket" is far better than "Admiralty Way". Add the customer's phone number so the rider can call if he is lost. Package the order so it survives a bike ride: seal food containers properly, wrap fragile items, and use a sturdy bag. A delivery that arrives damaged is worse than a late delivery.

Track your deliveries, even simply. When the rider leaves, send the customer a message: "Your order is on the way with our rider. He should arrive between 2 pm and 3 pm." When the rider delivers, ask him to confirm, and then message the customer: "Your order has been delivered. Thank you." These two messages cost you nothing, and they remove the customer's anxiety. A customer who knows where her order is will wait patiently. A customer who hears nothing will call you every ten minutes.

Have a backup plan. Keep the number of a second rider, or a dispatch company, for days when your regular rider is sick or his bike breaks down. On rainy days and during fuel scarcity, allow extra time and tell customers in advance. A WhatsApp status in the morning, "Heavy rain today, so deliveries may take one extra hour. Thank you for your patience", prevents dozens of angry calls.

## When Things Go Wrong: Communicating Delays

Things will go wrong. A bike will break down. Rain will flood a road. A rider will get stuck in traffic for two hours. What matters is not the delay itself, but how you handle it. Customers forgive delays when they are told early and honestly. They do not forgive silence.

Tell the customer as soon as you know there is a problem, not when the promised time has already passed. If the rider calls you at 1 pm to say he is stuck, message the customer at 1 pm: "Hello, I am sorry, your delivery will be about one hour late because of traffic on the Lekki-Epe Expressway. Your new arrival time is 3 pm." The customer may be disappointed, but she can plan around the new time. If you wait until 3 pm to tell her, she has already spent two hours waiting and wondering.

Apologise briefly and offer something. You do not need to give a long explanation. A short apology, a new time, and a small gesture go a long way: "I am sorry for the delay. Your delivery will arrive by 3 pm, and we have added a free drink to your order." The gesture costs little, but it turns a bad experience into a story the customer tells her friends in a good way.

## Holidays, Emergencies, and Planned Closures

Every business closes sometimes: public holidays, a family emergency, a trip to restock in Onitsha, or simply a day of rest. The mistake is to close without telling anyone. A customer who travels to your shop on a public holiday and finds it shut will feel you do not respect her time.

Announce closures early. For public holidays, post on your WhatsApp status and Instagram at least two days before: "We will be closed on Monday for the public holiday, and we will open again on Tuesday at 9 am." For planned closures, such as a holiday or a market trip, announce a week before, and remind customers the day before. Pin the message in your WhatsApp group if you have one.

For emergencies, announce as soon as you can. If you wake up sick and cannot open the shop, post on your status immediately and update your WhatsApp Business away message. If you have pending deliveries, call those customers personally. A personal call takes two minutes and saves a relationship.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I stay open late to catch more customers?

Only if the extra sales are worth the extra cost and exhaustion. Record your sales by hour for two weeks. If you earn very little after 6 pm, closing at 6 pm is smarter than staying open until 9 pm for a handful of sales. If evenings are your busiest time, stay open later and open later in the morning instead. Let your sales record decide, not the fear of missing out.

### What if a customer insists on delivery outside my hours?

Be polite but firm. Offer the closest option inside your hours: "We can deliver first thing tomorrow morning at 9 am." If you make an exception once, the customer will expect it every time, and other customers will hear about it. Save exceptions for genuine emergencies, such as a funeral or a hospital need, and even then, charge a fair fee for the extra effort.

### How do I handle customers who message at midnight?

Set up an automatic reply on WhatsApp Business for outside your hours, and do not answer until morning. Replying at midnight trains customers to message at midnight, and it burns you out. A friendly automatic reply is enough: "Thank you for your message. We are open 9 am to 6 pm, and we will reply first thing in the morning."

### Should my online shop have the same hours as my physical shop?

Not necessarily. An online shop can take orders at any time, but you should be clear about when orders are processed and dispatched. For example: "Orders are taken 24 hours a day. Orders are processed Monday to Saturday, 9 am to 5 pm, and dispatched the same day if ordered before 12 noon." Customers accept this happily, because it is clear and honest.

### What is a fair delivery time in Lagos?

It depends on the distance. Within the same area, such as Lekki to Lekki, one to two hours is fair. From Lekki to Ikeja or the mainland, three to five hours is realistic, depending on traffic. Inter-state deliveries take one to three days. Always promise the realistic time for the area, with buffer, rather than the fastest time you have ever achieved.
