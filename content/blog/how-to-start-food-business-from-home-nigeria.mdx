Every day in Lagos, Abuja, Port Harcourt and Ibadan, thousands of busy workers, students and families buy their lunch from someone cooking at home. They order jollof rice on WhatsApp, pick up a food pack from a neighbour, or get a weekly soup delivery from a woman they found on Instagram. The home food business is one of the most reliable ways to earn money in Nigeria, because people must eat every day, and a good cook with a clean kitchen and honest prices can build a loyal customer base without renting a shop.

You do not need a restaurant to start. You do not need a huge amount of money. What you need is a food people want, a way to reach them, and the discipline to cook well, price fairly, and deliver on time. This guide walks you through the whole journey: choosing what to sell, working out your costs, setting up your kitchen, handling food safety, finding customers, taking payments, and growing from a side hustle into a real business.

## Choose What to Sell

The first decision is what food to sell, and the best answer is usually what you already cook well. If your jollof rice makes people ask for a second plate, sell jollof rice. If your soups are your strength, sell soups. Customers come back for taste, not for variety. It is better to sell three dishes brilliantly than to sell fifteen dishes averagely.

Daily lunch packs suit office areas in Ikeja or Victoria Island: rice, stew, chicken and a drink for ₦3,500 to ₦6,000. In residential areas, families order soups in bulk — a litre of egusi or vegetable soup for ₦8,000 to ₦15,000. Near a university, students want affordable, filling food at ₦1,500 to ₦3,000. Snacks and small chops, baked goods, and weekly meal plans also sell well. A focused menu is easier to shop for, easier to cook, and easier for customers to remember.

## Know Your Costs Before You Price

Many home food businesses fail because the owner never works out the real cost of a plate. If you sell a lunch pack for ₦4,000 but it costs you ₦3,800 to make, you are working for almost nothing, and one bad week will wipe you out. Before you set a price, cost every dish properly.

List every ingredient and its cost: rice, tomatoes, pepper, onions, oil, chicken, seasoning, and salt. Then add the costs people forget: gas, water, packaging, transport to the market, and delivery. A takeaway pack costs ₦300 to ₦800 depending on the quality, and a branded sticker adds a little more. If you use a dispatch rider, that is ₦1,000 to ₦3,000 per delivery in Lagos, depending on distance. If you cook with a gas cylinder, work out how many cooking days one refill covers and divide the refill cost across them.

Add all of this up for one plate or one litre, and you have your true cost. Then add your profit. A healthy target is a profit margin of 30 to 40 percent on each plate after all costs. That means if a lunch pack costs you ₦2,800 to make and deliver, you should sell it for at least ₦4,000. If the market will not pay that, you have three choices: reduce the cost by buying ingredients in bulk, reduce the portion, or choose a different dish with a better margin. Never compete by selling at a loss. Cheap prices attract customers you cannot afford to keep.

Keep a simple record book or a spreadsheet on your phone. Each day, write down what you spent on ingredients, gas, packaging and delivery, and what you earned. At the end of the week, add it up. This is your business in numbers, and it tells you whether you are making money or just being busy.

## Set Up Your Kitchen for Business

Your kitchen is your factory, and it needs to work like one. You do not need a commercial kitchen to start, but you do need space, cleanliness, and the right basic equipment.

First, make space. Clear a shelf or a corner for business ingredients, separate from your family's food. Buy ingredients in bulk where you can: a bag of rice, a carton of chicken, a jerry can of oil. Bulk buying at Mile 12 market, Oyingbo, or a wholesale market near you can cut your costs by 20 to 30 percent compared with buying small quantities every day.

Second, get the basic equipment. You will need large pots, a deep freezer or a reliable fridge, food-grade containers, a weighing scale, and good packaging. A deep freezer is one of the best investments for a home food business: it lets you buy meat in bulk and store cooked food safely. A second-hand deep freezer in good condition can cost ₦200,000 to ₦400,000, and it pays for itself in saved food and bulk buying.

Third, keep it clean. Your kitchen is your shop floor, and customers judge your food by your hygiene. Wash your hands before cooking and after handling raw meat. Keep raw meat separate from cooked food. Cover food at all times. Clean your pots, surfaces and containers with soap and hot water after every cooking session. Do not cook when you are sick. A single case of food poisoning can destroy a reputation that took months to build, and in a WhatsApp world, one bad review spreads fast.

Fourth, think about packaging. Good packaging is not a luxury; it is part of your product. Food that arrives in a leaking, flimsy container looks cheap, however good it tastes. Use sturdy, food-grade packs that seal properly. Add a sticker with your business name and phone number. Customers keep your number when it is on the pack, and they share it with friends. Packaging costs money, but it is one of the cheapest forms of advertising you will ever buy.

## Food Safety and the Law

Food safety is not optional, and the law takes it seriously. In Lagos, the state government regulates food businesses, and local government areas have environmental health officers who inspect food premises. As a home food business, you should take the rules seriously from the start, even if nobody has inspected your kitchen yet.

The basics are simple: cook food thoroughly, keep hot food hot and cold food cold, store food at safe temperatures, and use clean water. Do not leave cooked food sitting at room temperature for hours. Refrigerate or freeze leftovers promptly. Label food with the date it was cooked. Throw away food that has gone bad, even if it hurts — selling it is worse.

You should also get a food handler's medical certificate. This is a certificate showing that you, and anyone who helps you cook, have been screened and are fit to handle food. You can get one from a government hospital or an approved laboratory, usually for a few thousand naira, and it is valid for a set period. It protects you and your customers, and it is something corporate clients and event organisers may ask for before they give you a contract.

Register your business. At the very least, register your business name with the Corporate Affairs Commission. It costs a few tens of thousands of naira, it protects your name, and it makes you look serious to customers and banks. You should also consider registering with SMEDAN, the Small and Medium Enterprises Development Agency of Nigeria, which is free and gives you a certificate that helps with loans, grants and contracts. As your business grows, open a business bank account and get a Tax Identification Number. None of this is glamorous, but it is what separates a hustle from a business.

## Find Your First Customers

Your first customers are the people around you: your neighbours, your church members, your colleagues, your friends, and their friends. Tell them what you are selling, and give them a reason to try it. A free tasting for a small group, a discount on the first order, or a small extra portion for a new customer costs you little and brings you your first reviews.

WhatsApp Business is your most important tool. Set up a WhatsApp Business account with your business name, a clear profile photo of your food, your opening hours, and a catalogue showing your dishes and prices. Post your daily menu every morning by 9 am, with a good photo of the food. Use broadcast lists to send your menu to regular customers — but only to people who have asked to receive it, or they will block you. Share your menu in estate groups, office groups, and church groups where you are a member, but do it once a day, not every hour.

Instagram and TikTok work well for food, because people eat with their eyes first. Post short videos of your cooking, your packaging, and your deliveries. Show the steam rising from a pot of soup, the colours of a well-plated lunch pack, the moment a customer receives their order. You do not need a professional camera — a clean phone camera and good natural light are enough. Post consistently, two or three times a week, and reply to every comment and message quickly.

Do not ignore offline marketing. A simple flyer in your estate, a banner outside your gate, and a food stall at a local event can bring customers that social media never will. Tell your local security guard, your hairdresser, and your market women what you sell — they know everyone, and word of mouth is still the most powerful marketing in Nigeria.

## Take Orders and Get Paid

Set up a simple ordering system. A Google Form, a WhatsApp message, or a simple order form on your phone is enough to start. Ask for the customer's name, phone number, order, delivery address, and delivery time. Confirm every order with a message, and confirm the price and the delivery fee before you cook. This avoids arguments later.

Take payment upfront, or at least a deposit, for large orders. For daily lunch packs, payment on delivery is common, but for orders above ₦20,000, ask for a deposit of 50 percent before you cook. This protects you from customers who order and disappear, and it gives you cash to buy ingredients. Use a bank transfer to your business account, or a payment link from Paystack or Flutterwave, which lets customers pay with a card or a transfer. Accept Opay and Moniepoint transfers too, since many customers use them. Avoid cash where you can, because it is harder to track, and always send a receipt or a confirmation message after payment.

Keep a delivery promise you can keep. If you say food will arrive by 12:30 pm, make sure it does. Late food is cold food, and cold food is a lost customer. If you deliver yourself, plan your route so that the furthest customer gets their food first, or use a dispatch rider for areas you cannot cover. Build a small list of reliable dispatch riders in your area, and agree on prices in advance so there are no surprises.

## Grow from Side Hustle to Real Business

Once you have regular customers and a steady income, start thinking about growth. Raise your prices slowly as your reputation grows — a small increase of ₦500 on a plate is easier for customers to accept than a sudden jump of ₦2,000. Add a new dish only when your current menu is running well. Hire help when you need it: a neighbour or a relative who can chop, pack, or deliver, paid per day or per order.

Save a fixed amount from every week's profit, even if it is only ₦5,000 or ₦10,000. This is your growth fund, for a freezer, a better stove, a branded banner, or a small shop. Do not spend all your profit on personal needs, or the business will never grow.

Consider a small shop or a food stall when your home kitchen can no longer cope. A small space in a busy area, with a signboard and a few tables, can double your sales. Before you take that step, make sure your numbers support it: your daily sales should comfortably cover the rent, the extra staff, and the extra costs, with profit left over.

Think about catering. Weddings, birthdays, burials, and corporate events in Lagos and Abuja pay well for food, and a single event can be worth ₦200,000 to ₦1 million or more. Start with small events for people you know, deliver excellently, and ask for a video testimonial. Catering is the fastest way to grow a home food business, but it is also the most demanding, so take it on only when your kitchen and your team can handle it.

Finally, protect your reputation. Reply to complaints quickly and honestly. If a customer says the food was late or the portion was small, apologise, fix it, and offer a small gesture — a free drink, an extra portion, a discount on the next order. A customer whose complaint was handled well often becomes more loyal than a customer who never complained at all.

## Mistakes That Kill Home Food Businesses

The first mistake is inconsistent quality. If your jollof rice tastes different every time, customers will not trust you. Use the same recipe, the same measurements, and the same ingredients every time. Write your recipes down. If you hire help, teach them your exact method.

The second mistake is poor hygiene. A dirty kitchen, a hair in the food, or a pack that smells of the fridge will end a customer relationship instantly. Cleanliness is not negotiable.

The third mistake is taking too many orders. It is tempting to say yes to every order, but if you take on more than you can cook and deliver well, everyone gets late, cold food. Learn to say no, or to take orders a day in advance so you can plan.

The fourth mistake is not keeping records. If you do not know your costs, you do not know your profit. If you do not know your profit, you cannot grow. Record your income and your expenses every day.

The fifth mistake is mixing business money with personal money. When the business money sits in the same account as your personal money, it disappears. Open a separate account, or at least a separate wallet, for the business, and pay yourself a fixed amount each week.

## Your First 30 Days

Week one: choose your dish, cost it properly, set your price, and set up WhatsApp Business with your menu and prices. Cook for your family and a few friends, and ask for honest feedback.

Week two: start selling. Post your menu every morning, take orders, and deliver on time. Aim for ten to twenty paying customers. Keep records of every naira you spend and earn.

Week three: ask your happy customers for a review and a referral. Post their feedback on your WhatsApp status and Instagram. Fix any problems they mention. If a dish is not selling, drop it and focus on what sells.

Week four: review your numbers. Which dish made the most profit? Which customers order most often? Use the answers to plan your second month, and set a simple goal — for example, to double your daily orders, or to win your first office contract.

A home food business will not make you rich in a month, but it can give you a steady income, a skill that grows with you, and a business you are proud of. Start small, cook well, keep your kitchen clean, and let your customers do the marketing for you. The food will speak for itself.
