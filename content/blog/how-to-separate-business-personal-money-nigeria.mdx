If you run a small business in Nigeria, you probably know this feeling. Money comes in from a customer on Monday. By Wednesday, part of it has gone to fuel, part to food, part to a family request, and part to restocking — and you cannot say exactly how much went where. At the end of the month, you are not sure whether the business made a profit or a loss. You only know the account balance looks low.

That is the cost of mixing business and personal money. It is the most common financial mistake among Nigerian small businesses, from tailors in Yaba to food vendors in Lekki, from Instagram sellers in Abuja to phone-accessories traders in Computer Village. It does not mean you are careless. It usually means nobody showed you a simple system, and the business started informally — one bank account, one wallet, one stream of cash.

This guide gives you that system, written for real Nigerian conditions: cash and transfers, POS and Opay, Moniepoint and Kuda, WhatsApp orders, market-day restocking, family obligations, and irregular income. You do not need an accountant to start — just separate accounts, a few rules, and a weekly habit that takes less than one hour.

## Why mixing money slowly kills a good business

A business can be busy every day and still die broke. When personal and business money share one account, four problems follow.

First, you cannot see profit. Profit is not the money in your account. Profit is what remains after you subtract the cost of goods, transport, packaging, data, rent, staff, and every other business expense from your sales. If personal spending sits in the same pot, the calculation is impossible. A trader who sold ₦800,000 worth of goods in a month may think she made ₦800,000. If the goods cost ₦550,000, transport cost ₦40,000, and shop rent is ₦50,000 a month, the real picture is very different — and only separation reveals it.

Second, you eat your working capital. Working capital is the money you need to buy the next batch of stock. When you dip into it for personal needs, the next order becomes smaller. Smaller stock means fewer sales. Fewer sales mean less money. Many Nigerian businesses shrink this way, slowly, without a single dramatic loss. One day the shop is half empty and the owner says business is slow. Often, business was fine. The capital was spent at home.

Third, you cannot plan or borrow. A bank, a cooperative, a fintech lender, or an investor will ask for bank statements and records. If your statement shows school fees, birthday contributions, betting, and stock purchases all mixed together, no lender can tell whether your business is healthy. Even if you never plan to borrow, you still need clean records to price properly, to know when to raise prices, and to decide whether a new product line is worth it.

Fourth, tax and registration become harder than they need to be. A registered business with clean records can file taxes, get a Tax Identification Number, and deal with the Federal Inland Revenue Service or a state revenue service with confidence. A business with no records guesses — and guessing with tax usually means overpaying, underpaying, or avoiding the conversation entirely until a problem arrives.

Separation is not about being rich. It is about knowing where you stand, so you can make decisions based on facts instead of feelings.

## Step 1: Open a separate business account this week

This is the foundation. Everything else is easier once the money flows through different accounts.

You do not need a corporate account with a big bank on day one, although that is the goal for a registered business. Start with what you can open this week:

1. **Open a second personal or business account in a different bank or fintech.** If your personal money lives in GTBank, open the business account in Kuda, Moniepoint, Opay, Palmpay, or another bank. The point is a different account number, a different card, and a different app login.
2. **Use the business account for all business money in, and all business money out.** Customer transfers, POS payments, Paystack or Flutterwave settlements, and cash deposits go here. Stock purchases, transport, packaging, data for the business phone, and rent come out of here.
3. **Keep your personal account for salary, food, rent at home, school fees, and family support.** Nothing business-related should come out of it, except a deliberate transfer that you record as a loan or an investment.
4. **Get a separate POS or payment link for the business.** If you use a Moniepoint or Opay POS, register it in the business name and link it to the business account. If you collect payments through Paystack or Flutterwave, set the settlement account to the business account, not your personal one.
5. **Tell customers the business account details, not your personal ones.** Put the business account name and number on your invoice, your WhatsApp catalogue, your Instagram bio link, and your receipts. Customers in Nigeria are used to paying a business name. It also builds trust.

If your business is registered with the Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC), open a proper corporate account. Most Nigerian banks will ask for your CAC certificate, Tax Identification Number, valid ID, proof of address, and a passport photograph. Ask for the exact list before you go, so you do not make two trips.

If you are not yet registered, do not wait for registration to separate your money. Open a second account now in your own name and label it clearly in your banking app as the business account. Separation first, registration next. A clean six months of separate business transactions will also make CAC registration and a corporate account much easier when you get there.

A practical Lagos example: a hair vendor in Lekki Phase 1 was receiving all payments into her personal Access Bank account. She opened a Kuda business account, changed her Paystack settlement to Kuda, and printed the Kuda details on her invoices. Within one month, she could see that she spent ₦180,000 on hair, ₦35,000 on delivery, and ₦25,000 on packaging — numbers she had never seen clearly before. That clarity let her raise prices on two slow-moving products by ₦5,000 each without losing customers.

## Step 2: Pay yourself a salary, even if it is small

This is the step most Nigerian business owners resist, and it is the most important one.

When you do not pay yourself a fixed salary, you take money whenever you need it. That is the same as having no boundary at all. A salary creates the boundary. The business pays you, like it would pay any staff member, and the rest of the money stays in the business.

Here is how to set it:

1. **Work out your real monthly personal cost.** Write down rent, food, transport, school fees, data, electricity, and a small amount for family support and emergencies. Be honest. If your total is ₦250,000 a month, write ₦250,000. If it is ₦120,000, write ₦120,000.
2. **Check what the business can actually afford.** Look at your average monthly profit over the last three months, not your best month. If your average profit is ₦300,000 and your personal cost is ₦250,000, you can pay yourself ₦200,000 and leave ₦100,000 in the business to grow. If your average profit is ₦150,000 and your personal cost is ₦250,000, you have a pricing or cost problem to fix — taking ₦250,000 anyway will drain the business.
3. **Pick a fixed salary and a fixed pay day.** For example, ₦150,000 on the 28th of every month. Transfer it from the business account to your personal account on that day, every month, and record it as "Owner salary".
4. **Do not take extra money outside salary except in a real emergency.** If you must take extra, record it as a loan from the business, write down the amount and date, and pay it back from a future salary. A loan you track is very different from money that quietly disappears.
5. **Review the salary every three months.** If profit grows, raise the salary a little. If profit falls, lower it temporarily and fix the business problem, rather than draining the account and hoping next month is better.

A salary forces you to see the real cost of running the business, including your own labour. A business that cannot pay its owner a modest salary and still survive needs higher prices, lower costs, or more sales — and you will only discover that if you separate the numbers.

If your income is very irregular — for example, you are a wedding photographer in Abuja who earns heavily in December and very little in February — pay yourself a smaller, steady monthly salary and keep a buffer in the business account during good months. The buffer is what pays your salary in slow months. That is exactly how a salary works in a real company, and your business deserves the same discipline.

## Step 3: Record every transaction, even cash

A separate account helps, but it is not enough on its own. Cash is still a large part of Nigerian commerce. Market purchases, transport, and small customer payments often happen in cash. If cash is not recorded, your books are incomplete.

You do not need complicated accounting software to start. You need a simple system you will actually use.

**Option A: A notebook and a daily routine.** Buy a hardcover ledger. Each day, write the date, what came in, what went out, and the balance. Example: "25 Nov — Sold 4 Ankara dresses, ₦120,000 in. Bought fabric, ₦45,000 out. Transport, ₦5,000 out. Balance carried forward." Do this every evening before you close. It takes ten minutes.

**Option B: A spreadsheet on your phone.** Google Sheets is free and works on MTN or Airtel data. Create columns for Date, Description, Money In, Money Out, Category, and Balance. Enter each transaction as it happens. Share the sheet with yourself on a second email so you do not lose it if your phone is stolen.

**Option C: A simple bookkeeping app.** Wave, QuickBooks, and local apps like Kippa or Bumpa are used by many Nigerian small businesses. Kippa and Bumpa are built for Nigerian traders and let you record sales, track debtors, and send receipts on WhatsApp. Pick one app and use it every day. The best app is the one you will open daily, not the one with the most features.

Whichever option you choose, follow these rules:

- Record cash the same day. Memory fades fast, especially when you handle many small cash payments.
- Keep receipts for stock, transport, and large expenses. Photograph paper receipts with your phone and save them in a folder named by month, for example "Nov 2026 receipts". Paper fades in Nigerian heat and humidity. A photo lasts.
- Separate your categories. At minimum, track Sales, Cost of Goods, Transport, Packaging, Data and Airtime, Rent, Staff, and Owner Salary. When you review the month, you will see which category is eating your profit.
- Reconcile weekly. Every Sunday evening, compare your notebook or app with your bank statement. If the bank says ₦450,000 came in and your book says ₦430,000, find the missing ₦20,000. It is usually a transfer you forgot to record or a cash sale you did not write down.

A trader in Onitsha who sells phone accessories told us he started writing down every sale in a small notebook after years of guessing. In the first month, he discovered that ₦68,000 had gone to "small small" expenses — transport, food while at the shop, and airtime — that he had never counted as business costs. He was not losing money on his products. He was losing it on unrecorded daily spending. That single insight changed how he priced his goods.

## Step 4: Set up three pots inside the business

Once money is separate and recorded, the next step is to give every naira a job. A simple method used by many small businesses is the three-pot system.

**Pot 1: Operating money.** This is the money for day-to-day running — stock, transport, packaging, data, and small expenses. It should cover roughly one month of operating costs.

**Pot 2: Tax and obligations.** Set aside a percentage of every sale for tax, CAC annual returns, and other obligations. Even 5 percent of sales, moved to a separate savings account or a locked sub-account, prevents the shock of a tax bill you cannot pay. If you are a sole trader, speak to a tax consultant or your state revenue service about what applies to you. Do not wait until a demand notice arrives.

**Pot 3: Growth and emergency.** This is money for the next level — a bigger shop, a new product line, a website, or a slow month. Aim to keep at least one month of operating costs here as an emergency fund. If a generator breaks down or a supplier raises prices suddenly, this pot keeps you trading.

You can run this with sub-accounts in Kuda or PiggyVest, or with three separate savings accounts. The tool matters less than the habit: when money comes in, split it immediately. Do not wait until the end of the month to split what is left, because often nothing is left.

A simple split for a small retailer could be: 70 percent stays for operating costs and restocking, 10 percent goes to tax and obligations, 10 percent goes to growth and emergency, and 10 percent is your salary reserve. Adjust the percentages to fit your business, but keep the principle: split on arrival, not on leftovers.


## Step 5: Handle family requests, pricing, and monthly review

Budget a fixed family-support amount inside your personal salary, never from the till. If you pay yourself ₦200,000 a month, decide that ₦30,000 of it is for family support; when it is finished, the answer is that you have reached your limit this month. Record any stock you give away as an owner withdrawal at cost price, and lend only from your personal account after salary day. Price from real costs, not from what you need that week: add direct cost, a share of rent, transport, data and your salary, then a margin of 20 to 40 percent for most retail goods. Review prices every three months, because fuel, food and exchange rates move often in Nigeria. Finally, spend one hour on the last Sunday of each month reviewing what came in, what went out, who owes you, and which product made the most profit. Write the answers down; after six months you will know your business better than most owners in your market.

## A 30-day plan to get separated

If you are starting from zero, do not try to fix everything in one day. Follow this plan.

**Week 1:** Open the second account. Change your payment links and POS to settle into it. Tell your regular customers the new business account details. Start recording every sale and expense in a notebook or app.

**Week 2:** Work out your monthly personal cost and your average monthly profit. Set your salary and your pay day. Make the first salary transfer on that day.

**Week 3:** Set up the three pots — operating, tax, and growth — using sub-accounts or separate savings accounts. Move a small percentage of each sale into the tax and growth pots. Review your prices for your top five products or services.

**Week 4:** Do your first monthly review. Compare your records with your bank statement. Find one leak and fix it. Set a family-support limit inside your salary. Decide your salary for next month.

After 30 days you will not be perfect, and that is normal. Each month the system gets tighter, the numbers get clearer, and the decisions get easier.

## Final word: separation is freedom

Keeping business and personal money separate is not about rules for their own sake. It is about freedom. Freedom to know whether your business is working. Freedom to pay yourself without guilt. Freedom to grow, to borrow, to hire, and to take a holiday without the business collapsing behind you.

Start this week. Open the account, record the first sale, and pay yourself the first salary, even if it is small. In six months you will see a business that is clearer, calmer, and stronger — not because you worked harder, but because you finally gave your money a proper home. Your business deserves its own account. You deserve your own salary. Keep them separate, and both will grow.
