Most business owners only think about website backups on the day something goes wrong. A plugin update breaks the layout, a login gets compromised, a hosting bill lapses, or an edit deletes the wrong section. In that moment, the question is always the same: can we get the site back, and how quickly?

A backup and update care plan answers that question before the emergency happens. It is not complicated, and it does not require technical skill from the business owner. It requires a routine, someone responsible for it, and a backup that has actually been tested.

This post focuses specifically on that routine: what to back up, how often, how to handle updates safely, and what to do when a site breaks. For the broader picture of life after launch, including support and ongoing improvements, see our guide on [website maintenance basics](/blog/website-maintenance-101). This post is the practical plan you can hand to whoever manages your site.

## What a backup actually covers

A complete website backup has two parts, and you need both:

1. Your files: the design, pages, images, and code that make up the site.
2. Your database: the stored content behind the site, such as posts, pages, forms, product details, and settings, depending on how your site is built.

A backup of only one part is like photocopying half a document. Your hosting company may keep its own backups, but you should not rely on those alone. Hosting backups can be short-lived, hard to access in an emergency, or tied to the same account that has the problem. Your own backup, stored separately from your live site, is what gives you real control.

Also back up the things around the site that people forget: your domain and hosting login details kept safely, your logo and brand files, and a copy of your important page content. If you ever need to rebuild, those save days of work.

## The backup routine

A workable routine for a typical small business website looks like this:

- Daily or automatic backups if your site changes often, for example if you publish posts, receive orders, or collect form entries every day.
- Weekly backups at minimum for a brochure-style site that changes rarely.
- A manual backup before any major change, such as a redesign, a move to new hosting, or a large update.
- At least one copy stored away from your hosting account, for example with a separate storage service or a copy held by your developer or agency.

Set backups to run automatically where possible. A plan that depends on someone remembering to click a button every week will eventually be forgotten. Check once a month that backups are actually being created and that the files are not empty or corrupted. A backup nobody has checked is only a hope, not a plan.

Keep several recent backups, not just the latest one. If a problem goes unnoticed for a week, the newest backup may already contain the same problem. Keeping a few older copies gives you a clean version to return to.

## The update routine

Updates keep your site secure and working, but careless updates break sites. The safe routine is simple:

1. Back up first, every time, before updating anything.
2. Update in a sensible order: small plugins and themes first, then larger system updates, testing as you go.
3. Check the key pages after each round of updates: homepage, contact page, service pages, and any forms or checkout steps.
4. Fix or roll back immediately if something breaks, rather than leaving a broken site live while you investigate.

Never update everything at once on a Friday evening and walk away. Update when you or your developer have time to check the result and fix problems the same day. If your site uses a system with many plugins, ask your developer to review which ones are still needed. Unused plugins are a common source of security problems, and removing them reduces both risk and update headaches.

For a business owner, the main job is not performing updates yourself. It is making sure someone is responsible for them, that the routine above is followed, and that you receive a short confirmation when updates are done.

## Test your restore before you need it

This is the step most people skip, and it is the most important one. A backup is only useful if it can be restored.

At least twice a year, ask whoever manages your site to prove the backup works. That proof can be restoring the backup to a test location and confirming the pages, images, and forms load correctly. If the restore fails in a test, you have learned something valuable while your live site is still fine. If it fails during a real emergency, you have learned it too late.

Write down the restore steps in plain language: where backups are stored, who can access them, and who to call first. Keep that note somewhere other than the website itself, so it is available even if the site is down.

## When your site breaks: what to do

Stay calm and work through these steps in order:

1. Confirm the problem: check the site on your phone and on another connection, and note exactly what is broken, whether it is the whole site or one page.
2. Contact your developer or host promptly, and tell them when the problem started and what changed recently.
3. Do not keep editing or updating a broken site in the hope that it fixes itself. Further changes can overwrite the clean backup you need.
4. Restore from the most recent clean backup if the problem cannot be fixed quickly, then investigate the cause on a copy, not on the live site.
5. Once the site is back, check the key pages and forms, then note what caused the problem so the routine can be improved.

If your site collects customer enquiries or orders, tell affected customers honestly and promptly once you know what happened. A short, clear message preserves more trust than silence.

## Questions to ask your developer or host

Use these questions to check whether your site is properly covered:

- Where are our backups stored, and are any stored outside our hosting account?
- How often do backups run, and how many recent copies are kept?
- When was the last time a restore was tested?
- Who performs updates, and how are they checked afterwards?
- If our site goes down, who do we contact first, and what happens next?

If the answers are vague, that is your signal to put a proper plan in place. Good providers answer these questions clearly, because they already follow a routine.

## A simple care plan you can adopt today

Here is a one-page version you can use immediately:

- Automatic backups running, with a copy stored separately from hosting
- A backup taken before every major change
- Updates performed carefully, with a backup first and a check afterwards
- Backups reviewed monthly and restore tested twice a year
- One named person responsible, and a written note of where backups live and who to call

That is the whole plan. It takes little time to set up, and it turns a website emergency from a crisis into a routine fix.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does my hosting company already back up my site?**

Many hosts keep backups, but the details vary widely. Some keep them only briefly, some charge for restoration, and some store them on the same system as your live site. Treat hosting backups as a bonus, not your plan, and keep your own separate copy.

**How much storage do backups need?**

For a typical small business site, backups are modest in size, mostly text and images. Media-heavy sites need more space, but storage is usually a small cost compared with rebuilding a lost site from scratch.

**Can I back up a site myself without technical skill?**

Yes, if your site uses a platform with a backup tool or plugin, and your developer shows you the steps once. The important part is that backups run automatically and someone checks them, not that you personally perform every step.

**What if I have no backup and my site is already broken?**

Contact your developer or host immediately and ask what copies exist on their side. Do not overwrite anything until they have checked. Afterwards, put the routine in this guide in place so the same situation cannot happen again.
