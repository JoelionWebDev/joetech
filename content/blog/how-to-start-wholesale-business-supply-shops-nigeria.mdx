Every morning in markets across Nigeria, from Balogun in Lagos to Ariaria in Aba to Bodija in Ibadan, shop owners open their stores and check what they need to restock. A provision shop needs rice, oil, and milk. A pharmacy needs drugs and toiletries. A cosmetics shop needs creams and soaps. A phone accessories shop needs chargers and earphones. Somebody has to supply those goods, and that somebody can be you.

A wholesale business is simple at its core. You buy goods in large quantities at a low price, and you sell them in smaller quantities to shops at a slightly higher price. The shops then sell to the final customer at the retail price. Your profit is the difference between what you paid and what you charged the shop, multiplied by the number of shops you supply.

It is one of the steadiest businesses in Nigeria, because shops always need stock. A shop that runs out of a popular product loses sales every day it is empty, so a reliable supplier is valuable. If you deliver on time, keep your prices fair, and stock the products shops actually want, you can build a business with regular weekly orders and a predictable income.

This guide explains how to start a wholesale business supplying shops in Nigeria, step by step. You will learn how to choose your products, find trustworthy suppliers, set prices that leave a profit, find shop owners who will buy from you, and manage money and credit without running into trouble.

## Understand How Wholesale Works in Nigeria

In wholesale, your customer is not the person who eats the food or wears the clothes. Your customer is the shop owner, and the shop owner has different needs from a retail customer. A retail customer buys one item and cares about the brand and the look. A shop owner buys in bulk and cares about the price, the margin, the speed of delivery, and whether the product will sell quickly.

Trust is also central. Shop owners in Nigeria often buy on credit, paying you after they have sold the goods. They will only do that with a supplier they trust, and you will only offer credit to a shop owner you trust. That trust is built slowly, one honest delivery at a time.

## Choose What to Supply

The first decision is what products to supply, and it is the most important one. Do not try to supply everything. A wholesaler who supplies foodstuff, cosmetics, electronics, and building materials all at once will have money tied up in too many places and will know none of them well. Pick one category, learn it deeply, and grow from there.

Look at what shops in your area need every week. Provision shops need rice, beans, garri, vegetable oil, palm oil, sugar, milk, tea, noodles, spaghetti, seasoning cubes, and tomato paste. Cosmetics shops need creams, soaps, lotions, perfumes, and hair products. Pharmacies need drugs, supplements, and toiletries. Phone shops need chargers, earphones, phone cases, and screen guards. Building materials shops need cement, paint, and fittings.

Consider your starting capital. Some products need a large amount of money to start, such as electronics or building materials, where one carton can cost hundreds of thousands of naira. Others, such as provisions or toiletries, let you start with a smaller amount and grow gradually. Start with what you can afford, and reinvest your profit to expand your range.

## Find Reliable Suppliers

Your supplier is the foundation of your business. A good supplier gives you genuine goods at a fair price, delivers on time, and does not disappear when you need stock urgently. A bad supplier gives you fake goods, changes prices without warning, or takes your money and delivers late. Choosing well matters more than almost anything else.

Start in the big markets. In Lagos, Balogun, Idumota, and Mile 12 are known for different goods. In Onitsha, the main market supplies traders across the South East. In Kano, the Kantin Kwari market is known for textiles. In Aba, Ariaria is known for clothing and shoes. Visit the market, walk around, and compare prices from several sellers before you buy. The first price you hear is rarely the best price.

Build a relationship with two or three suppliers, not just one. If your main supplier runs out of stock or increases prices suddenly, you need a backup. Pay promptly, especially at the start, because a supplier who trusts you will give you better prices and sometimes credit. Keep a record of every supplier: their name, phone number, location, and the prices they charge, so you can compare and negotiate.

## Register Your Business and Set Up Properly

A wholesale business needs to look like a business, not a side hustle. Register your business name with the CAC, because shop owners and suppliers take a registered business more seriously, and it helps you open a business bank account. A business account also keeps your business money separate from your personal money, which is essential when you are handling large amounts.

Open a business bank account in your business name. Use it for all your buying and selling, so you can track your money clearly. When you pay a supplier, pay from the business account. When a shop owner pays you, ask them to pay into the business account. At the end of the month, you should be able to see exactly how much came in and how much went out.

Keep proper records from day one. Use a notebook or a spreadsheet to record every purchase: the date, the supplier, the product, the quantity, and the price. Record every sale the same way: the date, the shop owner, the product, the quantity, the price, and whether they paid in full or owe you money. These records are the difference between a business and a gamble. They tell you which products make money, which customers pay on time, and whether you are growing or shrinking.

## Price Your Goods for a Real Profit

Pricing in wholesale is a careful balance. If your price is too high, shop owners will buy from someone else. If your price is too low, you will sell a lot and make nothing. Your price must cover what you paid the supplier, the cost of transporting the goods, the cost of storage, and a profit for you, while still leaving the shop owner enough margin to make their own profit.

Start by knowing your full cost. If a carton of noodles costs you ₦15,000 from the supplier, and transport costs ₦1,000 per carton, and you spend ₦500 per carton on storage and handling, your real cost is ₦16,500. If you sell that carton for ₦17,500, you make ₦1,000, and the shop owner can sell the packs inside for a profit. If you sell it for ₦16,800, you make only ₦300, which is too little to sustain the business.

Know the retail price of each product. A shop owner will not buy from you if they cannot make a profit after paying your price. If a pack of noodles retails for ₦500, and there are forty packs in a carton, the shop owner's total revenue is ₦20,000. Your price must be low enough that they can make a reasonable profit after paying you. Check retail prices in the shops you want to supply, and work backwards from there.

## Find Shops and Win Them as Customers

Your first customers will come from visiting shops in person. Make a list of shops in your area that sell the products you supply, and visit them one by one. Introduce yourself, tell them what you supply, show them your price list, and leave your phone number. Do not expect every shop to buy on the first visit. Many will want to see you again, or will buy a small quantity to test you first.

Start with a small area and cover it well. If you live in Lekki, start with the shops in Lekki, Ajah, and Sangotedo before you try to cover the whole of Lagos. If you live in Ibadan, start with Bodija, Dugbe, and Ring Road. Covering a small area well is better than spreading yourself thin across a whole state.

Be reliable above all else. If you promise delivery on Tuesday morning, deliver on Tuesday morning. If you promise a price, keep it. Shop owners talk to each other, and a reputation for reliability will bring you new customers without advertising. A reputation for broken promises will close doors across a whole market.

Use WhatsApp Business to stay in touch. Create a broadcast list of your shop customers, and send them a weekly message with your current price list and any new products. When a product is about to run out, message them first. When you get a new product, tell them. Regular, useful contact keeps you at the front of their mind when they need to restock.

## Deliver Fast and Manage Credit Carefully

Delivery is a big part of a wholesale business. Shop owners need their stock quickly, and a supplier who delivers fast wins. If you have a vehicle, use it. If not, hire a van or a truck for bulk deliveries, or use a dispatch rider for smaller orders. Agree on delivery fees in advance, and be clear about whether delivery is free above a certain order size. Many wholesalers offer free delivery for orders above a set amount, such as ₦100,000, and charge for smaller orders.

Credit is the biggest risk in wholesale. Many shop owners will ask to buy on credit, paying you after they sell the goods. Credit can grow your sales, but it can also destroy your business if customers do not pay. Start with cash or bank transfer only, and offer credit only to customers you know well and who have paid promptly for several orders.

Set clear credit rules and write them down. Decide how many days of credit you offer, such as seven or fourteen days, and what happens if a customer does not pay on time. Do not give credit to a new customer. Do not give a large credit to a small shop. Do not give credit to two customers at once when your cash is low. Keep a record of every credit sale: the customer, the amount, the date, and the due date, and follow up politely but firmly when payment is due.

If a customer consistently pays late, reduce their credit or stop it altogether. It is better to lose one customer than to lose your working capital. Your money is the lifeblood of the business, and when it is stuck in unpaid credit, you cannot buy new stock, and the whole business stops.

## Grow Your Wholesale Business Steadily

Growth in wholesale comes from more customers, more products, and bigger orders, in that order. First, get more shops ordering from you regularly. Then, add products that your existing customers already want. A shop owner who buys noodles from you may also want to buy seasoning and oil. Adding products your customers already need is easier than finding new customers for a new product.

Reinvest your profit. In the early months, do not spend your profit on personal items. Use it to buy more stock, to rent better storage, or to buy a small vehicle for deliveries. Each reinvestment makes the business stronger and lets you serve more customers.

Keep learning about your market. Prices change, new products appear, and customer tastes shift. Talk to your shop customers regularly and ask what is selling, what is not selling, and what they wish you supplied. Talk to your suppliers about new products and price changes. A wholesaler who knows the market can buy at the right time and sell at the right price.

## Common Mistakes New Wholesalers Make

The first mistake is starting with too many products. A new wholesaler who buys a little of everything will have money spread thin and will not be able to offer a good price on anything. Start with a few products you know well, build a customer base, and add products gradually.

The second mistake is giving too much credit too early. A new wholesaler who gives credit to every shop owner who asks will soon find their money stuck in unpaid debts and their shelves empty. Start with cash sales, build trust slowly, and offer credit only to proven customers, in small amounts, with clear due dates.

The third mistake is buying fake or expired goods because they are cheap. A shop owner who discovers fake goods will never buy from you again, and will warn other shop owners about you. Always check the goods, the expiry date, and the packaging before you pay, and buy from suppliers you trust.

The fourth mistake is not keeping records. A wholesaler who does not record purchases, sales, and credit will not know whether they are making a profit or a loss, or who owes them money. Keep a simple record from day one, and review it every week. A notebook is enough to start, but a spreadsheet is better as you grow.

The fifth mistake is neglecting delivery. A wholesaler who takes an order and delivers it three days late will lose that customer to a supplier who delivers the same day. Treat delivery as part of your product. Plan your routes, keep your vehicle in good condition, and communicate with customers if there is a delay.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much capital do I need to start a wholesale business in Nigeria?

You can start small with ₦200,000 to ₦500,000 if you choose products such as provisions or toiletries and start with a few shops in your area. Larger products such as electronics or building materials need much more capital. Start with what you can afford, prove the business works, and grow by reinvesting your profit.

### Do I need a shop or warehouse to start?

You need a secure, dry place to store your goods, but it does not have to be a large shop on a main road at the start. A small room, a secure space at home, or a small rented space in a market area can work. As your stock grows, rent a proper warehouse that is easy for vehicles to reach.

### How do I find shop owners who will buy from me?

Visit shops in person, introduce yourself, and leave a price list and your phone number. Start with shops in your own area, and offer a small incentive for the first order, such as free delivery. Follow up with a WhatsApp message, and be reliable on your first delivery. Good service will bring repeat orders and referrals.

### Should I give credit to shop owners?

Only after they have proven themselves. Start with cash or bank transfer, and offer credit only to customers who have paid promptly for several orders. Set a clear credit limit and a due date, keep a record of every credit sale, and follow up firmly when payment is due. Never let credit grow so large that you cannot buy new stock.

### How do I avoid fake products from suppliers?

Buy from trusted suppliers, importers, or manufacturers, and always check the goods before you pay. Open a carton, check the expiry date and the packaging, and compare the price with other suppliers. If a price is far below the market price, be suspicious. Building a relationship with a reliable supplier is worth more than a one-time cheap deal.
