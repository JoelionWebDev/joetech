Every Nigerian business owner who buys stock has a story like this, or knows someone who does. A supplier on Instagram offers goods at a price that seems too good to miss. You chat, you agree, you transfer the money — and then the messages slow down, the phone stops ringing, and the goods never arrive. Sometimes the supplier was never real at all. Sometimes they were real but unreliable. Either way, the money is gone, and for a small business, that loss can mean no stock for the week, no sales, and no profit.

Verifying a supplier before you pay is not about distrust. It is about protecting your business the same way you lock your shop at night. Most suppliers in Nigeria are honest people trying to earn a living, and a proper check will not offend them. In fact, a genuine supplier will usually welcome your questions, because they show you are a serious buyer. The suppliers who get angry, rush you, or refuse to answer simple questions are exactly the ones you need to worry about.

This guide gives you a practical checklist you can use before you send money to any new supplier, whether you found them on Instagram, in a WhatsApp group, at a market, or through a friend. You will learn how to check their business identity, how to judge their online reputation, how to confirm they are where they say they are, how to test them with a small order, and how to pay in the safest way available. It takes a little time, but it is far cheaper than losing your money to a scam.

## Start With Identity: Who Exactly Are You Paying?

Before you discuss prices, find out who the supplier is. Ask for their full business name, the name of the person you are dealing with, their phone number, and their business address. A real business can answer these questions without hesitation. Write the answers down. If the person gives you a different name each time you ask, or refuses to share a business name at all, stop there. You have already learned enough.

Next, check whether the business is registered. In Nigeria, most formal businesses are registered with the Corporate Affairs Commission, known as CAC. You can search for a business name on the CAC public search portal to see whether it exists in the registry. A registered business is not a guarantee of honesty — registered businesses can still disappoint you — but registration shows that the business is established enough to have gone through a formal process, and it gives you a real name to hold on to if something goes wrong. If a supplier claims to be a large company but has no registration and no traceable business name, treat that claim with caution.

Also check the bank account you are asked to pay into. The account name should match the business name or the name of the person you are dealing with. If a supplier called "Ada's Fabrics Ltd" asks you to pay into a personal account under a completely different name, with no explanation, that is a warning sign. Ask why. Sometimes there is an innocent reason, such as a family member's account, but a genuine supplier will explain it clearly. A supplier who cannot explain where your money is going is not a supplier you should trust with it.

## Check Their Online Presence and Reputation

A supplier's online presence is often the fastest way to judge them. Search for their business name on Google and on Instagram, Facebook, and TikTok. A real supplier usually has a history: posts going back months, photos of actual stock, customers commenting on their posts, and replies from the supplier. Look at the dates. An account created last week, with ten posts all uploaded on the same day, is not a history. It is a costume.

Read the comments, not just the posts. Are customers saying they received their goods? Are there complaints about late delivery or wrong items, and did the supplier respond? A supplier with a few complaints and polite replies is normal — every business has unhappy customers sometimes. A supplier whose comments are turned off, or whose page has no customer interaction at all, deserves a second look. Also search for the business name together with words like "scam" or "fraud" and see what comes up. Other victims often warn people online, and a quick search can save you from becoming the next one.

Ask the supplier for references. A genuine supplier should be able to give you the contact of one or two customers you can speak to, or at least point you to reviews on their page. If you know other business owners in your line of trade, ask them too. In markets like Balogun, Onitsha Main Market, or Computer Village, reputation travels fast, and a quiet question to a fellow trader can tell you more than an hour of online searching. Never feel shy about checking. Every careful buyer does it, and honest suppliers expect it.

## Confirm Their Location and See the Goods

A supplier who will not show you where they operate is a supplier you should not pay in advance. Ask for their shop address or warehouse location, and if you are in the same city, visit in person before your first big order. Seeing the shop, the stock, and the people behind the business is the strongest verification there is. Take note of whether the shop looks like a real, running business — customers coming and going, stock on the shelves, staff who know the products.

If you are in a different city, ask for a live video call, not just photos. Photos can be downloaded from the internet. A live video call, where the supplier walks around their shop and shows you the actual goods with your name or today's date written on a piece of paper beside them, is much harder to fake. If a supplier refuses a video call without a good reason, or keeps promising to call and never does, take that seriously. A genuine supplier with real stock has nothing to lose from showing it to you.

Also ask for a sample or a clear photo of the exact product you are buying, including its packaging and any brand markings. If you are ordering goods that are supposed to be original — branded shoes, electronics, or cosmetics — ask how the supplier sources them and whether they can show proof of purchase from their own supplier. Be especially careful with goods offered far below the normal market price. A price that is much lower than everywhere else is not always a bargain. Sometimes it is bait, and sometimes the goods are fake. If the price seems impossible, ask yourself what makes it possible, and do not accept an answer that does not make sense.

## Test With a Small Order Before You Commit

The safest way to verify a supplier is to do business with them on a small scale first. Place a small test order — one or two items, or the smallest quantity they will sell — and see how they handle it. Do they confirm the order clearly? Do they deliver on the day they promised? Is the quality what they described? Do they respond to your messages after you have paid? A supplier who handles a small order well has earned the right to a bigger one. A supplier who fails a small order has saved you from a large loss.

Agree on the terms in writing before you pay, even if the agreement is just a WhatsApp message. Write down the product, the quantity, the price, the delivery date, the delivery location, and what happens if the goods arrive damaged or wrong. Then ask the supplier to confirm the same details back to you. That written record protects both sides, and it makes it much harder for anyone to change the story later. Save the screenshots of the conversation, the payment receipt, and the delivery waybill. If everything goes well, you will never need them. If it does not, they are your evidence.

Be careful with pressure. A supplier who says "pay now or the price goes up in one hour", or "only one piece left, send the money immediately", is using urgency to stop you from thinking. Real stock does run out, and real prices do change, but a genuine supplier will give you time to verify and will still be there tomorrow. If the deal only works when you pay without checking, the deal is not worth doing. Walk away, and let the supplier come back to you with a proper offer.

## Pay in the Safest Way You Can

How you pay matters as much as who you pay. Never pay the full amount in advance to a supplier you have never worked with. A common and fair arrangement is to pay a deposit, with the balance paid when the goods arrive or when you have confirmed dispatch. For a first order, paying on delivery, if the supplier offers it, is the safest option of all. If the supplier insists on full payment upfront and refuses any other arrangement, ask yourself why, and consider whether the order is worth the risk.

Always pay through a traceable method. A bank transfer leaves a record: the account number, the account name, and the time of payment. Keep the receipt and the transfer confirmation. Avoid paying in cash to a stranger with no receipt, and avoid sending money through informal channels where there is no record of who received it. If you use a payment link or a fintech app such as Paystack, Flutterwave, or OPay, you still get a receipt, which is useful if you need to prove the payment later. Whatever method you use, the rule is the same: if you cannot prove you paid, you cannot claim it back.

Be very careful with payment details that change at the last minute. If a supplier sends you one account number, and then just before payment sends a different one, stop and call them on a number you already have to confirm. Criminals sometimes take over a supplier's WhatsApp account and send their own account details to customers. A quick phone call to a known number can prevent a loss that no one can undo. And never share your bank PIN, your one-time password, or your card details with a supplier for any reason. A real supplier will never ask for them.

## Red Flags, and What to Do If You Have Been Scammed

Some warning signs should make you stop immediately. The supplier has no verifiable address or business name. Their prices are far below every other seller. They refuse a video call or a visit. They pressure you to pay quickly. They change their account details. They ask you to pay into an account under a different name with no explanation. They have no reviews, no history, and no references. One of these signs alone might have an innocent explanation. Two or three together mean you should walk away, no matter how attractive the goods look.

If you have already been scammed, act quickly. Contact your bank immediately and report the transfer, giving them the account number you paid into, the amount, and the time. Banks can sometimes flag the receiving account, though recovery is not guaranteed. Keep every screenshot, receipt, and message as evidence. Report the matter to the police, and report the supplier's account on the platform where you found them — Instagram, Facebook, or WhatsApp — so others are warned. Tell other traders in your network what happened, with the supplier's name and number, so they do not fall for the same person. It is painful, and it is unfair, but the quickest response gives you the best chance of any recovery, and it protects the next person.

Prevention is still better than recovery. Build a small list of suppliers you have verified and worked with successfully, and buy from them first. When you need a new supplier, run through this checklist every time, even if a friend recommended them. A recommendation is a good start, but your money deserves your own check. Over time, your verified supplier list becomes one of the most valuable assets your business owns, because reliable supply is what lets you promise your own customers a delivery date and keep it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How can I check if a Nigerian supplier is registered with CAC?

You can search for the business name on the CAC public search portal to see whether it appears in the registry. Registration is a useful sign that the business is established, but it is not a guarantee of honesty. Combine the CAC check with the other steps in this guide — reputation, location, and a small test order — before you pay a large amount.

### What is the safest way to pay a new supplier?

For a first order, the safest options are paying on delivery, or paying a deposit with the balance due when the goods arrive or dispatch is confirmed. Always pay through a traceable method such as a bank transfer, keep the receipt, and make sure the account name matches the business or the person you are dealing with. Never pay the full amount in advance to a supplier you have never worked with.

### Should I trust a supplier with many followers on Instagram?

Not on follower count alone. Followers can be bought, and a large following says nothing about honesty. Look instead for a history of real posts, customer comments, replies from the supplier, and evidence of delivered orders. An account with a smaller following but genuine customer interaction is more trustworthy than a large account with no real engagement.

### What should I do if a supplier changes their account number before I pay?

Stop and verify by phone. Call the supplier on a number you already have — not a new number sent in the same message — and ask them to confirm the change. Criminals sometimes take over a supplier's WhatsApp account and send their own account details. If you cannot reach the supplier on a known number, do not send the money.

### Can I get my money back if a supplier scams me?

It is difficult, and recovery is not guaranteed. Contact your bank immediately to report the transfer, keep all your evidence, and report the matter to the police. Reporting quickly gives you the best chance, and it can help stop the same person from scamming others. The most reliable protection is prevention: verify before you pay, and start with a small order.

## What to Do Next

First, write down the verification checklist from this guide — identity, CAC search, online reputation, location check, small test order, and safe payment — and keep it on your phone. Use it every time you deal with a new supplier, without exception, even if they were recommended by a friend.

Second, take one supplier you already use and run the checks you have never done on them: search their business name, confirm their address, and check that the account you pay into matches their business name. If everything checks out, you have a verified supplier you can rely on. If something does not add up, you have learned something important before it cost you money.

Third, start your next new supplier relationship with a small test order, agreed in writing on WhatsApp, paid by bank transfer with the receipt saved. If the test goes well, increase your orders gradually. That slow, steady approach is how small businesses build a supply chain they can trust — one verified supplier at a time.
