Many Nigerian freelancers and small business owners have the same painful story. A client orders a website, a cake, a tailored outfit, or a set of graphics. You buy the materials, you spend days on the work, and then the client disappears, changes their mind, or says they will pay later — and later never comes. You are left with work nobody paid for, materials you cannot return, and time you cannot get back. A deposit prevents this. It is a payment made before work starts, and it is one of the simplest ways to protect a small business.

A deposit is not a sign that you do not trust your customer. It is a normal part of doing business, used by tailors, caterers, builders, photographers, web developers, event planners and many others across Nigeria. It shows that the customer is serious, it gives you money to buy materials, and it gives you something to fall back on if the job is cancelled. Yet many small business owners feel awkward asking for one. They worry the customer will think they are greedy, or will walk away to a competitor who asks for nothing upfront. This guide explains why a deposit protects both sides, how much to ask for, and how to ask in a way that customers accept without argument.

## Why a Deposit Protects You and the Customer

A deposit protects you in three ways. First, it covers your costs. Many jobs require you to spend money before you earn anything — fabric for a tailor, ingredients for a caterer, hosting and a domain for a web developer, or transport to a site for a photographer. Without a deposit, you pay for these out of your own pocket, and if the client cancels, you lose that money. With a deposit, the client's own money pays for the materials, and your risk is far smaller.

Second, a deposit shows commitment. A customer who has paid part of the price is a customer who will answer your calls, give you the information you need, and turn up for the appointment. A customer who has paid nothing has nothing to lose by disappearing, and in a busy market, disappearing is easy. A deposit turns a casual enquiry into a real booking, and it helps you plan your week honestly — you know which jobs are real and which ones are only talk.

Third, a deposit protects the customer too. When a customer pays a deposit, they have a clear agreement with you: the price, the work, and the date are written down, and both sides have a record. If something goes wrong, that record protects the customer as much as it protects you. A business that asks for a deposit and gives a receipt is a business that takes its work seriously. Customers understand this, and the serious ones respect it. The customers who refuse any deposit at all are often the ones most likely to waste your time.

## How Much Deposit Should You Ask For

There is no single correct deposit for every business, but there are clear guidelines. A common range for many Nigerian small businesses is between 30 and 50 percent of the total price, paid before work starts. For a job where you must buy expensive materials upfront, 50 percent is reasonable and often necessary. For a service where your main cost is time, such as writing, design, or consulting, 30 to 40 percent is a common starting point. The deposit should be large enough to cover your materials and to make cancellation costly for the customer, but not so large that it frightens a genuine customer away.

Think about your costs first. If a job costs you half the price in materials before you begin, a 30 percent deposit will not cover you — you would still be paying out of your own pocket. In that case, ask for 50 percent, or ask for the full cost of materials upfront plus a part of your labour fee. If the job has almost no upfront cost, a smaller deposit is fine, because your main risk is your time, not your money. Write down the costs of a typical job, and set your deposit to cover at least the money you must spend before the work is done.

Be consistent. Charge the same deposit percentage to every customer for the same kind of job. If you ask one customer for 50 percent and another for nothing, the customer who paid will feel cheated when they find out, and the customer who paid nothing has no reason to commit. Consistency also makes your business easier to run: you do not have to decide the deposit fresh for every order, and your staff can quote it without asking you. Put the deposit percentage in your price list and in your written agreement, so it is never a surprise.

## How to Ask for a Deposit Without Losing the Customer

The way you ask matters as much as the amount. The best time to mention the deposit is early, when you first quote the price, not after the customer has already agreed to everything. When you send a price, send the deposit in the same message. For example: "The total is ₦150,000, and we start work once a deposit of ₦75,000 is paid. The balance of ₦75,000 is due on delivery." The customer then decides with the full picture in front of them, and there is no awkward second conversation.

Use confident, professional language. Say, "We require a 50 percent deposit to begin," not, "Please, can you manage to pay something small first?" The first sounds like a business with a policy. The second sounds like a favour, and customers treat favours as optional. You are not begging — you are explaining how your business works. Every professional business in Nigeria, from hotels to event centres, asks for payment before service, and your customers already accept this in other parts of their lives.

If a customer hesitates, explain the reason plainly. "The deposit covers the materials I need to buy for your order, and it confirms your booking so I can reserve the date for you." That is honest, it is easy to understand, and it shows the deposit is about the work, not about you. If the customer still refuses, that tells you something useful about them. A serious customer who wants the work done will find a way to pay the deposit. A customer who refuses any deposit at all is often a customer who was never going to pay in full, and it is better to learn that before you start than after you have finished.

## Put the Deposit Agreement in Writing

A deposit without a written agreement is a misunderstanding waiting to happen. Before you collect the deposit, write down the agreement in a short message or document, and send it to the customer. It does not need to be a long legal contract. It needs to answer five questions: what work will be done, what the total price is, how much the deposit is, when the balance is due, and what happens if the customer cancels. A WhatsApp message with these five points, confirmed by the customer, is a real agreement that both sides can refer back to.

Be clear about cancellation. State plainly whether the deposit is refundable, and under what conditions. A common and fair rule is that the deposit is non-refundable once work has started or materials have been bought, because by then you have spent it on the customer's job. If the customer cancels before you have spent anything, a refund or a partial refund may be fair. Whatever your rule is, write it down before the deposit is paid, not after a cancellation. A customer who agreed to the rule in advance is far less likely to argue about it later.

Give a receipt every time. When the deposit arrives, send a short message confirming it: the amount, the date, the job it is for, and the balance remaining. This takes less than a minute, and it prevents the most common payment dispute in small business — "I already paid you" with no record on either side. Keep your own record too, in a notebook or a spreadsheet, with the customer's name, the job, the deposit, the balance, and the date. Good records protect you, help you plan your cash flow, and make your business look professional.

## Collect the Deposit Easily: Bank Transfer, Payment Link or Cash

A deposit policy only works if paying it is easy. Give your customer at least two ways to pay, and make the details simple to copy. Bank transfer is the most common option in Nigeria — send your account number, bank name, and account name as plain text, not as a screenshot, so the customer can copy and paste it into their bank app. Confirm the payment quickly when it arrives, and send the receipt in the same conversation.

A payment link is often even easier, because the customer taps a link and pays by card or transfer without leaving the chat. If you use Paystack, Flutterwave, or a similar payment company, create a link for the deposit amount and send it with your quote. The customer pays in one tap, and you both get a confirmation. For customers who prefer to pay in person, accept cash or a transfer at your shop or office, and give a written receipt on the spot. Never accept a deposit without a record of it.

Set a clear deadline for the deposit. A booking that waits forever for a deposit blocks your calendar and stops you from taking other work. Tell the customer plainly: "Your date is reserved for 48 hours. If the deposit is not paid by then, I will release the date to other customers." This is fair, it creates urgency, and it protects your time. When the deadline passes without payment, follow up once with a reminder, then release the date without guilt. A customer who is serious will pay within the window.

## What to Do When a Customer Cancels After Paying

Cancellations happen, even with a deposit. A customer may lose their job, change their plans, or have a family emergency. How you handle a cancellation decides whether that customer ever comes back. First, stay calm and stay kind. Ask what happened, and listen. Then apply the rule you wrote down before the job started. If work has begun or materials have been bought, explain plainly that the deposit covered those costs and cannot be refunded, but offer something in return — for example, credit towards a future order, or a smaller version of the product.

If you have not yet spent anything, consider refunding the deposit, or at least part of it. A refund in this situation costs you little and earns you a great deal of goodwill. The customer will remember that you treated them fairly in a difficult moment, and they are likely to return when their situation improves, and to recommend you to others. A business that keeps a deposit it has not earned will gain a small amount of money and lose a customer permanently.

Record every cancellation and what you did about it. Over time, you will see patterns — a particular type of job that cancels often, or a season when cancellations rise. You can then adjust your deposit or your booking rules to match. And if a customer cancels because of something you did — a delay, a misunderstanding, or a mistake in the quote — be honest about it, apologise, and refund the deposit in full. Taking responsibility when you are wrong is the mark of a professional, and it is what separates a business that lasts from one that does not.

## Deposits, Cash Flow and Growing Your Business

A deposit is more than protection against one bad customer. It is one of the best tools for managing the cash flow of a small business. Cash flow is the money coming in and going out each week, and many Nigerian small businesses fail not because they have no customers, but because the money arrives too late. You buy materials today, deliver the work next week, and the customer pays the week after that — meanwhile your rent, your transport, and your own feeding still need money. Deposits fix this timing problem by bringing part of the payment forward, to the moment when you need it most.

Use deposits to plan your work. At the start of each week, look at your confirmed bookings — the jobs with deposits paid — and you will know how much work you really have and how much money is already in hand. This is far more reliable than counting enquiries or promises. It also helps you decide when to take on more work and when to stop: if your calendar is full of deposit-paid jobs, you can confidently tell a new customer they must wait, rather than overpromising and disappointing everyone.

As your business grows, your deposit policy can grow with it. For large or long jobs, consider splitting the payment into three parts: a deposit to start, a middle payment when the work is half done, and the final balance on delivery. This keeps cash flowing through a long project and keeps the customer involved at each stage. Whatever structure you choose, keep it written down, keep it consistent, and keep giving receipts. A business that handles money clearly and professionally earns trust, and trust is what turns one-time customers into repeat customers.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What percentage deposit should I ask for?**

Between 30 and 50 percent of the total price is common for Nigerian small businesses. Use 50 percent when you must buy expensive materials upfront, and 30 to 40 percent when your main cost is time. Set the deposit to cover at least the money you must spend before the work is done.

**Is it rude to ask a customer for a deposit?**

No. Hotels, event centres, tailors, caterers and builders across Nigeria all ask for payment before service. A deposit is a normal business practice, and serious customers expect it. Ask confidently and early, when you first quote the price.

**Should a deposit be refundable if the customer cancels?**

It depends on how far the work has gone. If you have bought materials or started work, it is fair for the deposit to be non-refundable. If nothing has been spent yet, a refund or partial refund is fair. Write the rule down before the deposit is paid.

**What if a customer says they will pay everything after delivery?**

Explain politely that your policy is a deposit before work starts, because the deposit covers materials and confirms the booking. If the customer refuses any deposit, it is usually better to let the job go than to risk your time and money on a customer who may not pay.

**How do I record deposits properly?**

Keep a simple log with the customer's name, the job, the total price, the deposit paid, the date, and the balance remaining. Send the customer a written receipt for every deposit, and confirm the payment as soon as it arrives.
