Fuel scarcity in Nigeria does not only affect drivers. It affects every small business that moves goods, runs a generator, delivers orders, or depends on customers travelling to a shop. When queues grow long at filling stations in Lagos, Abuja, or Port Harcourt, prices rise, deliveries slow down, and many shops simply close because they cannot get fuel or cannot afford it.

The businesses that keep selling during scarcity are not the ones with the biggest fuel tanks. They are the ones that planned ahead, use fuel only where it earns money, and find other ways to reach customers. They take orders on WhatsApp before they move, they use dispatch riders instead of driving everywhere themselves, they keep their best-selling stock close, and they talk honestly to customers about delays and prices.

This guide is for Nigerian small business owners who cannot afford to stop selling every time fuel becomes scarce. Whether you sell foodstuff in Mile 12, clothes in Balogun Market, building materials in Onitsha, phone accessories in Computer Village, or cooked food in Yaba, the same ideas apply. Protect your fuel, protect your deliveries, and protect your customers.

## Plan Before Scarcity Hits

Fuel scarcity rarely arrives without warning. Prices rise, stations close early, queues get longer, and suppliers start warning about delays. The owners who cope best notice those signs early and act before their tank is empty.

Keep a small reserve at all times. Do not wait until your generator or your vehicle is empty before you look for fuel. When fuel is available at a normal price, keep your tank at least half full and keep a safe reserve in an approved container for your generator. Store fuel safely, away from heat, away from your shop, and away from children. Never store fuel inside a shop or near a cooking area.

Know how much fuel your business really uses in a week. Write it down for two normal weeks: how many litres for the generator, how many for your vehicle or bike, and how many for deliveries. That number is your baseline. During scarcity, your job is to cut that number without cutting your sales by the same amount. You cannot cut what you have not measured.

Make a list of your most important fuel uses. Deliveries to paying customers come first. Running a freezer that protects stock comes second. Driving around to look for stock or to visit customers who have not ordered yet comes last. When fuel is scarce, every trip must have a clear purpose and a clear payment attached to it.

Talk to your suppliers early. Ask them when they expect stock, whether prices will change, and whether they can deliver to you instead of you travelling to them. A supplier in Oyingbo Market who delivers to your shop in Surulere saves you a trip, a queue, and fuel. Build that relationship before scarcity, not during it.

## Keep Fuel for Deliveries, Not Everything

During scarcity, fuel is money. Spending it on a trip that does not bring in money is waste. Many owners lose money during scarcity because they keep moving the way they moved when fuel was cheap and available.

Stop driving to the market for small top-ups. Instead, buy in larger quantities when you go, or ask your supplier to deliver. If you sell provisions in Lekki, one well-planned trip to Mile 12 or Oyingbo that buys stock for a week is better than four small trips in the same week. Each trip costs fuel, time, and transport money.

Combine trips. If you must go out, do several jobs in one trip: collect stock, deliver orders on the same route, pick up packaging, and visit the bank. Plan the route before you leave. A rider or driver who moves in a straight line and finishes all jobs in one outing uses far less fuel than one who crosses Lagos three times in a day.

Use your generator only when it earns money. A generator that runs all day to power a television and an air conditioner during scarcity is burning profit. Run it in blocks to cool freezers, to serve customers during busy hours, and to charge phones and point-of-sale terminals. Switch off everything that is not essential. If the shop is quiet, it may be better to close for an hour and save fuel for the busy evening period.

Mrs. Ngozi sells frozen food in Oshodi. During the last scarcity, she stopped driving to Mile 12 every morning. She now orders by phone, her supplier delivers twice a week, and she uses her small generator only for three hours in the morning and three in the evening. Her fuel use fell by almost half, but her sales stayed steady because her customers could still buy what they needed.

## Ration and Record Every Litre

You cannot manage fuel if you do not track it. During scarcity, keep a simple fuel book. Write the date, how many litres you bought, the price per litre, where you bought it, and what it was used for. That record shows you quickly where fuel is going and where you can save.

Set a daily fuel limit for your business. For example, a small shop might set a limit of ten litres a day for the generator and five litres for deliveries. When the limit is reached, stop non-essential use. That discipline forces you to choose the most valuable work first. It also stops the common problem of running out of fuel on the busiest day because it was wasted on quiet days.

Do not buy fuel from roadside sellers at any price without thinking. Black market fuel is often expensive, sometimes mixed with water or other substances, and it can damage your generator or vehicle. If you must buy from an alternative source during a real emergency, buy only a small quantity, test it, and keep the receipt or the seller's details if you can. A damaged generator costs far more than the fuel you saved.

Keep your generator and vehicle serviced. A generator with a dirty air filter or old oil uses more fuel and breaks down more often. A vehicle with low tyre pressure uses more fuel. These small checks cost little and save fuel every day. Ask your mechanic to check your vehicle before scarcity gets worse, not after it breaks down in a queue.

## Use Dispatch Riders Instead of Driving Everywhere

Dispatch riders are one of the best tools for a small business during fuel scarcity. A rider on a bike uses far less fuel than a car, can move through traffic faster, and can deliver several orders on one route. You do not need to own a bike to use riders. You can pay per delivery.

Find two or three reliable riders before you need them urgently. Test them with small orders first. Check that they arrive on time, handle goods carefully, and collect payment properly. Agree on prices for common routes in advance: for example, a fixed price for deliveries within Lekki, a fixed price for deliveries to Ikeja, and a fixed price for deliveries to the Island. When prices are agreed in advance, you can quote delivery costs to customers without delay.

Use riders for deliveries, not for errands that can wait. If a customer has paid, that delivery is a priority. If you are sending a sample to a customer who has not ordered, that can wait until the rider has another delivery on the same route. Batch your deliveries. Instead of sending a rider out five times a day for one parcel each, collect orders until midday, then send one rider with several parcels on a planned route.

Give riders clear instructions. Write the customer's name, phone number, address, and amount to collect on a small card or in a WhatsApp message. Ask the rider to call the customer before they leave and when they arrive. Ask the rider to confirm payment by transfer before they leave the customer. A clear system prevents lost parcels, wrong addresses, and unpaid deliveries.

Tunde sells phone accessories in Computer Village, Ikeja. He used to drive his car to deliver orders across Lagos. During scarcity, he switched to two riders he trusts. He now stays in his shop, serves walk-in customers, and sends riders out twice a day with batched orders. His delivery costs are lower, his shop is never closed because he is stuck in traffic, and his customers get their orders faster.

## Take Orders on WhatsApp Before You Move

The most wasteful trip during fuel scarcity is a trip to a customer who is not ready, not at home, or not sure what they want. WhatsApp can prevent most of those trips if you use it properly.

Take full orders before you deliver. Ask the customer to confirm the item, the quantity, the size or colour, the address, and the delivery time. Send a photo of the exact item and the price. Ask for a deposit or full payment by transfer or Paystack or Flutterwave before you dispatch, especially for new customers. A customer who has paid is far more likely to be available when the rider arrives.

Use your WhatsApp status and broadcast list to tell customers what is available. If you have limited stock because you could not travel to the market, say so. Post what you have, the price, and the delivery areas you can cover that day. Customers who know what is available will order quickly. Customers who have to ask about everything will waste your time and your fuel.

Set clear delivery days and times. Instead of promising delivery at any time, tell customers you deliver on Mondays, Wednesdays, and Fridays, or that you deliver between 11 a.m. and 4 p.m. That lets you batch orders, plan routes, and use riders efficiently. Most customers accept a clear delivery window if you tell them in advance.

Amina sells fabrics in Balogun Market. She used to travel to deliver fabrics to customers in Lekki and Ikeja herself. Now she takes orders on WhatsApp, collects payment by transfer, and sends a rider twice a week. Her customers know her delivery days, and she no longer spends hours in traffic or queues for fuel.

## Adjust Prices Honestly and Protect Your Cash Flow

Fuel scarcity raises your costs. Transport costs rise, generator fuel costs rise, and suppliers raise their prices. You cannot absorb all of those costs and stay in business. You must adjust your prices, but you must do it honestly and clearly.

Tell customers before they pay. If a delivery charge has gone up from ₦1,500 to ₦2,500 because of fuel, say so when they order, not when the rider arrives. If the price of an item has gone up because your supplier raised prices, explain it briefly. Customers accept honest price changes far better than surprise charges.

Do not raise prices on old stock just because fuel is scarce. If you bought stock at the old price, keep the old price if you can, or raise it only a little to cover your extra delivery costs. Customers remember who treated them fairly during scarcity. They also remember who used scarcity as an excuse to charge far more than the extra cost justified.

Protect your cash flow. During scarcity, avoid buying large quantities of slow-moving stock. Buy what sells fast and what customers have already ordered. Collect payment before you deliver to new customers. Follow up on debts politely but firmly. If a customer owes you, remind them on WhatsApp and agree on a payment date. Cash in your hand is more useful during scarcity than stock sitting on a shelf.

Keep some cash aside for fuel. Do not spend all your sales money on stock and then have nothing left to buy fuel for the generator or for deliveries. Set aside a small fuel fund each day, even if it is only ₦5,000. That fund keeps your business moving when fuel is hard to find.

## Build Supplier and Neighbour Backup Plans

No small business survives fuel scarcity alone. You need suppliers who will deliver, neighbours who will share information, and customers who will wait a little longer because they trust you.

Keep more than one supplier for your most important stock. If your main supplier in Mile 12 cannot deliver because of fuel, a second supplier in Oyingbo or in your local market may still be able to help. Do not wait until scarcity to find a second supplier. Ask fellow traders who they use, test the second supplier with a small order during normal times, and keep their phone number saved.

Keep a list of nearby traders who sell what you need. If you run out of packaging, gas, or a small item, a neighbour may have it. Offer to help them in return. In many Lagos markets, traders lend each other small quantities of stock or fuel during scarcity and settle later. That trust is built over time, not in a crisis.

Join or start a small buying group. Several traders who buy the same stock can combine their orders and share one delivery, instead of each travelling separately. That saves fuel for everyone. A buying group also gives you more power to negotiate a better price with a supplier who delivers in bulk.

Tell your customers your plan. If deliveries will be slower, say so. If you can only deliver on certain days, say so. If your shop will open later because you are waiting for fuel, update your WhatsApp status. Customers who know what to expect will plan around you. Customers who are kept in the dark will go to a competitor.

## What to Do Next

1. Measure your weekly fuel use for your generator, vehicle, and deliveries, and write down your baseline.
2. Keep a small safe fuel reserve and a daily fuel limit, and record every litre you buy and use.
3. Find two reliable dispatch riders, agree on fixed prices for your common routes, and test them with small orders.
4. Take full orders and payment on WhatsApp before you dispatch, and batch deliveries into one or two trips a day.
5. Combine your trips, buy stock in larger quantities, and ask suppliers to deliver instead of travelling yourself.
6. Adjust your prices honestly, tell customers before they pay, and set aside a small fuel fund each day.
7. Keep a second supplier, share fuel and delivery information with fellow traders, and tell customers your delivery days and times.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How can I deliver orders when fuel is scarce and expensive?**
Batch your orders and use dispatch riders on planned routes instead of driving yourself. Take full orders and payment on WhatsApp before you dispatch, and deliver on set days so one rider can carry several parcels. That cuts fuel use and keeps your shop open while deliveries continue.

**Should I increase my prices during fuel scarcity?**
You can adjust prices to cover real extra costs, such as higher transport and generator fuel, but tell customers before they pay and keep the increase fair. Do not raise prices on old stock by a large amount. Customers accept honest changes and remember businesses that treated them fairly.

**Is it safe to buy fuel from roadside sellers?**
It is risky. Roadside fuel is often expensive and can be mixed with other substances that damage your generator or vehicle. Buy from a filling station when you can. If you must buy elsewhere in an emergency, buy only a small quantity and test it first.

**How do I keep my generator running during scarcity?**
Use it only for essential work, run it in blocks, and switch off non-essential items. Keep a small reserve, record every litre, and service the generator regularly so it uses less fuel. If fuel costs are higher than the profit from staying open, close for a short period and reopen when it makes sense.

**What if my supplier cannot deliver because of fuel?**
Keep a second supplier for your most important stock and keep their number saved. Ask fellow traders who they use, and consider combining orders with other traders so one delivery serves several shops. Tell your customers early if stock will be delayed.

**How can WhatsApp help during fuel scarcity?**
WhatsApp lets you take full orders, confirm payment, share what is in stock, and tell customers your delivery days before you move. That prevents wasted trips to customers who are not ready and lets you batch deliveries efficiently.
