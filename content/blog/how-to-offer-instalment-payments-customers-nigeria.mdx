"I will pay you small small." Every Nigerian seller has heard it. A customer wants a ₦250,000 phone, a ₦400,000 freezer or a ₦180,000 sofa, but cannot pay at once. The seller faces a choice: refuse and lose the sale, or accept instalments and risk never seeing the balance.

Done badly, instalments destroy small businesses. Goods leave the shop, money trickles in late or not at all, and the seller ends up chasing customers who have stopped picking calls. Done well, instalments are one of the most powerful sales tools in Nigeria. They open your products to salary earners, traders and families who can afford ₦50,000 a month but not ₦250,000 today.

This guide shows you how to offer instalment payments safely: which products qualify, how much deposit to collect, how to structure the plan, what to put in writing, and what to do when a customer defaults.

## Why Instalments Work in Nigeria

Most Nigerians do not lack money — they lack lump sums. A teacher in Ikeja earns a monthly salary. A trader in Onitsha earns daily but unevenly. A nurse in Port Harcourt can budget ₦40,000 a month for a new phone but cannot drop ₦200,000 at once without emptying her savings.

Instalments match how Nigerians actually earn and spend. That is why "pay small small" has always existed in our markets — from ajo and esusu contribution circles to the furniture seller in Mushin who lets a customer pay over three months. Formalising that tradition with clear rules turns a risky favour into a reliable sales channel.

The businesses that benefit most are those selling items between ₦50,000 and ₦1,000,000: phones, laptops, home appliances, furniture, generators, solar systems, fashion in bulk, and even event services like wedding packages. Below ₦50,000, the admin cost of chasing payments often eats the profit. Above ₦1,000,000, you need stronger security than a simple agreement.

## Decide Which Products Qualify

Not every product should be sold on instalment. Set clear rules before your first instalment customer:

- **High-value, durable items qualify.** Phones, laptops, freezers, televisions, sofas, generators, solar inverters, sewing machines. These hold value, and customers need them badly enough to keep paying.
- **Fast-moving consumables do not.** Do not sell foodstuffs, skincare or recharge cards on instalment. They are consumed before the payments finish, and the customer has no reason to keep paying.
- **Set a minimum order value.** Many sellers set the minimum at ₦50,000 or ₦100,000. Below that, full payment only.
- **Limit the product range.** Start with your five bestselling high-value items. Learn the system on those before extending it to everything you sell.
- **Keep some items full-payment only.** New arrivals, limited stock and low-margin items should be cash-and-carry. You choose what goes on instalment — the customer does not.

Write the list down and display it in your shop or pin it on your WhatsApp status. Clear rules prevent arguments and protect you from pressure in the moment.

## The Deposit: Your Most Important Protection

The deposit is what separates a serious buyer from a hopeful one. Never release goods on instalment without a meaningful deposit.

**Collect 30 to 50 percent upfront.** For a ₦200,000 phone, that is ₦60,000 to ₦100,000. For a ₦500,000 sofa set, ₦150,000 to ₦250,000. A customer who cannot raise 30 percent today is unlikely to complete the payments later — the deposit is itself a test of commitment and capacity.

**Hold the goods until the deposit clears.** Do not hand over the product on a promise. The deposit must be in your account and confirmed before the item leaves your shop. For large items like furniture, some sellers deliver after 50 percent and collect the rest on a schedule. For phones and electronics, many sellers keep the item until 70 percent is paid, or release it after the deposit plus a guarantor.

**Decide what happens to the deposit on default.** State it clearly in the agreement: if the customer abandons the plan, the deposit covers your costs of holding the stock, and the goods are returned or resold. Some sellers refund the deposit minus a restocking fee; others treat it as non-refundable after a stated period. Either way, agree it in writing before money changes hands.

## Structure the Payment Plan

A good plan is short, regular and easy to follow. Keep it to three rules:

**Keep the duration short.** Two to three months is the sweet spot for most products. Four to six months works for bigger items like solar systems or furniture sets. Beyond six months, the risk of default, price changes and forgotten payments rises sharply. A plan that drags on for a year is a loan, not a sale — and you are not a bank.

**Fix the payment dates.** Weekly or monthly, on a specific day. "₦50,000 on the 5th of every month" is clear. "Pay when you have money" is a recipe for chasing. Align dates with salary cycles where possible — many salary earners are paid at month end, so the 1st to 5th of the month is a realistic collection window.

**Add a small instalment fee, transparently.** Instalments cost you money: your cash is tied up, you chase payments, and prices may rise. A clearly stated fee of 5 to 10 percent over the cash price is fair and normal. For example, a phone that costs ₦200,000 cash might cost ₦220,000 on a three-month plan: ₦70,000 deposit plus ₦50,000 monthly for three months. Tell the customer the total before they commit. Hidden charges destroy trust.

**Show the customer the full schedule.** Write it out: deposit, each instalment amount, each due date, and the total. Send it on WhatsApp and keep a copy yourself. A customer who can see the finish line is more likely to reach it.

### Example: A ₦300,000 Laptop on a 3-Month Plan

- Cash price: ₦300,000
- Instalment total (with 10 percent fee): ₦330,000
- Deposit (40 percent of instalment total): ₦132,000
- Balance: ₦198,000
- Monthly payment: ₦66,000 on the 5th of each month for 3 months

The customer knows exactly what they owe and when. You know exactly what to expect and when to follow up.

## Put It in Writing: The Instalment Agreement

A verbal agreement is worth very little when a dispute arises. Every instalment sale needs a simple written agreement — it does not need a lawyer to be useful.

Your agreement should record:

- Full name, phone number and address of the customer
- Product name, description, serial number or IMEI where applicable
- Total price, deposit paid, balance, and the payment schedule with dates
- What happens if a payment is late — for example, a grace period of 3 to 5 days, then a late fee of ₦2,000 or 2 percent
- What happens on default — after how many missed payments the goods are recovered or the plan is cancelled
- The customer's signature and date, plus your signature

Send the customer a photo or PDF of the signed agreement on WhatsApp immediately. Keep the original. For higher-value items, take a photo of the customer holding the product and the agreement — it discourages disputes about whether they received it.

You can create the agreement template once in Google Docs or Microsoft Word and reuse it for every sale, changing only the names, amounts and dates. That takes ten minutes to set up and protects every sale after it.

## Verify the Customer Before You Commit

You are effectively lending money, so verify like a lender — quickly and politely:

- **Confirm identity.** Ask for a valid ID: National Identity Card, driver's licence, international passport or voter's card. Photograph it with the customer's permission and keep it with the agreement.
- **Confirm where they live and work.** A home address and a workplace or business address. You may never need them, but knowing them changes behaviour — customers who know you can find them pay more reliably.
- **Ask for a guarantor for larger amounts.** For items above ₦300,000, ask for a guarantor — a family member, employer or respected person who confirms they know the customer and will help if payments stop. Record the guarantor's name and phone number on the agreement. Many defaults never happen simply because a guarantor exists.
- **Check their history with you.** A customer who has bought from you three times and paid promptly is a far safer instalment candidate than a stranger who walked in today. Offer instalments first to your proven customers.
- **Trust your judgement.** If something feels off — evasive answers, no fixed address, pressure to skip the paperwork — decline politely. "I am sorry, this item is not available on instalment right now" is a complete sentence.

## Collect Payments Without Stress

Collection is where most instalment plans fail. Build the system before the first sale:

- **Send reminders before the due date.** A friendly message two days before: "Good morning, Mrs Okafor. A reminder that your ₦66,000 instalment for the laptop is due on the 5th. Thank you." Reminders prevent most late payments.
- **Offer easy payment channels.** Bank transfer, Moniepoint, OPay or PalmPay, or a Paystack payment link. The easier it is to pay, the faster they pay. Share your account details in the reminder message so the customer does not have to ask.
- **Record every payment immediately.** Date, amount, method and balance remaining. Send the customer a receipt on WhatsApp after each payment, showing the new balance. This prevents the most common dispute in instalment selling: "I have paid more than that."
- **Follow up on missed payments the same day.** A missed payment ignored for two weeks becomes a habit. Call the same day, politely: "Good afternoon sir, I noticed the payment due yesterday has not come in. Is everything okay?" Often there is a genuine problem, and a short conversation finds a solution — a split payment, a new date, a smaller amount.
- **Keep a simple tracker.** A Google Sheet with one row per instalment customer — name, product, total, paid so far, balance, next due date — lets you see at a glance who is current and who needs a call. Review it every Monday morning.

## What to Do When a Customer Defaults

Even with the best system, some customers will default. What you do next decides whether you recover your money or lose it quietly.

1. **Stay calm and professional.** Anger closes doors. A calm, firm approach keeps the relationship and the money recoverable.
2. **Contact the customer directly.** Call, do not only chat. Ask what happened. Job loss, illness or a family emergency may be real — and a restructured plan (smaller payments over a longer period) often recovers more than a fight.
3. **Contact the guarantor.** If the customer stops responding, call the guarantor and explain the situation factually. Guarantors often resolve the matter quickly.
4. **Offer a settlement.** If the customer genuinely cannot complete the plan, offer to take the product back in good condition and refund part of what was paid, minus a fair usage fee. Recovering 60 percent is better than recovering nothing.
5. **Recover the goods if the agreement allows.** For items like phones or electronics, your agreement should state that ownership remains with you until full payment. If the customer refuses to pay or return the item, this clause is your basis for recovery.
6. **Know when to escalate.** If the amount is significant and the customer has disappeared, report to the police with your agreement, ID copy, payment records and chats. For larger amounts, a lawyer's letter often produces payment faster than months of calls. Keep records of every attempt to resolve the matter — they are your evidence.
7. **Learn and adjust.** After a default, review what went wrong. Was the deposit too low? Was the customer unverified? Did you skip the guarantor? Fix the gap in your process so the next customer does not repeat it.

Never shame a customer publicly — no social media call-outs, no group messages. It feels satisfying for a day and damages your reputation for years. Handle defaults privately and professionally.

## Partner With Buy-Now-Pay-Later Platforms

You do not have to carry all the risk yourself. Several Nigerian platforms offer buy-now-pay-later services that let the customer pay in instalments while you get paid upfront by the platform. Examples include CredPal, Carbon Zero, and similar services that partner with merchants.

How it typically works: the customer applies through the platform, the platform checks their eligibility, you deliver the goods, and the platform pays you (minus a fee) while the customer repays the platform. The default risk moves from you to the platform.

The trade-off is the fee and the approval process — not every customer will qualify, and the platform takes a cut. But for high-value items, giving up a small percentage to remove default risk entirely is often a good deal. Compare two or three platforms, check their merchant fees and which products they cover, and offer it alongside your own in-house plan. Let the customer choose.

## Mistakes That Cost Sellers Money

- **No deposit, goods released.** The most expensive mistake in instalment selling. Without a deposit, you have no commitment and no cushion.
- **No written agreement.** When the dispute comes, it is your word against theirs — and your word without paper rarely wins.
- **Plans that are too long.** Twelve-month plans for a phone that will be outdated in six months. Keep plans short.
- **Offering instalments to everyone.** Instalments are a privilege for verified, committed customers — not a right for every walk-in.
- **Forgetting to follow up.** A plan without reminders is a plan that will be forgotten. Reminders are part of the product.
- **Selling on instalment what you bought on credit.** If you owe your supplier, do not sell their goods on a three-month plan. You will be squeezed from both sides.
- **No limit on total exposure.** Decide the maximum total balance you will carry at any time — for example, ₦500,000 across all instalment customers. When you hit the limit, stop offering new plans until balances come down.

## Start Small and Scale Carefully

If you have never offered instalments, do not launch it for your whole shop on Monday. Start like this:

- **Week 1:** Choose three qualifying products, set your deposit and fee rules, and write your agreement template.
- **Week 2:** Offer instalments to five of your best existing customers only — people with a payment history you trust.
- **Week 3:** Run those five plans, send reminders, record payments, and note what works and what feels awkward.
- **Week 4:** Review the results. If all five are paying on time, open instalments to new customers with the same rules. If two are already late, tighten the deposit or the verification before scaling.

Grow the programme slowly. A healthy instalment book is one where 90 percent of customers pay on time, your total outstanding balance stays within your limit, and the extra sales clearly exceed the extra admin time.

## Final Word

Instalment payments are not charity and they are not a trap — they are a tool. Used with a proper deposit, a short plan, a written agreement and consistent follow-up, they bring in customers who could never pay at once and turn them into loyal buyers who return for their next item.

The sellers who lose money on instalments are not unlucky. They skipped the deposit, skipped the paperwork, or skipped the follow-up. Do those three things properly, and "pay small small" becomes one of the most profitable sentences in your business.
