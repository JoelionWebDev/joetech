Many small businesses in Nigeria sell every day but cannot say how much they made last week. The shop was busy, customers paid by cash and transfer, yet at the end of the month there is no clear figure. Money comes in and goes out, stock runs out without warning, and decisions are made by feeling instead of facts. That is not a lack of hard work. It is a lack of records.

Tracking daily sales does not require an accountant or expensive software. It requires a simple system you can follow every day, even on busy days, and the discipline to write things down before you forget them. Whether you sell food in Surulere, clothes in Lekki, phone accessories in Computer Village, or hair products in Port Harcourt, the same principles apply. Record what you sold, how much you received, how you were paid, and what you spent. Do it daily, review it weekly, and use it monthly to make better decisions.

This guide walks you through the process in plain language. You will learn what to record, which method fits your business, how to handle cash, transfers and POS payments, how to track expenses, and how to turn daily figures into useful weekly and monthly summaries. By the end, you will have a system you can start tomorrow morning.


## Why Daily Sales Records Matter

Sales records are the memory of your business. Without them, you rely on guesswork. With them, you can see patterns that are invisible when you only look at your bank balance. A bank balance mixes business money with personal spending, supplier payments, and money still owed to you. It does not tell you which product is carrying your business and which one is quietly losing money.

Consider a trader in Onitsha who sells shoes and bags. On a good day she might collect ₦180,000. That sounds like profit, but if she spent ₦120,000 restocking, ₦15,000 on transport, and ₦10,000 on shop expenses, her real gain is far smaller. Without daily records, she might spend the ₦180,000 as if it were all profit, then struggle to restock the following week. Records prevent that mistake by separating sales from profit.

Records also help you spot theft, mistakes, and missing money. If you record every sale and compare the total with the cash in your drawer and the transfers in your bank app at the end of the day, differences stand out quickly. A missing ₦5,000 is easy to ignore once. A missing ₦5,000 every day for a month is ₦150,000. Daily tracking catches small leaks before they become large losses.

Good records help you plan. When you know December is your busiest month, Saturdays bring twice the sales of Mondays, or a product sells out every two weeks, you can order stock at the right time and avoid borrowing money in a panic. If you ever apply for a loan or talk to an investor, lenders will ask for records. A neat, consistent sales book is far more convincing than a confident story. Records also protect you during disputes. If a customer says they paid ₦25,000 and you recorded ₦20,000, your record, receipt, and bank alert together settle the matter calmly.

## What to Record Every Day

A daily sales record does not need to be complicated, but it needs to be complete enough to be useful. For each sale, record the date, the item or service sold, the quantity, the unit price, the total amount, and the payment method. If you sell the same item many times a day, group them. Instead of thirty separate lines for bottled water, write one line: 30 bottles at ₦500 each, total ₦15,000, with a note breaking down cash and transfer.

Also record who bought, when it matters. For regular customers, repeat buyers, or credit sales, write the name and phone number. Credit sales are the most dangerous sales to forget. If you give goods on credit without writing the customer name, amount, and date, you will chase money you cannot prove. Keep a separate page or column for credit, and mark it paid only when the money actually arrives.

Record discounts and returns separately. If you sold an item for ₦10,000 but gave a ₦1,000 discount, write both figures so your sales total reflects what you actually received. If a customer returned a faulty item and you refunded ₦8,000, record the refund on the same day. Otherwise your sales will look higher than the money you kept.

Do not forget expenses. Every naira spent on the business that day should be written down: stock purchases, transport, airtime, data, packaging, fuel for the generator, staff wages, market levies, and bank charges. Even small amounts count. A ₦500 delivery fee here and a ₦1,000 nylon bag purchase there add up over a month. At the end of the day, you should be able to calculate total sales, total expenses, and the balance left. A simple daily summary at the bottom of each page makes review easy. Write total cash sales, total transfer sales, total POS sales, total sales, total expenses, and cash expected in the drawer.

## Choose a Method That Fits Your Business

There is no single best method for every business. The best method is the one you will actually use every day. Three options work well for most Nigerian small businesses: a paper notebook, a spreadsheet on your phone or laptop, and a simple accounting or POS app. Each has strengths and limits.

A paper notebook is the simplest place to start. Buy a hardcover ledger, rule columns for date, item, quantity, price, total, and payment method, and keep it at your counter. It needs no electricity, no data, and no login. It works in a market stall in Mile 12, a kiosk in Enugu, and a shop in Kano. The risk is damage, loss, and handwriting that only you can read. Keep the book in a safe place, use a pen, and take a photo of each completed page at the end of the week as a backup.

A spreadsheet is a step up. Google Sheets is free and works on a phone. You can create columns for the same details, add formulas that total your sales automatically, and sort by date or product. If you use Google Sheets, your data is saved online and you can check it from another phone if yours is lost. Excel works offline and is familiar to many traders, but the file can be lost if the phone or laptop is damaged. If you choose a spreadsheet, keep the layout simple and do not change the columns every week. Consistency matters more than clever formulas.

A POS or accounting app can save time if you already use one. Many Nigerian businesses use apps that record sales automatically when a payment is made. Some let you add products, prices, and stock levels, then produce a daily sales report at the touch of a button. This is useful if you have many products or staff. The drawback is that cash sales still need to be entered manually, and some apps charge fees or need regular data. Do not buy an app because it looks impressive. Try the free version first and check that it exports your data. You can also combine methods. Many traders use a notebook during the day for speed, then transfer the totals into a spreadsheet in the evening.

## Build a Daily Routine You Can Keep

The secret to good records is routine, not motivation. Motivation fades on a rainy Monday or a slow afternoon. Routine carries you through. Build a short routine at the start, middle, and end of each business day.

In the morning, count your opening cash. Write down how much cash is in the drawer before the first sale. This is your float. If you start with ₦20,000 in change and end with ₦85,000, you know ₦65,000 came in as cash, before expenses. Without an opening figure, you cannot reconcile the day properly. Check that your book, pen, and receipt book are ready. If you use a phone app, make sure it is charged and open.

During the day, record each sale as it happens, or at least in small batches. If you are very busy, record every hour or after every ten sales. Do not wait until closing time to remember the whole day. Memory is unreliable, especially when you have served dozens of customers. If you use a notebook, keep it open on the counter. If you use a phone, keep the sheet open. Make recording part of serving the customer, not an extra task after they leave.

At the end of the day, close the book properly. Add up sales by payment method, add up expenses, and work out the cash you should have. Count the actual cash and compare it with the expected figure. If there is a difference, write it down and try to find the cause while the day is still fresh. Small differences often come from giving wrong change or forgetting a small cash expense. Write a short note, then move on. Do not leave the day unclosed because you are tired. An unclosed day becomes a missing day, and missing days become a missing month. Keep the routine short. A good closing routine takes ten to fifteen minutes. If it takes an hour, your system is too complicated. Simplify it.

## How to Record Cash, Transfers, and POS Payments

Nigerian customers pay in different ways, and each way needs a different check. Cash is immediate but easy to lose track of. Transfers are traceable but can be delayed or faked. POS payments are convenient but come with charges and occasional failed transactions. Your records should show the split clearly.

For cash, record the amount received and keep the cash separate from personal money. Do not pay for personal lunch from the business drawer without recording it as a drawing. If you take ₦5,000 for transport home, write it as an owner withdrawal. At the end of the day, count the cash and compare it with your recorded cash sales plus opening float minus cash expenses. If the figures match, you have a clean day.

For transfers, record the customer name, the bank, the amount, and the time. Ask the customer to show you the debit alert or the successful transfer screen before they leave, especially for large amounts. Check your own bank app or SMS alert before handing over goods when the amount is high. Some fraudsters show fake alerts. A quick check of your balance protects you. In your record, write "transfer confirmed" once you have seen it in your account. If a transfer is pending, note it as pending and follow up the next day.

For POS, record the amount and the approval code or receipt number. Keep the POS receipt with your daily records, or staple it to the page. Note the charge if your provider deducts it immediately. If a transaction fails but the customer is debited, do not charge them twice. Record the failed attempt, give them the goods only after the reversal or a confirmed second payment, and keep the receipt as evidence. At the end of the day, your total sales should equal cash plus transfers plus POS. If you also sold on credit, keep that separate until the money is paid. This split helps you see how your customers prefer to pay.

## Track Expenses Every Day, Not Just Sales

Many small businesses track sales but ignore expenses, then wonder where the profit went. Sales are only half the story. Profit is what remains after expenses. If you do not record expenses daily, your profit figure is a guess, and your pricing may be wrong.

Start by listing your regular expenses. Stock or raw materials are usually the largest cost. Then add transport, delivery, packaging, rent, electricity, generator fuel, data, airtime, staff wages, cleaning, market levies, bank charges, and POS charges. Some costs are daily, some weekly, and some monthly. Record each one on the day you pay it. For monthly costs such as rent, write the full amount on the day you pay, and note that it covers the month.

Keep receipts for larger expenses. A receipt from a supplier, a fuel station, or a wholesale market is useful if there is a dispute or if you need to compare prices later. You do not need to keep every small receipt forever, but keep them for at least a month, or photograph them and store them in a folder on your phone. If you buy stock in a market where receipts are not given, write the supplier name, items, and amount in your book immediately.

Separate business expenses from personal expenses. This is one of the hardest habits for a small business owner in Nigeria, because the business and the family often share the same pocket. If you use business money to buy food for the house, pay school fees, or send money to a relative, record it as an owner drawing, not as a business expense. That way, your business profit is not hidden by personal spending, and you can see how much you are actually taking from the business each month. A simple daily profit calculation helps. Total sales minus total expenses equals daily profit or loss. On some days, especially when you restock, the figure will be negative. That is normal. The weekly and monthly totals matter more than a single day.

## Common Mistakes That Ruin Sales Records

The first mistake is recording from memory at the end of the week. By then, you have forgotten small sales, small expenses, and the exact amounts. Record daily, even if it is only the totals. A rough daily record is better than a perfect weekly guess.

The second mistake is mixing personal and business money. When the same account receives business transfers and personal money, and the same cash pays for stock and school fees, your records will never balance. Open a separate business account if you can, or at least keep a separate wallet or envelope for business cash.

The third mistake is not recording credit sales. Many traders give goods to trusted customers and write nothing down. When the customer delays payment, the trader cannot remember the exact amount or date. Always write credit sales immediately, with the customer name, phone number, amount, and due date. Take a photo of the entry and send it to the customer on WhatsApp as a friendly confirmation.

The fourth mistake is having no opening and closing cash count. Without those counts, you cannot tell if money is missing. Count cash at the start and end of each day, even if it feels repetitive. The fifth mistake is using a system that is too complicated. A trader who tries to use a full accounting package with twenty columns will abandon it in a week. Start simple. A notebook with six columns, used every day, is better than a complex spreadsheet used once a month. The sixth mistake is never reviewing the records. Recording without reviewing is like cooking without tasting. Schedule the review and protect that time.

## Start Tomorrow Morning

Your sales book is a business asset. Keep it in a dry, safe place, photograph completed pages weekly, and back up any spreadsheet online. If you have staff, train them kindly, explain why records matter, and check the closing cash count yourself. Review your system every three months and change one thing at a time if it needs improving.
