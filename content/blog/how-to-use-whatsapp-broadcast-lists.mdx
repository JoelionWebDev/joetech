Your broadcast list is set up. Two hundred customers saved your number. You can reach all of them with one message. And now you are staring at your phone, wondering what to send — because you already know that the wrong message is worse than no message at all. One daily blast of "NEW STOCK!!!" and customers mute you. One long, boring announcement and they stop opening your messages entirely.

The problem was never the list. It is the content. A broadcast list is not a loudspeaker. It is a private message to two hundred people who trusted you with their number, and every message either earns that trust or spends it. This guide gives you a weekly plan: what to send, when to send it, the exact words to use, and how to know whether it is working.

## The 3-to-1 rule

Before the plan, the rule that makes the plan work. For every one message that sells something, send three messages that are useful, interesting or kind. Three to one. That ratio is what separates a broadcast list people open from one people mute.

A useful message teaches something: how to care for the product, how to style it, how to use it. An interesting message shows something: a new arrival, a behind-the-scenes photo, a customer's result. A kind message celebrates something: a customer's birthday, a thank-you, a small win. A selling message says: here is the product, here is the price, here is how to order.

Most small businesses get this backwards. They send four selling messages and zero useful ones, and then wonder why nobody reads the fifth. Flip the ratio and the selling messages start working, because they arrive in a stream the customer already enjoys opening.

## Monday: something useful

Start the week with a message that helps, not one that sells. It should be short enough to read in ten seconds, and it should be about the customer's life, not your shop.

A skincare seller might send: "Quick tip: apply your face cream on damp skin after washing your face, not on dry skin. It absorbs better and lasts longer. Try it tonight and tell me if you notice a difference."

A food vendor might send: "Our ofada rice cooks best if you soak it for 20 minutes before boiling. It comes out softer and you use less gas. Save this for your next pot."

A tailor might send: "If your ankara fades after washing, turn it inside out before you wash it and dry it in shade, not direct sun. The colour will stay bright much longer."

Notice what these messages do not contain: no price, no link, no "order now". They contain one useful thing, given freely. The customer reads it, uses it, and remembers who sent it. When Friday's selling message arrives, she opens it — because Monday's message was worth opening.

## Wednesday: something interesting

Midweek is for showing, not telling. A photo or a short video of something real: a new product arriving, a customer wearing your dress, an order being packed, a before-and-after.

A fashion seller might send a photo of a new fabric with the words: "This fabric just arrived from the market this morning. Only 6 yards available. I will post the finished styles on Friday."

A baker might send a 15-second video of a cake being decorated: "Today's birthday cake for a customer in Lekki. Chocolate, three layers, red velvet filling."

A phone accessories seller might send: "This is the new Oraimo power bank we just stocked. It charges a phone four times. I tested it myself yesterday."

Keep it to one photo or one short video and two or three sentences. Do not send five photos at once — that is a catalogue, not a message, and nobody scrolls through five photos from a broadcast. One good image, one short caption, and stop.

## Friday: the selling message

Friday is the day to sell, because customers are planning their weekend — the wedding, the owambe, the outing, the restock. Your selling message should have four things and nothing else: what it is, how much it costs, how to order, and when the offer ends.

"Our Ankara 6-yard fabric is now ₦18,000 (was ₦22,000) until Sunday evening. Only 8 pieces left. Send 'ANKARA' to order and I will confirm delivery for Saturday."

That message works because it is specific. The product is named. The price is stated. The deadline is real. The order word is one word. Compare it with the message most sellers send: "We have new stock available, quality materials, affordable prices, DM to order!!!" That message names nothing, prices nothing, and asks the customer to do the work of finding out. Nobody does.

Send the selling message once a week, on Friday, and only once. If the offer is still open on Saturday, do not resend it. One selling message a week, arriving after three useful ones, gets opened and acted on. Two selling messages a week get muted.

## Sunday: rest

Do not send anything on Sunday. Your customers are with family, in church, or resting, and a business message on Sunday feels like an intrusion. Rest is part of the plan. A list that messages six days a week is a list that gets muted. A list that messages three days a week, with something worth reading, is a list that gets opened.

The only exception is a reply. If a customer messages you on Sunday, answer her. Replies are conversations, not broadcasts, and a fast reply on a Sunday can win a customer for life. The rule is about what you send unprompted, not about how fast you answer.

## Segmenting: not everyone gets the same message

As your list grows, split it. Three small lists beat one big list, because different customers want different messages.

The buyers list is for people who have bought from you. They get the Monday tips, the Wednesday previews, and the Friday offers — and occasionally a thank-you message just for them.

The browsers list is for people who chatted with you but never bought. They get the useful messages and the interesting ones, but not the hard offers, until they buy. Pushing offers at people who have not bought yet feels like pressure. Teaching them and showing them is what turns them into buyers.

The VIP list is for repeat buyers — your best customers. They get early access: new stock on Thursday, before Friday's general message. They get a free delivery weekend once a month. They get a birthday message. This list should be small. If everyone is VIP, nobody is.

Splitting takes ten minutes. In WhatsApp, create a new broadcast list, add the right contacts, and name it clearly: "Buyers", "Browsers", "VIP". Send each list its own version of the message. It is a little more work, and it is worth it — because a message written for buyers will always outperform a message written for everybody.

## Writing messages people open

Three rules for the writing itself. First, the first line is everything. WhatsApp shows the first few words as a preview, and that preview decides whether the message gets opened. Start with the useful thing, not with your business name. "Quick tip: your face cream works better on damp skin" gets opened. "Joetech Beauty Store wishes to inform our esteemed customers..." does not.

Second, keep it short. A broadcast message should fit on one phone screen without scrolling. If your message is longer than five short lines, cut it in half. Long messages are not read. They are skimmed, or skipped entirely.

Third, one action per message. "Send ANKARA to order" is one action. "Send ANKARA to order, follow our Instagram, join our group, and check our website" is four actions, and the customer will do none of them. Pick the one thing you want, say it plainly, and stop.

## How to know it is working

WhatsApp does not give you analytics, but you can measure a broadcast list with three simple signs.

Replies. A good message gets replies — questions, thank-yous, orders. If a message gets no replies at all, it was either uninteresting or it went to people who do not care. Count the replies for each message for a month and you will see which type of message your customers actually want.

Orders. The Friday message should bring orders. If it brings none for three weeks in a row, the problem is the offer, the price, or the product — not the list. Change one thing at a time and watch.

Silence. If people stop replying, stop ordering, or ask to be removed, the list is tired. That usually means too many messages, too many selling messages, or messages sent at the wrong time — late at night, early in the morning, or on Sundays. Cut back to the plan: three days a week, three useful to one selling, and nothing on Sunday.

Once a month, prune the list. Remove anyone who has not opened or replied in 60 days. A smaller list of people who actually read your messages is worth more than a big list of people who muted you months ago.

## The mistakes that get you muted

Five mistakes end most broadcast lists. Sending every day. Sending long messages. Sending only selling messages. Sending late at night or early in the morning. And adding people who never saved your number — those messages are never delivered anyway, and if those people report you, WhatsApp can restrict your number.

Avoid all five and your list becomes one of the most valuable things your business owns: a direct line to the people who already bought from you, who already trust you, and who will keep buying if you keep showing up with something worth their ten seconds. That is what a broadcast list is for. Not shouting. Showing up, three times a week, with something useful, something interesting, and — once — something to buy.
