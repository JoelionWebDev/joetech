Ada sells wigs from her flat in Lekki. Her Instagram page is beautiful, her wigs are good, and new customers find her every week. But when she checked her order book last month, she noticed something painful: almost every customer bought once and never came back. Not because they were unhappy. Because nothing brought them back. On Instagram, a buyer who does not see your post today has already found three other wig sellers by tomorrow.

That is the loyalty problem for online sellers, and it is different from the problem a market stall faces. A stall owner sees her customers walk past every day. An online seller is invisible the moment the algorithm moves on. Your customer cannot bump into your shop on her way home. You have to go to her — on her phone, in her DMs, at the right moment, with a reason to buy again.

The good news is that you can run a full loyalty plan without an app, a website or a budget. Everything in this guide runs on the two apps your customers already open every day: WhatsApp and Instagram.

## Why online buyers disappear

First, understand what you are up against. A woman who buys a dress from a shop in Ikeja passes that shop again next week. A woman who buys a dress from an Instagram vendor has to remember the vendor's handle, search for it, and hope the page still appears in her feed. Most of the time, she does not. She buys from whoever appears in front of her next.

Second, online buying is forgettable when it goes well. A smooth delivery and a nice product should earn a second order, but the customer has no reminder. No bag with your logo sitting in her wardrobe. No shop sign she sees on her street. The memory fades in days.

Third, your competitors are one tap away. On the street, a customer has to walk to the next shop. Online, the next shop is the next post. Loyalty online is not built by being good once. It is built by staying in touch on purpose, between orders.

## The 48-hour follow-up

The most valuable message you will ever send is the one after the first delivery. Within 48 hours of a customer receiving her order, send a short WhatsApp message: "Hi Ada, your package arrived yesterday. How is the wig sitting? If anything is not right, tell me and I will sort it." That is it. No selling. No link. Just care.

Two things happen. The customer feels looked after, which is rare enough online that she remembers it. And you learn whether there is a problem while you can still fix it — before she quietly decides never to buy from you again and tells her friends why.

Then, seven days later, send one more message. Not a broadcast. A personal note tied to what she bought: "Hope the red velvet dress worked for the wedding. We just got a new fabric in a similar style if you want first look before I post it." This is the moment most sellers miss. The customer is still happy, the product is still fresh in her mind, and you are offering something relevant, not shouting into a group.

Tunde sells sneakers on WhatsApp from Surulere. He started doing the 48-hour follow-up for every order in October. Within six weeks, his repeat orders doubled — not because he discounted anything, but because customers who felt checked on came back when they needed their next pair, instead of scrolling for someone new.

## A points system that runs on WhatsApp

You do not need an app to run points. You need a notebook or a Google Sheet and a rule your customers can remember in one sentence: every ₦1,000 you spend earns 10 points, and 500 points gets you ₦5,000 off your next order.

Here is how it works in practice. A skincare seller in Abuja keeps one sheet with four columns: name, phone number, points, and last order date. When a customer orders, she adds the points and sends a message: "Thank you! You now have 320 points. At 500 points you get ₦5,000 off." The customer can see progress. Progress is the whole game — it is what makes her choose you for the next purchase instead of a stranger.

Keep the maths dead simple. If a customer needs a calculator to work out what her points are worth, she will ignore the programme. One rule to remember: the reward should feel reachable within two or three orders, not ten. A customer who needs a year to earn a reward will forget the programme exists.

And pay the reward fast. The moment a customer crosses the line, send the discount code the same day. A reward that arrives two months late feels like a broken promise, and one broken promise travels through group chats faster than ten kept ones.

## The VIP list: your Close Friends and your private broadcast

Your best customers should not get the same messages as everybody else. Make two small lists.

On Instagram, use the Close Friends list for buyers only. Post new stock there first, a few hours before it goes public. A wig seller in Lekki posts new arrivals to Close Friends on Thursday evening and to everyone else on Friday morning. Her regulars get first pick, and they know it. That feeling — being inside before everyone else — is a reward that costs nothing.

On WhatsApp, keep a separate broadcast list for repeat buyers. This list gets the good messages: early access, a free delivery weekend, a small gift with the next order. It does not get the daily "new stock" blast. When a message arrives on the VIP list, the customer knows it is worth opening, because it is not the same message everyone else got.

Keep both lists small and honest. If everyone is VIP, nobody is. A customer joins the VIP list after her second order. That is the rule, and it makes the second order itself a goal.

## The reorder reminder

Some products run out on a schedule, and that schedule is money. A bottle of hair cream lasts about three weeks. A pack of lashes lasts a month. A 5kg bag of chin chin lasts two weeks in a house with children. If you sell anything people use up, you know roughly when they will need it again. Message them just before that day.

"Hi Blessing, your hair cream from three weeks ago should be running low. I have your usual in stock if you want me to hold one for you." That message is not marketing. It is a reminder of a real need, at the exact moment the need returns. Sellers who do this well say it brings back more customers than any discount, because it arrives when the customer was about to start looking anyway.

Write down the date of every first order and the product. Three weeks later, send the reminder. It takes two minutes per customer, and it is the closest thing to a shop bell ringing when a regular walks in.

## Turn loyal buyers into your marketers

Your happiest repeat customers are also your best source of new ones. Give them a simple code based on their name — ADA10, for example — and tell them: share it with a friend, and when the friend orders, you both get 10 percent off. The buyer feels generous rather than salesy, because she is giving her friend a discount, not just earning one for herself.

Do not run this for everyone. Offer it to customers after their second order, as part of the VIP list. That way the code carries weight: it comes from a real customer, not from a random post.

## What kills online loyalty

Three mistakes end most online loyalty plans. The first is broadcast spam. If you message your whole list every day with "new stock", customers mute you, and then they never see the messages that matter. Two or three messages a month to the general list is enough. The VIP list can take one a week, because it is smaller and the messages are better.

The second is discounting everything. If every message is a price cut, customers learn to wait for the cut and never pay full price. Rewards should feel like a thank-you for loyalty, not the normal price. Free delivery, early access, a small gift with the order — these keep your price intact while still rewarding the customer.

The third is forgetting to reward at all. A points system nobody updates is worse than no system, because the customer was promised something and watched it never arrive. If you start a programme, update it the same day as every order. Ten minutes at the end of the day is all it takes.

## Your first 30 days

Week one: message your last twenty customers with the 48-hour follow-up style check-in, even if their orders were a while ago. You will be surprised how many reply, and a few will order again on the spot.

Week two: set up your points sheet and your VIP lists. Write the rules in one short message and pin it in your notes so you can copy it whenever a customer asks.

Week three: start the reorder reminders for your most common product. Pick ten customers whose products should be running out and message them.

Week four: look at the numbers. How many customers ordered a second time? How many points did you give out, and what did the rewards cost? If the second orders are rising and the rewards cost less than a tenth of the extra sales, the plan is working. Keep it going and add one improvement a month.

Online loyalty is not about tricks. It is about doing, deliberately and on a schedule, what a good shopkeeper does naturally: remembering the customer, checking on her, and giving her a reason to come back to you instead of the next page in her feed. Do that consistently, and the customers you already have become the foundation your business grows on.
