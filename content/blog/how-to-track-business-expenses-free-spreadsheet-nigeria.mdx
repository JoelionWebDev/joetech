Emeka sells phone accessories from a small shop in Computer Village, Ikeja. He does good business most weeks, but every month ends the same way: he looks at his bank balance and cannot say where the money went. In November he stopped guessing and built a spreadsheet, on his phone, in about thirty minutes, with a free Google account. This guide builds the same spreadsheet, cell by cell, so you can copy it exactly.

You need nothing you do not already have: a phone, a Gmail account, and the Google Sheets app, which is free. Open the app, tap the plus sign, and create a blank spreadsheet. Name it after your business and the year — Emeka called his "Emeka Accessories Expenses 2026".

## The Expenses Tab: Seven Columns, Nothing More

Your first tab is where every expense lives. Tap the tab at the bottom, rename it "Nov 2026" — not "Expenses", and you will see why in a minute — and type these seven headings across row 1, one per column:

| Column | Heading | What goes in it |
|---|---|---|
| A | Date | The day the money left, like 03/11/2026 |
| B | Description | What you bought, in plain words |
| C | Category | One word from your category list |
| D | Payment method | Cash, Transfer, POS, or Card |
| E | Amount | The naira figure, numbers only |
| F | Receipt? | Yes or No |
| G | Notes | Supplier name, or anything extra |

Each column earns its place. The date lets you find a week later what happened on a particular day. The description is your memory: "50 phone cases from Alaba supplier" will still make sense in March, while "Cases" will not. The category is what makes the totals work — it is the column your formulas will read. The payment method tells you where the money came from, which matters when your cash and your bank balance disagree. The amount is numbers only, no naira sign, no commas, no words: type 45000, not ₦45,000. A spreadsheet cannot add up a column that has text mixed into it, and one stray naira sign in one cell will break every total below it. The receipt column is a simple Yes or No, and the notes column holds the supplier name or the customer the expense relates to.

Two small jobs finish the setup. Make row 1 bold, then freeze it: tap View, then Freeze, then 1 row, so the headings stay visible when you scroll. Widen column B a little, because descriptions are the longest thing you will type. Seven columns, one frozen header row, and you are ready to enter expenses.

## The Categories Tab and the Dropdown That Stops Typos

Categories are where most homemade spreadsheets quietly break. If you type "Transport" on Monday and "transport" on Wednesday, Google Sheets treats them as two different categories, and your transport total comes out wrong. The cure is a second tab and a dropdown.

Create a new tab and call it "Categories". In column A, type your list, one category per row, starting from A1:

Stock, Transport, Generator fuel, Data and airtime, Packaging, Rent and shop costs, Salaries, Marketing, Bank charges, Repairs, Food for staff, Miscellaneous.

Twelve is enough for most small businesses. Emeka added a thirteenth, "Owner drawings", for money he takes from the till for himself — that money is not a business cost, and if it hides inside "Miscellaneous" his profit figure lies to him every month.

Now go back to the "Nov 2026" tab, select column C from C2 down to C200, tap Data, then Data validation, and set the rule to "Dropdown from a range" pointing at Categories!A1:A13. From now on, every time you tap a cell in the Category column, you pick from a list instead of typing. No typos, no two spellings of the same category. This one step takes two minutes and saves every total you will ever calculate.

## The Formulas: SUM, SUMIF and COUNTIF, Written Out

You need three formulas. Type them exactly as shown, starting with the equals sign.

The first is SUM, which adds up a column. At the bottom of your Amount column, in cell E201, type:

=SUM(E2:E200)

That adds up every amount from row 2 to row 200. The formula must cover all the rows you use. Every time you add an expense, the total changes by itself.

The second is SUMIF, which adds up only the expenses in one category. In a spare area of the tab, columns I and J, build a small summary. In I2 type "Stock", and in J2 type:

=SUMIF(C2:C200,"Stock",E2:E200)

Read it aloud and it explains itself: look down column C, find every row that says "Stock", and add up the matching amounts from column E. Copy the formula down for each category, changing only the word in quotes. Each row of the summary now shows what one category cost you this month, updating itself as you enter expenses.

The third formula is COUNTIF, which counts rather than adds. In any spare cell, type:

=COUNTIF(F2:F200,"No")

That counts how many expenses have no receipt. If the answer is 40 out of 60 expenses, two-thirds of your spending has no proof, and you know what to fix next week.

Formulas read exactly what you typed: if your SUMIF says "Transport" but your dropdown says "Transport and delivery", the answer comes back zero. When a formula returns zero and you know you spent money, check the spelling first — it is the spelling almost every time.

## Tabs by Month, Not by Category

Here is the decision that decides whether your spreadsheet survives past January: organise your tabs by month. One tab per month — "Nov 2026", "Dec 2026", "Jan 2027" — each with the same seven columns and the same formulas. Add a final tab called "Summary" that pulls the months together.

Why months and not categories? A tab per category means twelve tabs for November alone, and you would have to open each one to enter a single day's spending. Nobody keeps that up for a week. A tab per month means you open one tab, enter everything for today, and close the app. At the end of the month you duplicate the tab, rename the copy for the new month, clear the entries, and start again — duplicating keeps your headings, your dropdown and your formulas intact, so January takes ten seconds to set up.

Your Summary tab is one row per month. In column A, the month name. In column B, the month's total expenses, pulled straight from that month's tab with a formula like this:

=SUM('Nov 2026'!E2:E200)

The single quotes matter, because the tab name has a space in it. In column C, the month's total income, from an Income tab built the same way. In column D, the profit, which is income minus expenses — income is in column C and expenses in column B, so type =C2-B2.

After three months, that Summary tab is the most valuable page in your business: it shows whether November beat October, and whether the business is growing or just busy.

## A Worked Week: Emeka's First Seven Days

Here is a real week from Emeka's sheet — his first week of November, as he entered it:

| Date | Description | Category | Payment | Amount |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 03/11/2026 | 50 phone cases, Alaba supplier | Stock | Transfer | 45,000 |
| 03/11/2026 | Bus fare to Computer Village | Transport | Cash | 1,500 |
| 04/11/2026 | 30 chargers, wholesale | Stock | Transfer | 28,000 |
| 05/11/2026 | MTN 20GB data bundle | Data and airtime | Transfer | 9,000 |
| 05/11/2026 | Nylon bags and packaging | Packaging | Cash | 3,500 |
| 06/11/2026 | Petrol, 10 litres, generator | Generator fuel | Cash | 9,500 |
| 07/11/2026 | Dispatch rider to Lekki customer | Transport | Transfer | 3,000 |
| 07/11/2026 | Bank transfer charges | Bank charges | Transfer | 850 |
| 08/11/2026 | 40 earphones, wholesale | Stock | Transfer | 22,000 |
| 08/11/2026 | Lunch for two shop staff | Food for staff | Cash | 4,000 |

Ten rows, and the SUM formula at the bottom reads ₦126,350. The SUMIF summary breaks it down: Stock ₦95,000 (45,000 + 28,000 + 22,000), Transport ₦4,500, Data and airtime ₦9,000, Packaging ₦3,500, Generator fuel ₦9,500, Bank charges ₦850, Food for staff ₦4,000. Add the category totals and you get ₦126,350 again — the parts must equal the whole. If they do not, a category is misspelled or a row sits outside the formula's range.

Emeka's income that week, from his Income tab, was ₦182,000 in sales. Profit is income minus expenses: 182,000 minus 126,350 is ₦55,650 for the week. Notice what the sheet reveals that his memory never did. Stock is three-quarters of his spending, which is normal for his trade — but generator fuel and data together cost him ₦18,500 in one week, which is ₦74,000 in a month if it holds. He now charges his power bank and inverter while his neighbour's generator runs, and he buys data in a bigger bundle. Two small changes, found by one small table.

## The Month-End Close: Thirty Minutes on the First

A spreadsheet you never close is a notebook you never read. Emeka closes his month on the first of the new month; it takes half an hour. He opens the finished month's tab and checks the Receipt? column first, using the COUNTIF formula — every "No" is an expense he should chase a receipt or a bank alert for while he still remembers it. Then he scrolls down the descriptions and fixes any that are vague, which is why the close happens on the first and not the fifteenth.

Next he reads the SUMIF summary. Which category grew compared with last month, and does he know why? Is the profit figure real, or is it hiding money he took from the till? He checks the Owner drawings row. If it is empty but he took money for lunch and his younger brother's school fees, the profit figure is lying — he enters the drawings before believing anything else on the page.

Then he copies the month's totals into the Summary tab, writes down two decisions for the new month, and files the paper receipts in an envelope marked with the month and year.

## When the Formulas Misbehave

Three errors cover almost everything that goes wrong. The first is #VALUE! in your total, which almost always means a cell in the Amount column contains text — a naira sign, or a word like "pending". Delete the text, type the number only, and the total returns. The second is a SUMIF that returns zero when you know you spent money: the category word in the formula does not match the dropdown spelling. The third is a total that is too small, which usually means your formula range stops at row 100 and you have entered expenses down to row 120. Change E2:E100 to E2:E200 and the missing rows appear in the total.

Build the sheet tonight from yesterday's spending and your bank alerts. Enter ten real expenses, write the three formulas, and watch the totals appear. Ten honest rows beat a perfect system you plan to build next month. By your third month-end close, you will know exactly where your money goes — the point where a small business stops guessing and starts deciding.
