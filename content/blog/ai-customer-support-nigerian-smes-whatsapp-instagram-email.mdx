It is Tuesday morning in Surulere. A customer named Blessing saw a dress on your Instagram at 9 p.m. last night and asked in the comments, "Is size 14 available?" Nobody replied. At 7 a.m. she sent the same question on WhatsApp. At 9 a.m., still no answer, she emailed. By 10 a.m. she has bought the dress from a competitor in Yaba — and your business has answered her three times, in three different places, like three different shops that never talk to each other.

The fix is not hiring three support staff. It is building one system that sits behind all three channels, answers from one set of facts, and knows when to step aside for a human. That is what this guide builds: the routing, the split between AI and human, the exact handover words, and the honest cost in naira.

## How One Customer Moves Across Three Channels

First, understand how your customers actually behave, because the system has to follow them, not the other way round.

Instagram is where discovery happens. A customer finds you through a video or a photo, and her first message is usually short and visual: "How much?", "Do you have this in blue?", "How many left?" She is browsing, not yet committed, and she will vanish if the reply takes six hours. Speed matters more here than completeness.

WhatsApp is where buying happens. The same customer moves to WhatsApp when she is serious — she wants the delivery cost to Enugu, the account details, a confirmation that her size is in stock. WhatsApp messages are longer, more specific, and closer to money. This is also where she comes back after buying: "Has my order been dispatched?" "The rider is calling me, is this your number?"

Email is where the formal stuff lives. Receipts, complaints, corporate orders, "please send a quotation for 50 gift boxes for our end-of-year party". Email customers expect a slower reply — a few hours is fine — but they expect it to be complete, correct, and in writing.

One customer, three channels, three moods. A system that treats them as three separate businesses will fail her at least once. A system that treats them as one conversation — with the customer's name, her order, and her history carried across — will keep her.

## The Routing: One Brain, Three Doors

The heart of the system is a single knowledge sheet. Not three documents, not a price list on Instagram and a different one on WhatsApp. One Google Sheet, or one document, that every channel reads from. It holds your products and prices in naira, your delivery zones and costs, your opening hours, your refund and exchange policy, your payment details, and the answers to the twenty questions customers ask most.

Routing works like a receptionist with a good memory. When a message arrives on any channel, the system checks two things before it answers: who is this, and what is this about?

Who is this, it answers from the phone number on WhatsApp, the Instagram handle, or the email address. If Blessing messaged on Instagram last night and is now on WhatsApp, the system matches her by the phone number she gave in her first conversation and pulls up the thread. Her WhatsApp reply can then say, "Yes, size 14 is available — same dress you asked about on Instagram last night." That one sentence tells her she is talking to one business, not three strangers.

What is this about, decides where the message goes. Price, availability, delivery cost, delivery time, opening hours, order status — these go to the AI, which answers in seconds from the knowledge sheet. Complaints, refunds, damaged goods, payment disputes, and anything involving large amounts of money go to a human, immediately. There is no middle queue where a complaint waits for the AI to finish guessing.

You do not need expensive software for this. A free setup uses WhatsApp Business, Instagram's built-in quick replies, Gmail, and a single AI tool — ChatGPT or Claude on a free or paid tier — connected through a free automation tool like ManyChat or Make.com. The automation is the receptionist: it takes the message, checks the knowledge sheet, and either answers or passes it to you.

## What the AI Answers, and What Only a Human Touches

This split is the whole system. Get it wrong and you either burn out answering the same question two hundred times a week, or you let a machine insult a customer who deserved a person.

The AI answers anything with a written, factual answer. Price of the dress in size 14: ₦48,000. Delivery to Port Harcourt: ₦4,500, three days. Opening hours: 9 a.m. to 7 p.m., Monday to Saturday. Order status: dispatched yesterday, rider's number is this. Stock: three pieces left in blue, none in red. These answers are fast, consistent, and identical on all three channels, because they all come from the same sheet.

A human handles everything where the customer is upset, confused, or spending serious money. The triggers are simple, and you should write them down and pin them where anyone working the phones can see them:

- The customer is angry, is writing in capitals, or has used the words "complain", "refund", "return", "scam", or "lawyer".
- The customer has asked the same question twice, which means the first answer did not satisfy them.
- The order is damaged, wrong, or late — anything where the customer has already paid and is disappointed.
- The order is large or corporate — fifty gift boxes, a wedding order for 300 guests, a company asking for a quotation. Money that size deserves a person.
- The customer is asking about something the AI does not know. A machine that guesses at a price invents a price, and an invented price becomes a promise you have to keep.

There is one more human-only case that owners forget: the customer who is clearly vulnerable or distressed. Someone who has been scammed before and is nervous about paying, someone who is ordering for a funeral, someone who writes that they are confused. A machine cannot read that. You can, and the customer will remember that you did.

The split, in one line: the AI handles the questions you have already answered a hundred times; you handle the conversations that have never happened before.

## The Handover: Exact Words That Keep the Customer

A handover is the moment the AI steps aside, and it is where most systems fall apart. The customer has been talking to a fast, helpful responder, and suddenly she is staring at "please wait for an agent" with no name, no time, and no idea whether anyone is coming. That silence is where customers leave.

A good handover does four things in two sentences: it names the human, it says when they will arrive, it proves the human already knows the story, and it gives the customer something to do while she waits.

On WhatsApp, when a complaint comes in at 8 p.m., the AI says: "Thank you for letting us know, Blessing. I am passing you to Adaeze now — she handles all deliveries and she already has your order details. She will reply here within 30 minutes. If the rider calls before then, his number is 0803 555 0142."

On email, the handover is slower but more formal, which suits the channel: "Thank you for your email. I have passed your complaint to our manager, Chidi Okafor, who will reply to this same thread within one working day with a resolution. Your order reference is ORD-2214, and it is attached to your case so nothing needs to be repeated."

Notice what none of these say. None of them says "this is a bot". None of them says "please wait" with no time attached. None of them asks the customer to start over. Starting over is the insult that makes a handover feel like a punishment. The customer told her story once. The system carries it forward.

Set a clock on every handover and keep it. WhatsApp and Instagram: a human within 30 minutes during business hours, and by 9 a.m. the next morning after hours. Email: within one working day. If you cannot keep the 30 minutes, change the promise — "within two hours" kept is worth more than "within 30 minutes" broken.

## What It Costs, in Naira

Here is the honest money, for a small business doing roughly 100 to 300 customer conversations a week.

The ₦0 route: WhatsApp Business app, Instagram Business account, Gmail — all free. ChatGPT or Claude on the free tier, connected through ManyChat's free tier or Make.com's free tier, answering from your Google Sheet. Your only cost is data and the afternoon it takes to write the knowledge sheet. This covers a business doing under 100 conversations a week comfortably.

The comfortable route, ₦15,000 to ₦55,000 a month: a paid AI tier at roughly ₦10,000 to ₦20,000 a month, depending on the plan and the exchange rate that month; a paid automation tool at roughly ₦8,000 to ₦25,000 a month; and a WhatsApp Business API number through a provider like Twilio or a Nigerian aggregator, which charges per conversation — budget ₦5,000 to ₦10,000 a month at this volume. At this level the replies are faster, the limits disappear, and the system handles 300 conversations a week without you touching your phone at midnight.

Compare that with the alternative. One part-time support staff member in Lagos costs ₦100,000 to ₦180,000 a month, works eight hours a day, and still cannot answer at 2 a.m. The AI system answers at 2 a.m., on all three channels, for a fraction of that — and the human you already have spends their hours on the conversations that actually need them.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Will customers know they are talking to AI?

Most will not care, as long as the answer is correct and fast. Be honest if asked — "I am the shop's automated assistant, and Adaeze will take over if you need a person" — but do not open every conversation by announcing the machine. Customers care about the answer, not the author.

### What if the AI gives a wrong price?

That happens when the knowledge sheet is out of date, not because the AI is clever. One sheet, updated the day a price changes, is the entire prevention. If a wrong price does go out, honour it for that customer and fix the sheet. A ₦5,000 loss on one order is cheaper than a customer telling her group chat you change prices.

## One Conversation, Three Doors

Blessing's story ends differently with one system behind the three channels. She comments on Instagram at 9 p.m. and gets an answer in forty seconds: size 14 is available, ₦48,000. She moves to WhatsApp at 7 a.m., and the reply already knows her: same dress, delivery to Enugu is ₦4,000, three days. She pays, and the confirmation arrives by email before lunch. Three channels, one business, one conversation — and a customer who tells her friends that your shop actually replies.

Start with the knowledge sheet this week. One document, every price, every policy, every answer. The channels, the automation, and the AI all hang off that one sheet. Build it well, and your customers will stop experiencing your business as three shops that never talk — and start experiencing it as one shop that is always awake.
