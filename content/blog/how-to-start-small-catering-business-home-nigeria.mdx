If you can cook well, people have probably told you to start a catering business. They are right. Nigerians eat out, order in, and hire caterers for weddings, birthdays, office parties, church events, and burial ceremonies all year round. A home catering business lets you turn a skill you already have into steady income, starting from your own kitchen, without renting a shop or hiring a large team.

But cooking for your family and cooking for paying customers are different things. Customers pay for consistency, cleanliness, punctuality, and presentation, not just taste. A delicious pot of jollof rice that arrives two hours late, in a leaking pack, will not bring a repeat order. A good meal that arrives hot, on time, and beautifully packed will bring referrals.

This guide explains how to start a small catering business from home in Nigeria. You will learn how to choose your menu, price your food for profit, set up your kitchen for business, handle food safety and power problems, find your first customers, and grow from small orders to full events.

## Start with a Menu You Can Cook Well Every Time

Do not offer twenty dishes on day one. A small, focused menu is easier to cook well, easier to buy ingredients for, and easier for customers to choose from. Pick five to eight dishes you already cook confidently: jollof rice, fried rice, ofada rice, amala and ewedu, pounded yam and egusi, grilled chicken, small chops, or whatever your strength is.

Think about who you are cooking for. Office workers in Lekki want lunch packs delivered by noon. Families in Surulere want weekend bulk orders. Event organisers want full menus for fifty to two hundred guests. Choose one group first and build your menu around them.

Test your menu with real buyers before you print it. Cook your dishes for friends, neighbours, and colleagues, and ask for honest feedback about taste, portion size, and presentation. If three people say your fried rice is too oily, reduce the oil. If they say the portion is small for the price, increase it or reduce the price. Testing with ten paying customers teaches you more than cooking for your family for a year.

Write your menu down with prices, portion sizes, and minimum orders. For example: "Jollof rice with chicken, ₦3,500 per pack, minimum 10 packs" or "Small chops box, ₦5,000, minimum 20 boxes". A written menu stops confusion, helps you quote fast, and makes you look professional when a customer asks for prices on WhatsApp.

Plan for ingredients. Know which market you will buy from — Mile 12 for tomatoes and peppers, Oyingbo for rice and oil, or your local market for daily needs — and check your costs weekly. Food prices in Nigeria change often, so adjust your menu prices when a key ingredient becomes much more expensive.

## Price Your Food for Profit

Pricing food is where many home caterers lose money. They charge what a neighbour charges, or what feels fair, without adding up the real cost of cooking. The result is a busy kitchen and an empty pocket.

Start with the cost of ingredients for one serving. Weigh and measure. If a bag of rice costs ₦55,000 and gives you 100 servings, that is ₦550 per serving. Add the chicken, the oil, the tomatoes, the spices, and the seasoning. Then add the packaging: the food pack, the foil, the cutlery, and the bag. Then add the gas or charcoal, and the delivery cost if you deliver. That total is your cost per serving.

Then add your time and your profit. Cooking for fifty people takes hours of shopping, preparation, cooking, and packing. If your cost per serving is ₦2,000 and you sell at ₦3,500, you have ₦1,500 to cover your time, your gas, and your profit. If you sell at ₦2,200, you are working almost for free. Always know your cost per serving before you quote a price.

Price events differently from daily packs. An event order for 100 guests is not just 100 packs. It includes serving dishes, chafing dishes, staff to serve, transport, and setup time. Quote events as a package: a price per head that covers the food, the service, and the equipment.

Take a deposit before you buy ingredients for an event. A common rule is fifty percent upfront, with the balance paid before or on the day of delivery. This protects you from a customer who cancels after you have bought food for 200 guests. Write the deposit, the balance, the date, the menu, and the number of guests in a simple written agreement, even if it is just a WhatsApp message the customer confirms. ## Set Up Your Home Kitchen for Business

Your home kitchen can work for a small catering business, but it needs to be organised like a business kitchen. Start with cleanliness. Wash your hands often, keep your work surfaces clean, cover your hair while cooking, and keep pets out of the kitchen during cooking. Customers may never see your kitchen, but the quality of your food will show whether it was cooked in a clean place.

Organise your space. Dedicate shelves or a cupboard to your business ingredients, separate from family food. Keep your cooking pots, serving dishes, and packaging in one place so you are not searching for a lid when an order is due.

Plan for power and water. NEPA supply is unreliable, and catering depends on light for cooking, refrigeration, and packing. Keep a charged power bank and a rechargeable lamp in the kitchen, and plan your cooking so that the most power-dependent work happens when light is available. Store perishable ingredients in a freezer that stays cold, and buy ice blocks as a backup for transporting food on hot days. Always have stored water available, because cooking and washing need a steady supply.

Keep your ingredients fresh. Buy perishables close to the cooking day, store meat and fish in the freezer, and check your stock before every order. Food that has gone off will ruin your reputation faster than any other mistake. When in doubt, throw it out. The cost of replacing an ingredient is small. The cost of making fifty guests sick is enormous.

## Food Safety and Hygiene Customers Can Trust

Food safety is not optional. It is the foundation of your business. One case of food poisoning can end a home catering business completely, because news travels fast in every estate and office.

Wash your hands with soap before cooking, after handling raw meat, and after touching money or your phone. Use separate chopping boards and knives for raw meat and for cooked food or vegetables. Cook meat and chicken thoroughly, and keep cooked food hot until it is served or packed. Never leave cooked food sitting at room temperature for hours.

Store food at the right temperature. Hot food should stay hot. Cold food should stay cold. When you transport food, use insulated boxes or cooler bags, and deliver as quickly as possible. On a hot Lagos afternoon, food that spends three hours in traffic in an open bag will not be safe to eat. Plan your delivery route, leave early, and use a reliable rider or driver.

Use clean water for cooking and washing. If your tap water is not reliable, keep clean stored water for cooking. Wash vegetables and fruits properly, and wash your cooking utensils with soap and clean water after every use.

## Find Your First Customers

Your first customers are usually people who already know your cooking: neighbours, colleagues, church members, and friends. Tell them you have started catering, show them your menu, and offer them a first order at a fair price. Ask them to recommend you to others, and thank them when they do.

Use WhatsApp Business. Set up a business profile with your menu, your prices, and photos of your food. Post your dishes on your status every day, especially at meal times when people are hungry. When a customer compliments your food, ask permission to share their message as a review. Real reviews from real people convince new buyers faster than any advert.

Use Instagram and TikTok. Photograph your food in daylight, on clean plates, with a tidy background. Film short videos of you cooking, packing orders, or setting up at an event. Food videos perform very well online, because people eat with their eyes first.

Target offices and events. Visit offices in your area with a small sample and your menu, or send a polite message to the admin officer. Offer a trial lunch for a small team. One office that orders lunch every Friday is worth more than ten one-off customers, because it gives you steady weekly income. Also connect with event planners, MCs, and decorators in your area. They recommend caterers to their clients, and a planner who trusts you can bring you several events a year.

Deliver on time, every time. In catering, punctuality is part of the product. A wedding that starts at noon cannot wait for food that arrives at 2pm. Leave early, allow for Lagos traffic, and have a backup plan if your rider cancels. Customers forgive a lot, but they rarely forgive lateness at an event.

## Delivery, Packaging, and Managing Orders

Delivery and packaging decide how your food arrives, and how it arrives decides whether the customer orders again. Hot food should arrive hot. Cold drinks should arrive cold. Nothing should leak.

Invest in good food packs. Use sturdy, food-safe packs with tight lids for rice and soups, and separate packs for sauces so nothing spills. Wrap hot food in foil inside the pack to keep it warm. For events, use chafing dishes to keep food hot at the venue, and bring serving spoons, plates, and napkins if your package includes them. Cheap, leaking packs save you a few naira and cost you customers.

Label your packs. Write the dish name on each pack, especially when an order has several dishes. A customer who ordered jollof and fried rice should not have to open both packs to find out which is which.

Plan your delivery. For small lunch orders, use a reliable dispatch rider and confirm the fee with the customer before dispatch. For events, transport the food yourself or with a trusted driver, and arrive at least an hour before serving time so you can set up properly. Always confirm the venue address, the contact person's name and phone number, and the serving time the day before the event.

Keep a simple order book. For every order, write down the customer name, phone number, menu, quantity, price, deposit paid, balance, delivery address, and date. A notebook works, but a simple sheet on your phone is easier to search. This record stops confusion, helps you plan your shopping, and becomes your customer list for future marketing.

## Growing from Small Orders to Big Events

Growth in catering comes from doing small orders well, again and again. A customer who orders lunch packs for her office every week will recommend you for her sister's wedding.

As orders grow, get help. You cannot cook for 200 guests alone. Train one or two assistants — a family member, a neighbour, or a hired hand — to help with washing, chopping, packing, and serving. Teach them your recipes and your hygiene rules, and pay them fairly. Good helpers let you take bigger orders without burning out.

Buy equipment gradually. A bigger pot, an extra gas cylinder, more chafing dishes, a large cooler box, and a deep freezer are all useful, but you do not need them all in week one. Buy each item from the profit of the orders that need it. A freezer bought from the profit of three events is a business asset. A freezer bought on credit before your first order is a burden.

Raise your prices carefully as your reputation grows. When you have steady customers, good reviews, and a full order book, you can charge more than when you started. Increase prices in small steps, and explain the change when a regular customer asks. Quality and reliability are worth paying for, and customers who value them will stay.

Keep learning. Try new dishes, watch how other caterers present their food, and ask your customers what they would like to see on your menu. A caterer who adds one new dish every few months stays interesting.

## Common Mistakes

The first mistake is underpricing. Many home caterers charge only for ingredients and forget gas, packaging, delivery, and their own time. Add up every cost before you quote, and never take an order that loses you money.

The second mistake is taking on an event that is too big for your capacity. If you can cook for fifty people comfortably, do not accept an order for three hundred. A failed big event damages your name far more than a polite refusal. Grow your capacity first, then take bigger orders.

The third mistake is poor time management. Cooking that starts late ends late. Plan backwards from the delivery time: if food must arrive at noon, know when shopping, cooking, and packing must start, and start on time. Build in extra time for Lagos traffic and for NEPA problems.

The fourth mistake is ignoring food safety. Using questionable ingredients, reusing old oil too many times, or leaving cooked food out for hours will eventually make someone sick. When it happens, the damage to your business is far greater than the money you saved.

The fifth mistake is mixing business money with household money. Keep your catering money separate, even if it is just a different wallet or account. Track every order, every expense, and every profit. A business you cannot measure is a business you cannot grow.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need a licence to start a home catering business in Nigeria?

There is no single national catering licence for small home caterers, but you should register your business with the CAC as you grow, and follow basic food hygiene rules. Some estates and venues may ask for a food handler's certificate, so check the rules in your area and get one if it is required.

### How much capital do I need to start catering from home?

You can start with between ₦50,000 and ₦150,000 if you already have basic kitchen equipment. That covers ingredients for your first orders, packaging, and a small amount for delivery. Start with small orders, and use the profit to buy bigger pots and equipment as you grow.

### How do I price a catering order for an event?

Work out the cost per head: ingredients, packaging, gas, transport, serving staff, and equipment, plus your time and profit. Multiply by the number of guests, and add any extra costs like venue setup. Always confirm the menu, guest count, and what is included before you quote.

### How do I handle a customer who cancels an event order?

That is why you take a deposit. State clearly when you take the order that the deposit is non-refundable if the customer cancels after you have bought ingredients. For cancellations made well in advance, you can offer to move the deposit to a new date. Put the terms in writing when you take the order.

### How do I get my first catering customers?

Start with people who already know your cooking: neighbours, colleagues, and church members. Use WhatsApp Business and Instagram to show your food daily, ask happy customers for reviews, and target offices and event planners in your area.
