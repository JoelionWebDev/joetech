Ask a Nigerian small business owner how the business is doing and you will often hear the same answer: "We are managing." Ask to see the numbers and there is usually silence. The money comes in through transfers, cash, and POS. It goes out to suppliers in Balogun Market, to the dispatch rider, to fuel for the generator, to data, to food. At the end of the month, the owner cannot say whether the business made a profit or a loss.

Managing your business finances does not require an accounting degree or expensive software. It requires a simple system, a few free apps on the phone you already own, and a habit of recording every naira that moves. This guide shows you exactly how to set that up, with free tools that work in Nigeria and examples in naira you can copy.

## Separate Your Business Money From Your Personal Money

This is the first rule, and it is the one most Nigerian small businesses break. When your business money and your personal money sit in the same account, you can never tell whether the business is profitable. You also spend business money on personal needs without noticing, and then wonder why there is no cash to buy stock.

Open a separate bank account for the business. It does not need to be a corporate account at first. A second personal savings account, used only for business, is a big improvement over mixing everything. Many Nigerian banks let you open an account quickly, and some offer business accounts with no monthly charges. Pick one and commit to it.

Pay yourself a salary. Decide on a fixed monthly amount — say ₦100,000 — and transfer it from the business account to your personal account on the same date every month. That is your pay. If the business cannot afford your salary, that tells you something important about your prices or your costs. If the business earns more than your salary, the extra stays in the business to fund stock, equipment, or emergencies.

Stop the habit of dipping into the till. The shopkeeper in Ikeja who takes ₦5,000 from the day's sales to buy food for the house, then ₦3,000 the next day for transport, is slowly eating the business. If you need money for the house, take it as part of your salary, not as a random withdrawal. The discipline feels hard for the first month, and then it becomes normal.

Keep a small float for daily expenses. Put ₦20,000 or ₦30,000 in a separate wallet or a mobile money account for small daily costs like transport and airtime. When it runs out, top it up from the business account and record it.

## Track Every Sale and Every Expense

A business that does not record its money is flying blind. You do not need a complicated system. You need one place where every sale and every expense is written down, the same day it happens.

Start with what you already have: your phone. A simple Google Sheet, free and available on any Android phone or iPhone, can be your entire accounting system. Create one sheet with columns for date, description, money in, money out, and balance. Every evening, spend ten minutes entering the day's sales and expenses. A food vendor in Yaba who writes down 40 sales of ₦2,500 each knows she earned ₦100,000 that day. A vendor who relies on memory knows nothing for certain.

If you prefer an app, several free ones work well in Nigeria. Wave, free for basic accounting, lets you record income and expenses and send invoices. QuickBooks has a free trial and a simple mobile app for tracking sales. For very small businesses, even a notes app or a paper ledger works, as long as you use it every day. The best system is the one you will actually maintain.

Record cash as carefully as transfers. Cash is where Nigerian businesses lose track of money. A tailor in Surulere who receives ₦15,000 cash for a job and forgets to write it down has lost the record, even though the money is real. Write cash sales down immediately, before you spend any of it.

## Know Your Real Costs

Many Nigerian sellers price their products without knowing their true costs, and then wonder why the business never grows. Your true cost is more than the price you paid your supplier. It includes everything it takes to get the product to the customer.

List every cost that touches a sale. The product itself. Transport to the market — a trip to Mile 12 or Balogun Market costs money, whether you take a bus or drive. Packaging. Delivery by a dispatch rider. The share of your generator fuel and your data. Bank charges and Paystack or Flutterwave fees on transfers. Your rent, divided across your monthly sales. Your staff salary, if you have one.

Take the example of Chidi, who sells phone accessories from a small shop in Computer Village, Ikeja. He buys a phone case for ₦2,000, pays ₦200 in transport, ₦100 in packaging, and ₦500 for delivery to a customer in Lekki, making his true cost ₦2,800. He sells it for ₦4,500, leaving ₦1,700 before rent and other overheads. If he only counted the ₦2,000 purchase price, he would think his profit was ₦2,500 — and he would be wrong by ₦800 on every sale.

Do this calculation for your five best-selling products. You may discover that your most popular product is your least profitable one. That is valuable information. It tells you which products to promote, which to reprice, and which to stop selling.

## Build a Simple Monthly Budget

A budget is not a punishment. It is a plan that tells your money where to go before the month begins. Without one, every expense feels like an emergency, and the business lurches from one crisis to the next.

Start with your expected income. Look at the last three months of sales and take the lowest month, not the best. If you earned ₦400,000, ₦550,000, and ₦480,000, plan for ₦400,000. Planning for the worst month keeps you safe; if you earn more, that is a bonus.

Then list your fixed costs — rent, salary, data — and your variable costs — stock, transport, fuel, packaging — plus a small line for emergencies.

Now compare. If your expected income is ₦400,000 and your costs add up to ₦380,000, you have ₦20,000 of breathing room. That is tight, and it tells you to either raise your prices or cut a cost. If your costs add up to ₦450,000, you have a problem that needs fixing before the month starts, not after it ends.

Use a free budgeting app to keep the plan visible. Google Sheets works fine, and apps like Money Manager or Wallet let you set monthly limits per category and see at a glance when you are close to overspending.

## Get Paid on Time, Every Time

In Nigeria, late payment is one of the biggest threats to a small business. A client who owes you ₦200,000 for three months is holding your stock money, your rent money, and your salary in their pocket. Getting paid on time is a finance skill, not a favour you beg for.

Always agree payment terms before you start work. For products, collect at least a deposit — 50 percent is standard — before you order or make anything. For services, agree in writing on WhatsApp: "50 percent now, 50 percent on delivery." A customer who will not pay a deposit is a warning sign. Believe it.

Send professional invoices. An invoice is not just a request for money; it is a record that protects you. Free tools like Wave and Zoho Invoice let you create clean invoices with your business name, the items, the amount, your account details, and a due date. You can also create a simple invoice in minutes with Joetech's free invoice generator at joetech360.ng/tools/invoice-generator. Send the invoice on WhatsApp or by email the same day the job is done, not a week later.

Follow up politely but firmly. If payment is due on Friday, send a reminder on Thursday: "Good afternoon, this is a friendly reminder that the balance of ₦80,000 is due tomorrow. Thank you." If it is late, call. Many Nigerian clients pay the supplier who follows up, and delay the one who stays quiet.

## Save for Tax and Emergencies Before You Spend

Tax is not optional, and it is not something to think about in December. The Federal Inland Revenue Service and your state tax authority expect small businesses to pay tax, and the penalties for ignoring it are real. Even if your business is small, set money aside for tax every month so it never becomes a crisis.

A simple rule is to set aside 5 to 10 percent of your monthly income in a separate savings account the day the money comes in. If you earn ₦500,000 in a month, move ₦25,000 to ₦50,000 into a tax savings pot immediately. By the time tax is due, the money is there. Apps like PiggyVest and Cowrywise let you lock savings so you are not tempted to touch them, and they pay small interest on top.

Build an emergency fund for the business. Aim for one month of fixed costs — rent, salary, data, and essential stock — saved and untouched. For a business with ₦150,000 in monthly fixed costs, that is ₦150,000 sitting in a separate account. When your generator breaks down or a supplier demands payment before delivery, the emergency fund saves you from borrowing at high interest.

Keep the emergency fund in a different account from your daily spending. If it sits in the same account as your working money, you will spend it. Out of sight, even slightly, is out of reach.

## Use Free Apps to Run the Whole System

You do not need to buy expensive accounting software. The free apps on your phone can run the whole system described in this guide, and they work with Nigerian banks and payment methods.

For receiving payments, your bank app and a POS or transfer service are enough. Paystack and Flutterwave, both free to set up, let customers pay you by card, transfer, or USSD, and they send you a notification for every payment. Moniepoint and Opay give you a POS and an account with instant settlement. Pick the one your customers already use.

For invoicing, Wave and Zoho Invoice are free and produce professional invoices you can send on WhatsApp. For expense tracking, Money Manager or a Google Sheet does the job. For saving, PiggyVest and Cowrywise lock your money away from temptation and pay interest. For budgeting, Google Sheets is free, flexible, and easy to share with an accountant or a partner.

For receipts, use your phone camera. Photograph every paper receipt from the market, the fuel station, and the supplier, and save the photos in a Google Drive folder for the month. When tax time comes, or when you need to check a cost, the evidence is there.

## Check Your Numbers Every Week

A finance system only works if you look at it. Set aside 30 minutes every Sunday evening, or Monday morning, to review the week. This single habit separates businesses that grow from businesses that drift.

Look at five numbers. Total sales for the week. Total expenses for the week. Profit — sales minus expenses. Money owed to you by customers. Money you owe to suppliers. Write them in your sheet or your notebook. Over a few weeks, patterns appear. You will see which days are your best, which products earn the most, and which expenses keep growing.

Compare the week with the same week last month. Are sales rising or falling? Are expenses creeping up? A dispatch rider who charged ₦2,500 last month and ₦3,500 this month is a cost worth noticing. A product that sold well in October and stopped selling in November may need a price change or a promotion.

Once a month, do a deeper review. Check your budget against reality. Check your tax savings pot. Check whether customers owe you more than they did last month — if they do, your follow-up needs attention.

## Avoid the Money Mistakes That Kill Small Businesses

The first mistake is spending revenue as if it were profit. If you sell ₦500,000 of goods in a month but spent ₦400,000 buying them, your profit is not ₦500,000. It is ₦100,000 before your other costs. Many Nigerian businesses feel rich after a good sales month, spend freely, and then cannot afford stock the next month. Always subtract your costs before you celebrate.

The second mistake is borrowing to fund daily expenses. A loan to buy a machine that earns money is an investment. A loan to pay rent or buy food is a warning that the business model is broken. If you must borrow, borrow for something that will earn the money back, and have a clear repayment plan.

The third mistake is giving too much credit. Selling on credit to friends, family, and regular customers feels kind, but unpaid credit is the most common way small Nigerian businesses run out of cash. Limit credit to a few trusted customers, set a clear repayment date, and stop supplying anyone who does not pay on time. Your business is not a bank.

The fourth mistake is keeping no records at all. "I know my business in my head" works until it does not — until a customer disputes a payment, a supplier claims you owe more, or tax season arrives. Records are your protection. Write it down, every time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the best free app for managing a small business in Nigeria?**
There is no single best app. Use a combination: your bank app and Paystack or Flutterwave for receiving payments, Wave or Zoho Invoice for invoicing, Google Sheets or Money Manager for tracking, and PiggyVest or Cowrywise for saving. Pick one app per job and use it every day.

**Do I really need a separate bank account for my business?**
Yes. Mixing business and personal money is the most common reason small businesses cannot tell whether they are profitable. A separate account, even a second personal savings account used only for business, makes your finances visible and your decisions better.

**How much should I set aside for tax?**
Set aside 5 to 10 percent of your monthly income in a separate account as soon as the money comes in. By the time tax is due, the money is already there. If you are unsure what you owe, ask a local accountant — a short consultation is cheaper than a penalty.

**What if my customers refuse to pay a deposit?**
A customer who will not pay a deposit before you order or make a product is a risk. Politely explain that a 50 percent deposit covers materials and is standard practice. If they still refuse, consider whether the job is worth the risk of doing it on credit.

**How do I handle cash sales so they are not lost?**
Record cash sales immediately, in your sheet or app, before you spend any of it. Keep cash in a separate purse or tin for the business, and bank it or transfer it to your business account at the end of each day.

**Can I manage my business finances without an accountant?**
Yes, for a small business, a simple system of records, a budget, and a weekly review is enough to start. But have an accountant review your books at least once a year, and ask for help when you employ staff or your income grows.
