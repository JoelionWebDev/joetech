Many Nigerian small businesses make sales every day but still struggle with money at the end of the month. The owner works hard, customers come and go, and the shop looks busy, yet there is never enough cash for rent, restocking, or savings. In most cases, the problem is not low sales. The problem is that nobody is tracking expenses properly.

If you do not know how much you spend on transport, airtime, packaging, food, repairs, and small daily purchases, you cannot know whether your business is truly profitable. You might think you made ₦50,000 profit in a week when you actually made ₦10,000 after expenses. That gap is where many small businesses quietly fail.

The good news is that you do not need an accountant or expensive software to track your expenses. You need a simple system, a few minutes a day, and the discipline to record every naira. This guide shows you how to build that system using tools you already have, such as a notebook, your phone, and a spreadsheet, and how to turn those records into decisions that protect your profit.

## Why Tracking Expenses Matters More Than Tracking Sales

Sales feel good. Expenses feel boring. That is why most business owners focus on sales and ignore expenses until money runs out. But profit is sales minus expenses. If expenses grow faster than sales, your business can be busy and broke at the same time.

Tracking expenses helps you in five practical ways. First, it shows you where your money actually goes. You may discover that you spend more on transport or on feeding yourself from the business than you realised. Second, it helps you price correctly. If you know your true costs, you will not set prices that look attractive but lose money. Third, it helps you spot waste and theft early. A sudden rise in a particular expense is easier to notice when you have records to compare. Fourth, it makes tax and record-keeping easier. When you need to show your income and expenses for a loan, a grant, or a tax filing, clean records save you stress. Fifth, it helps you plan. When you know your average monthly expenses, you can set realistic savings and restocking targets.

A fashion designer in Lekki who tracks expenses might discover she spends ₦18,000 a month on transport to buy fabric in Yaba, and decide to buy in bulk twice a month instead of weekly. A food vendor in Ikeja might discover that gas and packaging cost more than expected, and adjust portion sizes or prices slightly. Those small decisions, made with real numbers, add up to real profit.

## Step 1: Separate Business Money From Personal Money

This is the most important step, and it must come before any tool or spreadsheet. If business money and personal money live in the same pocket, the same wallet, or the same bank account, you will never track expenses accurately. You will take ₦2,000 for lunch, ₦5,000 for fuel, and ₦3,000 for a family need, and at the end of the day you will not remember which spending was business and which was personal.

Open a separate bank account or mobile wallet for your business if you do not have one already. It does not need to be a formal business account at first. A separate personal savings account used only for business is a good start. All business income goes into that account, and all business expenses come out of it. Your personal spending comes from a different account.

Use a separate cash box or envelope for business cash. At the start of each day, put a fixed amount of cash in the business cash box for change and small purchases. At the end of the day, count what is left and record the difference. Never dip into the business cash box for personal needs without recording it as a withdrawal. If you need money for personal use, transfer a fixed amount to yourself as your salary or allowance, and record it as an owner withdrawal. That single habit will transform your records.

Tell anyone who works with you about this rule. If a staff member buys something for the business, they must bring back the receipt and the change, and the purchase must be recorded the same day. No receipt, no reimbursement without a record.

## Step 2: List Every Type of Expense Your Business Has

Before you start recording, make a list of all the expenses your business typically has. This list becomes your categories. Categories help you see patterns. Without categories, your records are just a long list of random spending.

Common categories for Nigerian small businesses include stock or raw materials, transport and delivery, rent, electricity and generator fuel, internet and airtime, packaging, salaries and wages, marketing and adverts, repairs and maintenance, bank charges, food and refreshments for the business, and miscellaneous small purchases.

Write your categories on the first page of your notebook or at the top of your spreadsheet. Keep the list short enough to use daily. Ten to twelve categories are enough for most small businesses. If you have too many categories, recording becomes slow and you will stop doing it. If you have too few, your records will be vague.

For each category, write a one-line description of what belongs there. For example, under transport, write "bus fare, bike fare, fuel for delivery, courier fees." Under packaging, write "nylon bags, cartons, labels, bottles, food packs." Clear descriptions prevent confusion later when you review your records.

Review your categories once a month. If you notice a new type of expense appearing often, add a category for it. If a category is never used, remove it. Your system should fit your business, not the other way round.

## Step 3: Choose a Tracking Method You Will Actually Use Daily

The best expense tracking method is the one you will use every day. A perfect system you abandon after three days is worse than a simple system you use for a year.

Option one is a notebook. Buy a hardcover ledger or a simple exercise book and rule columns for date, description, category, amount, and payment method. Record each expense as it happens, or at least twice a day: once at midday and once at closing. A notebook works without electricity, data, or a smartphone, and it is easy to carry in a shop or market. Its main risk is loss or damage, so keep it in a safe place and take a photo of each completed page at the end of the week.

Option two is a phone notes app or a simple expense app. Many free apps let you record expenses by category and show you a summary at the end of the week or month. If you are comfortable with your phone, this method is fast. You can record an expense in ten seconds while waiting for a customer. Choose an app that works offline, backs up your data, and lets you export your records if needed.

Option three is a spreadsheet on Google Sheets or Excel. A spreadsheet is the most powerful option for a growing business because you can sort, total, and compare expenses by category and by month. Create columns for date, description, category, amount, payment method, and receipt number. Use simple formulas to total each category automatically. If you use Google Sheets, your records are saved online and you can check them from your phone or laptop.

Many business owners use a combination. They record expenses in a notebook during the day, then transfer them to a spreadsheet in the evening. That double entry takes extra time, but it creates a backup and forces a daily review. Choose the combination that fits your day. If you are a market trader who is always on your feet, a notebook plus a weekly spreadsheet update may work best. If you run an online business from home, a phone app or spreadsheet may be enough.

Whatever method you choose, set a fixed time to record. The best times are immediately after the purchase, or at a set time each evening before you close. Do not wait until the end of the week to remember expenses. You will forget half of them.

## Step 4: Record Every Expense, Even the Small Ones

Small expenses are the silent killers of small business profit. A ₦500 bike fare here, a ₦1,000 airtime top-up there, a ₦2,000 repair, and a ₦1,500 lunch from the business account may seem too small to record. Over a month, those small amounts can add up to ₦30,000 or ₦50,000. If you do not record them, you will wonder where the money went.

Make it a rule: if business money left your hand, write it down. The amount does not matter. The description does not need to be long. "Bike to Yaba market — ₦800 — Transport" is enough. "Packaging nylon — ₦3,500 — Packaging" is enough. The key is consistency.

Keep receipts whenever possible. For larger purchases, always ask for a receipt. For small purchases where a receipt is not given, write the details in your notebook immediately. Take a photo of receipts with your phone at the end of the day and store them in a folder named by month, such as "Expenses November 2026." That photo backup will help you if the paper receipt fades or gets lost, which happens often in Nigeria's heat and humidity.

If you buy stock, record the supplier name, the items bought, the quantity, and the total cost. That record helps you compare suppliers over time. If one supplier in Oshodi consistently charges more than another in Balogun for the same fabric, your records will show it.

If you pay for something by transfer, write the transfer reference or take a screenshot and save it with the date. Bank transfers are easy to trace later, but only if you know what each transfer was for. A transfer labelled "₦15,000" with no description is useless in a month's time.

## Step 5: Do a Daily Close and a Weekly Review

Recording is only half the job. Reviewing is what turns records into useful information.

At the end of each day, take five minutes to close your books. Count your cash, check your bank and wallet balances, and add up the day's expenses and sales. Write the daily totals in your notebook or spreadsheet: total sales, total expenses, and the difference. That difference is your daily profit or loss before rent and other monthly costs. Even if the number is small, seeing it daily builds awareness.

At the end of each week, take thirty minutes to review the week. Add up expenses by category. Which category was highest? Was it stock, transport, or something else? Compare the week with the previous week. Did expenses rise suddenly? If so, why? Did you buy extra stock, pay for a repair, or spend more on delivery? Write one or two sentences about what you noticed. For example: "Transport was high this week because I went to the market three times. Next week I will buy in bulk twice."

At the end of each month, do a fuller review. Add up total sales, total expenses by category, and total profit. Compare the month with the previous month. Calculate your profit margin by dividing profit by sales and multiplying by 100. If you sold ₦400,000 in a month and spent ₦320,000, your profit is ₦80,000 and your margin is 20 percent. Knowing your margin helps you set targets. If your margin is falling, you need to either increase prices, reduce costs, or sell more.

Keep your monthly summaries in one place, such as a single spreadsheet tab or the last page of each month's notebook. Over six months, those summaries will show you trends you cannot see day by day, such as a slow season, a rising cost, or a product line that is losing money.

## Step 6: Use Your Records to Price Correctly and Plan Ahead

Many Nigerian small businesses set prices by copying competitors or by guessing. Your expense records let you price based on your real costs, which is far more reliable.

For each product or service, add up the direct costs: the cost of materials, packaging, delivery, and any direct labour. Add a share of your monthly fixed costs, such as rent and electricity, divided by the number of units you sell. That total is your true cost per unit. Your selling price must be higher than that cost by enough to leave a profit after discounts and unexpected expenses. If your true cost for a plate of food is ₦1,800 and you sell it for ₦2,000, your margin is too thin. A small rise in the price of rice or gas could wipe out your profit. Your records will show you that risk before it becomes a crisis.

Use your records to plan for known expenses. Rent is due every month or every year. School fees, festive season stock, and equipment repairs can be predicted. Set aside a small amount each week for these known costs so they do not catch you by surprise. For example, if your annual rent is ₦240,000, setting aside ₦5,000 a week will cover it without stress.

Use your records when you apply for a loan, a grant, or a partnership. Lenders and grant providers want to see that you understand your business numbers. A business owner who can show six months of sales and expense records is far more credible than one who can only say "business is moving well." Clean records open doors.

If your records show that your business is consistently profitable, you can plan growth. You might hire an assistant, buy a better machine, or open a second location. If your records show losses, you can act early: adjust prices, reduce costs, or change your product mix before the losses grow.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid When Tracking Expenses

The first mistake is starting with a system that is too complicated. A spreadsheet with twenty categories and complex formulas will discourage you. Start simple, with five to eight categories and a notebook or a basic sheet. You can add detail later.

The second mistake is recording sales but not expenses, or recording expenses but not sales. You need both to know your profit. Record sales daily, even if they are cash sales with no receipt. A simple daily sales total is enough to start.

The third mistake is forgetting cash expenses. Bank transfers leave a trail, but cash does not. Cash purchases in the market, transport fares, and small repairs are the easiest to forget. Make a habit of writing them down immediately.

The fourth mistake is not reviewing your records. Records that sit in a notebook unread do not help you. Set a weekly review time and keep it, even when you are busy. Thirty minutes a week can save you thousands of naira in waste.

The fifth mistake is giving up after a missed day. If you forget to record expenses for a day or two, do not abandon the system. Reconstruct what you can from bank alerts, receipts, and memory, and start again the next day. Consistency over months matters more than perfection on any single day.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Tracking Business Expenses

**Do I need an accountant to track expenses?**

No, not at first. A small business can track expenses with a notebook or spreadsheet. An accountant becomes useful when your business grows, when you need tax filings, or when you want a professional review of your finances. Even then, your own daily records make the accountant's work faster and cheaper.

**How much time does expense tracking take each day?**

About five to ten minutes if you record as you go, plus thirty minutes for a weekly review. That small investment saves you hours of confusion at the end of the month and helps you avoid costly mistakes.

## Final Thoughts

Tracking business expenses without an accountant is not about becoming a finance expert. It is about knowing where your money goes, so you can keep more of it. Separate your business money from your personal money, choose a simple method you will use daily, record every expense, review your numbers weekly, and use what you learn to price correctly and cut waste. Start today with a notebook and five minutes. In a month, you will know your business better than you ever have, and in six months you will make decisions with confidence because they are based on your real numbers, not on guesses.
