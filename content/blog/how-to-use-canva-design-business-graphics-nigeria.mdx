Tolu Adeyemi runs FreshPress Laundry on Allen Avenue in Ikeja. Wash and fold, wash and iron, duvets, suits, free pickup and delivery around Ikeja. His work is good — shirts come back crisp, duvets come back smelling fresh — but if you lined up his graphics on a table, you would never guess they belonged to the same business. His price list is green and yellow, made two years ago by a cousin. His Instagram posts are blue, because he liked a template. His business card is black and gold. A customer who sees his Instagram post and then gets his card wonders whether they are dealing with one laundry or three.

That is the problem this article solves, for one business from start to finish. One workflow, in Canva, that produces a matching set: a logo, a price list, an Instagram template, and a business card — all for FreshPress Laundry, all using the same colours, the same fonts, and the same logo in the same place. Follow it with your own business in place of Tolu's, and you will finish the day with a brand that looks like it was planned, because it was.

## Start with the kit, not the designs

The mistake is to open Canva and start designing a logo. The logo is not the first step. The first step is the kit: the small set of decisions that every design in the set will obey. Tolu and I sat down with a notebook before we touched Canva and wrote down five lines. Business name: FreshPress Laundry. Main colour: deep teal. Second colour: warm orange. Text colour: charcoal, nearly black but softer. Fonts: Archivo Black for headlines, Montserrat for everything else. Logo position: top left on every design, no exceptions.

That is the whole kit, and it fits on a sticky note. Canva Pro has a Brand Kit where you can save all of this in one place and pull it into every design with one tap. Tolu is on the free plan, so we did the free version: we saved the colour codes in his phone notes and made a one-page reference design called "FreshPress Brand Notes" with the colour blocks, the two font names, and the logo in the top left corner. Every new design starts by copying from it.

Picking the colours took longer than anything else, and that is normal. Teal and orange work for a laundry because they say two things at once: teal says clean and fresh, orange says warm and fast. They also sit far apart, so text in one colour reads clearly on a background of the other. Before we committed, we printed a small test strip at a business centre in Ikeja — teal, orange, charcoal, and white — for about ₦200, and all four printed true. Colours that only work indoors, in perfect light, are not brand colours. They are decorations.

## The logo: simple enough to survive being small

A logo in Canva is a starting point, and for a small business it is often all you need for the first year. The rule is that a logo has to survive being small. It will appear tiny in the corner of an Instagram post, smaller still as a WhatsApp display picture, and smaller again on a business card. If it has five colours, a detailed drawing, and a tagline in small text, it will turn into a smudge at that size.

Tolu's logo took twenty minutes. We started a blank square design, typed "FreshPress" in Archivo Black in teal, and put "LAUNDRY" underneath it in Montserrat, spaced out in orange, smaller and lighter. Next to the words, we placed a simple icon from Canva's free elements: a shirt on a hanger, drawn in a single line, in teal. No washing machine, no bubbles, no water drops, no tagline. The name does the talking, the icon does the remembering. We downloaded it as a PNG and saved it in the uploads folder so every future design could pull it in with one tap.

The test for a logo is stricter than the test for a flyer. Shrink it to thumbnail size and look at it from arm's length. If you can still read the name and recognise the icon, it passes. Tolu's first attempt had "Pickup & Delivery Available" curled under the icon in small script. At thumbnail size it looked like a stain. We deleted it. A logo is a name and a mark. The services belong on the price list, not in the logo.

## The price list: the hardest-working design you own

The price list is the single most useful graphic a business like FreshPress owns, because it answers the question customers ask on WhatsApp all day: how much? Before the redesign, Tolu typed prices into chat fifty times a week. After it, he sent one image and the conversation moved straight to booking.

We built it on an A4 portrait design, because a price list lives on WhatsApp and on a shop wall, and A4 prints cleanly on both. The logo went in the top left, exactly where the kit says it goes. Under it, a headline in Archivo Black: "FreshPress Price List." Then the list itself, in Montserrat, product on the left and price on the right. Wash & Fold, ₦1,200 per kg. Wash & Iron, ₦1,500 per kg. Shirt, laundered and pressed, ₦800. Trousers, ₦800. Suit, two-piece, ₦3,500. Duvet, single, ₦5,000. Duvet, double, ₦7,000. Curtains, from ₦2,500. At the bottom, a teal band: "Free pickup & delivery in Ikeja. Order on WhatsApp: 0803 456 7890."

Two things about that list matter more than the rest. The first is that every price is real and current, because a price list with old prices is worse than no price list at all. Tolu updates it the day a price changes, so nobody gets quoted a price from 2024. The second is that it fits on one page. When the list ran to two pages, customers stopped reading halfway down and messaged to ask about the things on page two. One page forces you to choose what matters, and choosing is the whole skill.

## The Instagram template: the same set, shrunk to a square

With the logo and the price list done, the Instagram template took ten minutes, because there was almost nothing left to decide. The colours were decided. The fonts were decided. The logo position was decided. All that was left was the layout of a square post, 1080 by 1080 pixels, that Tolu could reuse all year.

The template has three zones. The top zone is a photo — a real one, taken on Tolu's phone in the shop in the morning light: a stack of freshly folded shirts, a duvet hanging on the line, the ironing table mid-press. One photo, filling the top two-thirds of the square. The middle zone is a single line of text in Archivo Black, teal on white: "Fresh Shirts, Same Day." Or "Your Duvet, Back by Friday." One message, five or six words, never more. The bottom zone is a thin teal band with the logo on the left and the WhatsApp number on the right, in white, in Montserrat. Same band, same position, every single post.

The trick that makes the template a template is duplication. Tolu does not start a new design each week. He opens last week's post, duplicates it, swaps the photo, changes the one line of text, and downloads. Four minutes, and the new post looks like it belongs to the same business as the last twenty. After a month, customers recognised FreshPress posts before reading the name — one woman in Ogba knew a post was his by the teal band at the bottom. That is branding, and it cost nothing but consistency.

The template also covers the days when there is nothing new to announce. A folded shirt, a clean press, a van loaded for delivery — ordinary work, photographed honestly, with the same template around it — outperforms a fancy graphic with a stock photo every time. Customers trust a laundry that shows its own work. They distrust one that shows a photo of a laundry in another country.

## The business card: the set, in your customer's pocket

The business card is the last piece of the set, and the easiest, because by now every decision is already made. We started a business card design in Canva — the standard card size, 3.5 by 2 inches — and the front took shape in minutes. Teal background. The shirt-and-hanger icon in white, centred, large. "FreshPress Laundry" underneath in white Archivo Black. Nothing else. No list of services, no motto, no photo. A card front with one job: to be recognised in a wallet three weeks later.

The back is where the information lives, on a white background so the text stays readable. "Tolu Adeyemi, FreshPress Laundry." Then, in Montserrat, the services in one line: "Wash & Fold · Wash & Iron · Duvets · Suits." Then the WhatsApp number, big: 0803 456 7890. Then the shop address: 14 Allen Avenue, Ikeja. Then, small at the bottom: "Free pickup & delivery in Ikeja." Same colours, fonts, and logo position as every other piece — a customer who saw the Instagram post would know instantly it was the same business.

Tolu printed a hundred cards at a printer in Ikeja for ₦6,000, on thick matte paper, and he printed one test card first to check the teal. He keeps a stack by the counter and slips one into every delivery bag. A digital version of the same card — downloaded as a PNG from Canva — is his WhatsApp display picture on slow days and a status post whenever he wants to remind people the shop exists. One design, two jobs.

## Keeping the set matching after the first week

A matching set is easy to build once and hard to keep. The drift starts quietly. A new staff member designs a promo flyer in a hurry and uses a pink template because it looked nice. A price change gets typed into WhatsApp instead of onto the price list. A holiday post uses a different font because the template was on another phone. Six months later, the set is four different businesses again.

The fix is a habit, not a tool. Every new design starts by duplicating an old one, never by starting blank. The brand notes page in Canva is the first thing opened and the last thing closed. And once a month, he lines up his last four posts, his price list, and his card and asks: would a stranger say these belong to one business? If not, the fix is usually one thing — a colour, a font, a logo that moved — and five minutes puts it right.

That is the whole workflow. One afternoon, one business, one kit, four designs that match. Tolu still designs his own graphics, and he still does it for free in Canva. The difference is that now his price list, his Instagram posts, and his card all tell the same story: a clean laundry on Allen Avenue, with prices you can see, a number you can reach, and a look you can recognise before you read a word. Pick your two colours, pick your two fonts, and build the four pieces in one sitting.
