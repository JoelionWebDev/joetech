Every day, Nigerian cities produce mountains of waste: plastic bottles, cans, cardboard, scrap metal, and old electronics. Most of it ends up in dumpsites, drains, and waterways. But to a recycling entrepreneur, that same waste is raw material — free or cheap to collect, and saleable to factories that turn it into new products. Waste recycling is one of the few businesses in Nigeria where your raw material is literally lying on the ground, and the market for it keeps growing as more companies look for recycled inputs.

This guide explains how to start a waste recycling business in Nigeria, step by step, from choosing what to collect to finding buyers and making a profit. It is a hands-on, low-glamour business — but it is real, it pays, and it cleans up your city at the same time.

## 1. Understand the Business Model First

The recycling business has a simple logic: collect waste materials cheaply, sort and process them, and sell them to buyers at a higher price. Your profit is the difference between what you pay (or spend) to collect and process, and what the buyer pays you — minus your running costs.

There are three levels at which you can operate:

**Collector or aggregator.** You buy or collect recyclables from households, businesses, and waste pickers, sort them roughly, and sell them to a bigger buyer or a processing plant. This is the easiest entry point. You need a small yard, a weighing scale, and working capital to pay your suppliers.

**Processor.** You collect, sort, clean, and compress materials into bales or granules, then sell to factories. A baling machine for plastic bottles or a shredder for PET costs money, but processed material sells for significantly more per kilogram than loose waste.

**Manufacturer or upcycler.** You turn recycled material into finished products — for example, plastic pellets sold to manufacturers, or paving tiles made from recycled plastic. This is the most capital-intensive level and usually comes later, after you have mastered collection and processing.

## 2. Choose Your Materials Carefully

Not all waste is worth the same. Focus on materials with steady buyers and clear prices. Here are the main ones in Nigeria:

**PET plastic bottles.** The clear plastic bottles used for soft drinks and water are the most popular recyclable in Nigeria. They are everywhere, easy to identify, and bought by weight by recycling plants. Clean, sorted PET sells better than mixed, dirty bottles.

**HDPE plastics.** These are the thicker, opaque plastics used for detergent containers, jerry cans, and bottle caps. They fetch a good price per kilogram and are bought by many local processors.

**Aluminium cans and scrap metal.** Drink cans, old roofing sheets, iron rods, and other metals are heavy and valuable. Scrap metal dealers buy by weight, and prices for aluminium and copper are among the highest in the recycling trade. Copper from old cables is especially valuable — but be careful to buy only from legitimate sources, never from stolen cables or vandalised infrastructure.

**Cardboard and paper.** Cartons from shops, supermarkets, and warehouses are bought by paper mills. They are bulky and low-value per kilogram, so you need volume and cheap transport to make them pay.

Start with one or two materials — PET bottles and scrap metal are a good combination — and master them before adding others. Trying to collect everything at once spreads your money and your attention too thin.

## 3. Research the Market in Your Area

Before you spend money, spend time. Visit the dumpsites, markets, and industrial areas in your city and find out who is already in the recycling trade. In Lagos, for example, there are established aggregators in areas like Olusosun, Ojota, and parts of Ikorodu. In Abuja, Kano, Onitsha, and Port Harcourt, there are smaller networks of buyers and processors.

Talk to waste pickers — the men and women who collect bottles and scrap from streets and dumpsites. They are your future suppliers. Ask them what they collect, what they are paid per kilogram, and who buys from them. Talk to factory owners and recycling plants: ask what materials they need, in what quantities, and at what prices. Prices change with the market, so confirm current buying and selling prices in your area before you make any financial plan.

Also study the waste flow in your area. Estates, hotels, restaurants, supermarkets, event centres, and schools generate large quantities of plastic bottles and cardboard every week. A single busy hotel can produce dozens of kilograms of PET bottles weekly. Map out these sources — they are your most reliable suppliers, because their waste is cleaner and more consistent than what comes from a dumpsite.

## 4. Register the Business and Handle Compliance

Register a business name with the Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC). It is affordable, and it gives you credibility with the corporate clients — hotels, supermarkets, and factories — whose waste you want to collect. Open a business bank account and get a Tax Identification Number (TIN) from the Federal Inland Revenue Service.

If you plan to operate a yard or depot, check the zoning and environmental rules for the location. A recycling yard is a noisy, dusty operation, so residential areas are usually a poor choice. Industrial areas or the outskirts of town are better, and they are also cheaper. Ask your local government about any trade permit or environmental clearance you need before you sign a lease.

## 5. Find a Yard and Set It Up

Your yard is where materials are received, weighed, sorted, stored, and loaded for sale. You do not need a fancy building — a fenced plot of land with a shed or canopy is enough to start.

Choose a location that trucks can reach easily, with enough space to store baled material and to turn a vehicle around. If you are near your sources of waste — markets, estates, or industrial areas — your transport costs drop sharply. Rent for a suitable plot in the outskirts of Lagos or Abuja can range from ₦300,000 to ₦1,000,000 a year depending on size and location. In smaller cities, it will be less.

Set up the yard in zones: a receiving area with a weighing scale, a sorting area with tables or raised platforms, a storage area for sorted materials, and a loading area. Buy a reliable weighing scale — it is the most important piece of equipment in the business, because everything you buy and sell is priced by weight. A good platform scale costs roughly ₦150,000 to ₦400,000. Calibrate it regularly and keep it honest; a scale that cheats suppliers will destroy your reputation, and a scale that cheats you will destroy your profit.

You will also need sacks or big bags for storage, a hand trolley, and basic tools. If you plan to bale plastic, a manual or hydraulic baling machine costs roughly ₦800,000 to ₦3,500,000 depending on size and whether it is new or fairly used. You can start without one and add it later, once your volumes justify it.

## 6. Build Your Supply Network

A recycling business is only as good as its supply. You need a steady flow of material, and that means building relationships with many small suppliers rather than depending on one big source.

**Waste pickers.** These are the backbone of the informal recycling trade. There are thousands of them in every Nigerian city. Pay them fairly, weigh honestly, and pay cash on the spot. A picker who trusts you will bring their materials to you every day instead of to your competitor. Some aggregators give their regular pickers small advances or help with transport — that loyalty pays for itself many times over.

**Households and estates.** Offer residents a simple deal: separate your plastic bottles and cans, and we collect them weekly. Some estates will give you access for free because you are solving their waste problem. Provide them with large sacks and a regular collection day.

**Businesses and institutions.** Hotels, restaurants, supermarkets, schools, and event centres generate clean, high-volume waste. Approach the facility manager, explain that you will collect their recyclables regularly and keep their premises clean, and agree on a schedule. Some businesses will give you the material free because it saves them disposal costs; others will expect a small payment per kilogram. Either way, these are your best suppliers because the material is cleaner and the volumes are predictable.

Set your buying prices carefully. You need to pay enough to keep suppliers coming, but low enough to leave a margin after your processing and transport costs. Check the prices your competitors pay and stay competitive. And always weigh in front of the supplier — transparency builds trust.

## 7. Sort, Clean, and Process Properly

Sorting is where your money is made. Mixed waste sells for the lowest price; clean, well-sorted material sells for the highest. Train your workers to sort accurately: PET bottles separate from HDPE, clear PET separate from coloured PET, caps removed from bottles, labels stripped where buyers require it, aluminium separate from steel.

Baling compresses loose bottles into dense blocks that are cheaper to transport and easier for buyers to handle. A baled tonne of PET takes up far less space in a truck than the same weight in loose bottles, so your transport cost per kilogram falls. If you cannot afford a baler yet, you can still sell loose material — just accept that your transport costs will be higher and your price lower.

Store sorted material under cover and off the ground. Rain-soaked cardboard loses value, and PET left in the sun for months becomes brittle. Keep your yard tidy: buyers who visit a clean, organised yard trust the quality of what you sell.

## 8. Find Buyers and Negotiate Well

Your buyers are recycling plants, plastic manufacturers, paper mills, and metal foundries. Some are large factories that buy in tonnes; others are medium-sized processors who buy regularly in smaller quantities. Find them by asking other aggregators, searching online, and visiting industrial areas.

Before you commit to a buyer, check three things: their price per kilogram, their payment terms, and their reliability. A buyer who pays a high price but takes three weeks to pay can be worse than one who pays slightly less but pays on delivery or within a few days. Cash flow is everything in this business because you pay your suppliers daily in cash.

Start with two or three buyers for each material, not one. If your only buyer stops buying — because their machine broke down or the market dropped — your yard fills up and your money is trapped in stock. Having alternatives protects you.

Negotiate on volume and quality. A buyer who knows you can deliver five tonnes of clean PET every week will offer a better price than to someone who brings a few hundred kilograms now and then. Keep records of every sale: date, buyer, material, weight, price per kilogram, and total amount. These records are also useful when you apply for a loan or a grant later.

## 9. Work Out Your Startup Costs

Here is a realistic budget for a small recycling operation:

- **Yard rent:** ₦300,000 to ₦1,000,000 per year, depending on location and size.
- **Weighing scale:** ₦150,000 to ₦400,000 for a reliable platform scale.
- **Working capital for buying waste:** ₦500,000 to ₦1,500,000 to start. You will recycle this money constantly — buy waste, sell it, use the proceeds to buy more.
- **Basic equipment:** Sacks, trolleys, sorting tables, protective gear, and a small shed: ₦150,000 to ₦400,000.
- **Transport:** A fairly used truck or tricycle (keke) for collection. A keke costs roughly ₦800,000 to ₦1,500,000; a small truck costs more. Many beginners start by hiring transport per trip.
- **Baling machine (optional at start):** ₦800,000 to ₦3,500,000.
- **Running costs:** Wages for two to four workers, fuel, water, and phone: roughly ₦200,000 to ₦500,000 a month.

A lean start is possible with roughly ₦1,500,000 to ₦3,000,000 if you skip the baler and the truck and focus on aggregation. A more complete setup with a baler and your own transport is realistically ₦4,000,000 to ₦8,000,000. Start lean, prove the model, and reinvest your profits into equipment.

## 10. Run the Daily Operation

A recycling yard runs on a simple daily rhythm. In the morning, receive materials from pickers and collection runs, weigh each delivery in front of the supplier, record it, and pay. During the day, workers sort and clean the material while you take calls from business clients and plan the next collection run. In the afternoon, load sold material onto the buyer's truck and confirm the weight and payment. In the evening, count the stock, reconcile the cash, and record the day's figures.

Keep two records that you never skip: a buying ledger (who supplied what, the weight, and the price paid) and a sales ledger (who bought what, the weight, and the price received). If your buying and selling prices are right and your records are honest, your profit will show up clearly. If the numbers do not add up, something is wrong — theft, a faulty scale, or a pricing mistake — and you need to find it quickly.

Weigh everything twice when the amounts are large: once when it arrives and once when it leaves. Keep your scale calibrated and your yard secure. Theft in a recycling yard is usually small amounts taken often, so daily stock counts and a locked gate matter more than cameras.

## 11. Hire and Manage Your Team

You will need sorters, a storekeeper or yard supervisor, and possibly a driver. Sorters are usually paid daily or weekly; in many Nigerian cities, a sorter earns roughly ₦3,000 to ₦5,000 a day depending on the area and the work. Pay on time, every time — workers who are paid late will not sort carefully, and careless sorting costs you money at the buyer's gate.

Provide protective gear and insist on its use. Gloves and boots protect against cuts from metal and broken glass; masks protect against dust. This is both a safety duty and a business decision: an injured worker is a cost, and a yard known for treating workers well attracts the best pickers and sorters.

## 12. Common Problems and How to Handle Them

**Price fluctuations.** The prices of recyclables rise and fall with the global price of virgin plastic, oil, and metals. When prices fall, your margin shrinks. Protect yourself by not holding too much stock when prices are high and falling, and by keeping your buying prices in line with what buyers are actually paying you.

**Dirty or mixed material.** Buyers reject or discount loads that are poorly sorted or contaminated. The fix is training and supervision, not arguing with the buyer.

**Competition for supply.** Other aggregators will try to win your pickers with higher prices. You cannot always match the highest price, but you can win on reliability: pay on time, weigh honestly, and treat people well. Most pickers value a steady, honest buyer over a slightly higher price from someone unreliable.

**Health and safety incidents.** Cuts, infections, and accidents happen in this trade. Keep a first-aid kit, insist on protective gear, and keep the yard clean and organised.

## 13. Growing the Business

Once your yard runs profitably, there are several ways to grow. Add a baling machine to increase your selling price and cut transport costs. Add new materials — start with PET, then add HDPE, cardboard, and metals as your team learns them. Open a second collection point in another part of the city, managed by a supervisor you trust. Or move up the value chain: wash and shred PET into flakes, which sell for more than baled bottles.

You can also build a more formal collection service for estates and companies, with branded sacks, scheduled pickups, and monthly reports. Corporate clients like this because it helps them show they are environmentally responsible, and they will pay for a reliable service.

## Conclusion

Waste recycling is not glamorous, but it is honest, necessary work with a real market. The raw material is abundant, the buyers exist, and the business rewards the operator who is organised, fair to suppliers, and disciplined about quality. Start small with one or two materials, learn the prices, build your supply network, and reinvest your profits into equipment that raises your margins.

Do it well, and you will build a business that pays you while making your city cleaner — and that is a combination few businesses can offer.
