Most Nigerian small business owners do not start Monday with a plan. They start with a rush: the phone is ringing, a customer wants a price, a rider is waiting, and a supplier has not replied. By Wednesday, you are reacting to whatever is loudest. By Friday, you are tired, and you cannot say what the week achieved. The business was busy, but was it moving forward?

A one-hour Monday planning session solves that. It will not make the week perfect, but it answers three questions before the noise starts: what matters most, what money is coming in and going out, and who needs attention. Then interruptions are easier to handle, because you can tell what is urgent from what can wait.

This guide is for a real small business in Nigeria: a shop in Lekki, a fashion brand on Instagram, a food vendor, a hair salon, or a printing business. You need no expensive software or big team — just one hour, a notebook or a phone, and the discipline to protect that hour every Monday.

## Why One Hour on Monday Is Enough

Many owners avoid planning because they imagine it takes a whole day. It does not. A weekly plan is not a business plan for a bank. You are simply deciding what to do this week, in what order, with the money and stock you already have.

Monday sets the tone for the week. A new request on Tuesday can be checked against your plan instead of pushing everything aside. A customer asking when an order will be ready gets a real answer, because you have already looked at deliveries. Money getting tight on Thursday is no surprise, because you checked your cash on Monday. Without a plan, your time belongs to whoever shouts loudest. With one, you choose your three priorities first, then fit everything else around them.

Pick a fixed time and protect it. Monday 8:00 to 9:00 works well, before customers arrive and deliveries start. If your shop opens at 8:00, plan from 7:00 to 8:00, or plan on Sunday evening and review it on Monday morning. Tell your staff, if you have any, that the hour is not to be interrupted except for a genuine emergency.

## Before You Start: Set Up Your Planning Spot

Gather four things before Monday. First, your sales record from last week: notebook, POS report, bank alerts, or WhatsApp messages. Second, your money position: cash, bank balance, what you owe, and what you are owed. Third, your stock list: what is left, what is running low, and which orders are pending. Fourth, your calendar: appointments, deliveries, and market days.

Keep a notebook for your weekly plan, or use the notes app on your phone. What matters is that last week's plan sits next to this week's, so you can compare them. Put your phone on silent for the hour. One hour without replying will not lose you a customer, but one hour of clear thinking can save you a week of confusion. Use the same template every week, so the thinking gets faster.

## Minutes 0 to 10: Review Last Week Honestly

Look back before you look forward. The key word is honestly. Do not write what you hoped happened. Write what actually happened. A plan built on wishes fails by Tuesday. A plan built on facts holds.

Ask five questions. What did I sell, and how much came in? What were my biggest expenses? Which orders did I finish, and which are pending? What went well, and what went wrong? What should change this week? Write short answers. For example: "Sold 42 items, ₦185,000 in sales. Spent ₦60,000 on stock. Two orders pending: Mrs Ade's dress and the church printing job. Best seller was the Ankara gown. Lost one customer by replying late. Lesson: reply within one hour."

That short review shows whether last week made or lost money, which products actually sell, and whether a problem is repeating. If the same issue appears two weeks in a row, it is a pattern, not bad luck, and this week's plan should fix it. Spend no more than ten minutes here.

## Minutes 10 to 20: Check Your Money

Next, check your money. This is the part many owners skip, which is exactly why it matters. A business can be busy and still run out of cash, when money comes in slowly but goes out quickly, or when you buy stock before collecting payment from customers. A Monday money check prevents that surprise.

Write down four numbers: your cash and bank balance; how much customers owe you, and when they promised to pay; how much you owe suppliers, rent, or staff, and when it is due; and what you expect to spend this week on stock, transport, data, power, and running costs. Then ask: can I pay what I owe this week with the money I have and expect to receive? If not, act now — chase an overdue payment, delay a stock purchase, or choose a smaller order. A calm decision on Monday beats a panic on Thursday.

Also decide how much you will pay yourself this week, and write it down. Many owners take money from the business whenever they need it, without recording it, then wonder why the business never grows.

## Minutes 20 to 30: Choose Your Three Priorities

Now choose your three priorities for the week. Not ten. Three. If everything is a priority, nothing is, and you will end the week having finished nothing important.

A priority is something that will make a real difference if you finish it this week: a large customer order, a new product launch, restocking your best seller, or collecting payment from a big debtor. It must be specific enough that you will know on Friday whether you did it. "Grow my business" is not a priority. "Deliver the 20 Ankara gowns to Mrs Ade by Thursday" is. "Post more on Instagram" is not a priority. "Post one product photo and one customer review every day, Monday to Friday" is.

Pick your three from your review and money check. What is overdue? What will bring in money? What is a customer waiting for? Write them in order, and for each one, write the first small step you will take on Monday and the day you will finish it. A priority without a first step is a wish. Do not fill the three slots with routine work, such as answering messages. Your priorities are the important work that gets pushed aside when the day gets busy, unless you protect it.

## Minutes 30 to 40: Plan Your Content and Marketing

Customers in Nigeria today usually find you online before they find you in person. Your photos and posts on Instagram, WhatsApp, Facebook, or TikTok are how people discover you and decide to buy. Yet content is usually the first thing dropped when the week gets busy. Planning it on Monday stops that.

Decide what you will post this week. You do not need to create it all now, just decide the topics. Use a simple pattern: one product post, one customer story or review, one useful tip or behind-the-scenes post, and one offer or reminder. Write the topic and the day next to each one — for example, Monday: photo of the new Ankara gown. Wednesday: video of how you package orders. Friday: a customer review, with permission. Saturday: a weekend offer, such as free delivery within Lekki above a set amount. When the topic is decided, making the post takes minutes.

Also plan one marketing action beyond posting: a broadcast message to WhatsApp customers, a follow-up with three old customers, or asking a happy customer for a review. One small outreach action a week brings more repeat business than a dozen random posts.

## Minutes 40 to 50: Sort Stock, Deliveries, and Daily Operations

Now handle the practical side: stock, deliveries, and jobs that must happen on specific days. A plan that ignores stock and deliveries is a plan that cannot be delivered.

First, check your stock. What is running low? What has run out? Write what you need to order, from whom, and by when. If you need to visit Balogun, Onitsha, or Mile 12, decide which day you will go and how much you will spend. The most common reason a small business loses a sale is simple: the customer wanted something out of stock.

Second, list every delivery and appointment. Which orders go out on which day? Who collects, and who needs a rider? Does any job depend on someone else — a tailor, a printer, a supplier? Write the day and time next to each one, then check for clashes. Three deliveries due on Thursday with only one rider is a problem you can fix on Monday by moving one to Wednesday or Friday. Discovered on Thursday, it is a crisis.

## Minutes 50 to 60: Follow-Ups and the One-Page Plan

The last ten minutes are for people: customers who are waiting, customers who owe you money, and customers you have not heard from in a while. Follow-up is one of the most profitable habits in a small business, and one of the most neglected.

Make a short list. Customers with pending orders: message them on Monday to confirm the delivery day, so they know you have not forgotten them. Customers who owe you money: send a polite reminder with the amount and the date they promised. Old customers you have not served in a while: send a friendly message, not a sales pitch, just to check in. Five follow-up messages on Monday can bring in more business than a whole day of advertising.

Then write your one-page plan: your three priorities, your money position in one line, your stock orders, delivery days, content plan, and follow-up list. If it does not fit on one page, it is too long. Stick it where you can see it and look at it every morning, crossing things off as you finish them. End the hour with one small action: send the first follow-up message, place the urgent stock order, or take the first product photo.

## A Simple Template You Can Copy

Copy this template into your notebook or phone and use the same one every week.

**My Week: Monday [date] to Saturday [date]**

**Last week in one line:** Sales, money in, money out, what went well, what went wrong.

**Money this week:** Cash and bank balance now. Money owed to me. Money I owe. Expected spending.

**My three priorities:** 1. [Priority, first step, finish by which day] 2. [Priority, first step, finish by which day] 3. [Priority, first step, finish by which day]

**Content this week:** Monday — [topic]. Wednesday — [topic]. Friday — [topic]. Saturday — [topic].

**Stock to order:** [Item, supplier, amount, day to order].

**Deliveries and appointments:** Monday — [what]. Tuesday — [what]. Wednesday — [what]. Thursday — [what]. Friday — [what]. Saturday — [what].

**Follow-ups:** [Customer name, what they need, message sent?]

**First action after planning:** [The one thing I will do immediately.]

A complicated template will be abandoned by the third week. A simple one will still be in use in six months.

## Sticking to the Plan When Lagos Life Interrupts

A plan is only useful if you can stick to it, and in Lagos that is a real challenge. Traffic can turn a one-hour trip into three, and a supplier can disappoint you. The goal is not to follow the plan perfectly, but to return to it after the interruption.

Expect interruptions, so leave space in the plan. Do not fill every hour from Monday to Saturday. Leave at least one free slot each day, or one free morning in the week, for the unexpected. If nothing interrupts you, use that time to get ahead or to rest. If something does, you have room to handle it without breaking the whole plan.

Protect your three priorities. When an interruption happens, ask: does this change my priorities, or just my timing? Most interruptions only change timing. Only change a priority for something genuinely more important. Finally, review the plan for five minutes every evening: what did I finish today, what moves to tomorrow, and what needs to change? Without that check, Monday's plan is forgotten by Wednesday. If a whole week goes badly, do not throw the habit away. Note what happened and start again next Monday.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

The first mistake is planning too much. A plan with fifteen tasks for Monday is a wish list. Keep the list short. A short plan that gets finished beats a long plan that gets abandoned.

The second mistake is planning without looking at money. A week planned without a money check is planned in the dark. You might order stock you cannot afford, or spend on marketing while a supplier payment is overdue. Ten minutes on money every Monday prevents that.

The third mistake is keeping the plan in your head. Thoughts disappear the moment a customer calls. Write it down, on paper or on your phone, where you can see it. Writing also forces you to be specific: "I will post the Ankara gown photo on Monday at 10am" is easy to do.

The fourth mistake is planning once and never reviewing. Check it every morning and evening. The fifth mistake is copying someone else's routine exactly. Take the structure — review, money, three priorities, content, stock, follow-ups — and adjust the details to fit your business.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What if Monday is my busiest day and I cannot spare an hour?

Plan on Sunday evening instead, or early on Monday before you open. It has to be at the same time every week, before the week's work begins. Spend five minutes on Monday morning reviewing the plan. The habit matters more than the hour.

### Do I really need to write the plan down, or can I keep it in my head?

Write it down. A written plan is easier to check, easier to share with staff, and much harder to forget. A page in a notebook, a note on your phone, or a photo of a whiteboard all work.

### What should I do if I finish my three priorities by Wednesday?

First, enjoy it — it means the planning is working. Then choose the next most important task, or use the extra time for neglected work, such as updating your prices or cleaning your stock records.

### How do I plan when my income changes every week and I never know what will come in?

That uncertainty is exactly why the Monday money check matters. Plan around what you know: the cash you have now, the payments you are owed, and the costs you must pay. Keep spending flexible — order stock in smaller amounts and delay non-urgent spending. Over time, your weekly reviews will show patterns, such as which days are busiest.

### What if I miss a Monday? Should I skip planning that week?

No. Plan on Tuesday, even if it is only for thirty minutes. A late plan is far better than no plan. Shorten the routine — five minutes on last week, five minutes on money, ten minutes on priorities and deliveries — and get on with the week. Then return to the full hour next Monday.

## What to Do Next

First, choose your planning hour for next Monday and write it in your calendar now — for example, Monday 8:00 to 9:00, before you open your shop. Tell your staff, if you have any, that this hour is protected, and gather your sales record, money position, stock list, and calendar.

Second, copy the template from this guide into your notebook or phone today, and fill in what you already know about this week — your pending orders, deliveries, and follow-ups. Preparing it now makes Monday's hour faster and easier.

Third, run the full one-hour routine next Monday, and again the Monday after that. After four weeks, look back at your four one-page plans and ask what changed: did you finish more work, run out of stock less often, collect money faster, or feel less rushed? If yes, keep the habit. That one hour, protected every week, is one of the cheapest ways to grow a small business in Nigeria — one planned week at a time.
