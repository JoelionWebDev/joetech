Every day in Nigeria, thousands of parcels need to move: food from restaurants, clothes from online shops, documents from offices, medicines from pharmacies, and goods from small businesses to customers. The rise of Instagram shops, WhatsApp sellers and online stores has made delivery a daily necessity — and that is why a dispatch rider business is one of the most practical businesses you can start in Nigeria today.

You do not need a university degree or a large office. You need a reliable bike, a phone, knowledge of your city, and a reputation for delivering on time and intact. Start with one bike and one rider — even if that rider is you — and grow into a fleet that serves businesses across your city.

This guide covers the full journey: understanding the market, startup costs, choosing the right bike, registering your business, finding clients, setting prices, and growing safely.

## Understand the Delivery Market in Nigeria

Delivery demand in Nigeria has grown fast in recent years, and it keeps growing. Online shopping, food delivery, pharmacy deliveries and business-to-business courier work all need riders.

### Who needs dispatch riders?

Your clients will come from many places:

- **Instagram and WhatsApp vendors** — fashion sellers, food vendors, skincare brands and gadget sellers who need daily deliveries to customers. These are your bread and butter: they order again and again.
- **Restaurants and food businesses** — they need riders for lunch and dinner rushes, especially on weekends.
- **Pharmacies and medical suppliers** — they deliver medicines to homes and hospitals and value reliability above all.
- **Offices and corporate clients** — documents, contracts and parcels that must arrive the same day.
- **E-commerce and logistics companies** — larger firms sometimes outsource last-mile delivery to small dispatch companies.
- **Individual customers** — people sending parcels to family and friends across town.

### The opportunity for small operators

Big logistics companies serve the whole country, but they are often slow, expensive or impersonal for small local deliveries. A small dispatch business wins on speed, personal service and local knowledge. A vendor in Lekki would rather call a rider she knows by name — one who picks up in thirty minutes and confirms delivery with a photo — than wait for a large company. Your advantage is trust and speed, not size.

### Know your city like a professional

Delivery is a local knowledge business. You must know the shortcuts, the traffic patterns, the one-way streets and the estates that confuse newcomers. In Lagos, knowing the difference between Lekki Phase 1 and Chevron, or the fastest route from Ikeja to Surulere at 5pm, is a real competitive advantage. Spend your first weeks learning your area thoroughly, and keep a simple map or notes of estates, landmarks and difficult addresses.

## Work Out Your Startup Costs

A dispatch business can start small, but you must budget honestly — a bike that breaks down in week two will cost you clients.

### A realistic budget for one bike

- **A good motorcycle:** ₦700,000 to ₦1,500,000 for a reliable new or fairly used bike. Popular choices include durable, fuel-efficient models with good parts availability. A cheap bike with no spare parts in the market will spend more time at the mechanic than on the road.
- **Delivery box:** ₦60,000 to ₦150,000 for a sturdy, lockable, branded box. For food delivery, an insulated box keeps food warm.
- **Rider's licence and registration:** ₦50,000 to ₦120,000, depending on your state. This includes the rider's permit, vehicle papers and plate number.
- **Branded wear and safety gear:** ₦40,000 to ₦80,000 for helmets, reflective jackets and rain gear. Safety gear is not optional — it protects your rider and it makes your business look professional.
- **Phone and data:** ₦80,000 to ₦150,000 for a reliable smartphone if you do not already have one, plus monthly data.
- **Fuel and maintenance reserve:** ₦100,000 to ₦200,000 for your first month of fuel, servicing and minor repairs.
- **Branding (stickers, banner, flyers):** ₦30,000 to ₦60,000.

Altogether, starting with one bike costs roughly ₦1,000,000 to ₦2,000,000. If you already own a bike and a phone, you can start with far less — sometimes under ₦300,000 for papers, a delivery box and branding.

### Choose between a bike and a van

Bikes are cheaper, faster in traffic and easier to park, which makes them ideal for small parcels, documents and food. Vans and small cars carry bigger loads and are better for bulk deliveries, furniture and large orders. Most dispatch businesses start with bikes and add a van later, when clients start asking for bigger deliveries. Do not buy a van before you have the clients to fill it.

### Keep a maintenance fund

Set aside money every week for servicing, tyres, brake pads and oil changes. A bike that is serviced regularly rarely breaks down; a bike that is ignored will fail at the worst moment — usually while carrying a customer's urgent parcel. Treat maintenance as a business cost, not an afterthought.

## Register Your Business and Get the Right Papers

Running a delivery business without proper papers is risky. The authorities can impound your bike, and serious corporate clients will not work with an unregistered operator.

### Register with the Corporate Affairs Commission

Register your business name with the CAC. It is affordable, it protects your brand, and it lets you open a business bank account. Corporate clients and bigger vendors will ask for your CAC certificate before signing a delivery contract.

### Get the right licences

Requirements vary by state, but you will generally need a rider's permit, valid vehicle papers, a plate number and insurance. In Lagos, dispatch operators should also check the requirements of the state transport authorities and any local government permits for commercial motorcycles in your area. Ask other dispatch operators in your city what papers they hold — local experience is the best guide.

### Insure your bike and your parcels

Third-party insurance is the minimum. If you can afford it, comprehensive cover protects you when a bike is stolen or damaged. Also consider a simple goods-in-transit arrangement for high-value parcels, and be clear with clients about your liability if a parcel is lost or damaged. Put your terms in writing — a short document or a WhatsApp message that clients see before they book.

## Build a Simple, Reliable System

Customers choose a dispatch service they can trust. Trust comes from a system that works the same way every time.

### Set up your booking channel

WhatsApp Business is the simplest booking system for a small dispatch business. Set up your profile with your business name, your service areas, your prices and your opening hours. Customers send a message with pickup and delivery addresses, you confirm the price and the pickup time, and you send a confirmation when the parcel is delivered. Keep a simple log — a notebook or a phone spreadsheet — of every delivery: date, client, pickup, destination, price and status. That log is your business record.

### Use Google Maps and a second phone number

A rider who uses Google Maps and knows the area will deliver faster and get lost less. Keep your business phone separate from your personal phone if you can, so that customers always reach you and your personal calls do not interrupt business.

### Confirm every delivery

Always confirm delivery with the customer: a short message, a photo of the parcel at the door, or a signature on a delivery note. Proof of delivery protects you from disputes and builds trust with vendors who cannot be there in person. Many vendors will choose you over a cheaper rider simply because you always confirm.

### Handle parcels with care

Food, fragile items and documents need different handling. Keep food upright, protect fragile items with padding, and keep documents dry in a waterproof bag. A rider who damages parcels will lose clients faster than a rider who is slow. Train every rider you hire on how to handle different types of goods.

## Find Clients and Keep Them

Your first clients are the hardest to get. After that, good service brings referrals.

### Target online vendors first

Instagram and WhatsApp vendors are your best early clients because they need deliveries every day. Search for vendors in your area, introduce yourself politely, and offer a trial: deliver their first few orders at a fair price and on time. Many vendors are frustrated with unreliable riders and will switch quickly if you prove yourself. Join vendor groups on WhatsApp and Facebook, but do not spam them — introduce yourself once, share your service clearly, and let your work speak.

### Approach restaurants and pharmacies

Visit restaurants, bakeries and pharmacies in your area with a simple flyer and your price list. Offer to handle their deliveries during their busy hours. Some restaurants will give you regular daily work if you are reliable. Pharmacies value discretion and care — a rider who handles medicine deliveries professionally can become their permanent partner.

### Offer corporate delivery contracts

Offices, law firms, banks and corporate organisations often need same-day document delivery. Approach them with a simple proposal: your rates, your coverage area, your delivery times and your proof-of-delivery system. A corporate client who signs a monthly arrangement gives you predictable income, and one good corporate client can keep one rider busy all week.

### Use social media wisely

Post on Instagram and Facebook: your branded bike, your delivery box, testimonials from happy vendors, and short videos of your riders at work. Use your location in your posts so that people in your area can find you. Encourage your vendor clients to tag you when they post about fast delivery — their followers are your future clients.

### Reward loyalty

Offer regular clients a better rate for volume: for example, a vendor who sends ten parcels a day pays a slightly lower rate per delivery than a one-off customer. Offer a free delivery after a certain number of orders. Small rewards keep vendors from shopping around.

## Price Your Deliveries for Profit

Pricing is where many dispatch businesses fail. Charge too little and you cannot maintain your bike; charge too much and vendors will look elsewhere.

### Know your costs per trip

Work out your real cost for each delivery: fuel, bike wear and tear, your rider's pay, data and phone costs, and a share of your insurance and branding. A delivery that costs you ₦1,200 in fuel and wear cannot be priced at ₦1,000, no matter how much you want the client.

### Common pricing models

- **Distance-based pricing:** a base fee for deliveries within your area, plus a charge per kilometre beyond it. This is fair and easy to explain.
- **Zone pricing:** divide your city into zones — for example, deliveries within Lekki cost one price, deliveries to Ikeja cost another. Zone pricing is simple for customers to understand.
- **Flat rate for regular clients:** a vendor who sends many parcels daily can pay a fixed daily or weekly rate.
- **Urgent and express fees:** same-hour or express deliveries cost more, because they disrupt your schedule. Charge for urgency.

In Lagos, a typical short-distance bike delivery costs between ₦2,000 and ₦5,000 depending on distance, while longer cross-city deliveries cost more. Check what other dispatch riders in your area charge, then set your prices to cover your costs with a healthy margin. Never compete by being the cheapest — compete by being the most reliable.

### Collect payment properly

Take payment before or immediately after delivery, and accept transfers, POS and cash. For regular vendors, a weekly settlement works well: you deliver all week and they pay every Friday. Keep records of every payment, and never let a client owe you for more than a week without a reminder. Unpaid deliveries are lost income.

## Hire and Manage Riders

When demand grows beyond what one rider can handle, you will need to hire. Hiring well is the difference between a business and a headache.

### What to look for in a rider

A good rider is honest, careful, punctual and polite. Riding skill matters, but character matters more — your rider carries your customers' goods and represents your brand. Check references, verify their licence, and test their knowledge of the area before hiring.

### Pay fairly and clearly

Agree on pay before work starts: a daily wage plus commission per delivery is a common arrangement, or a monthly salary with a delivery bonus. Be clear about fuel: who pays for it, and how it is accounted for. Riders who are paid fairly and on time stay loyal and treat parcels with care. Riders who feel cheated will cut corners.

### Train every rider

Teach your riders your system: how to confirm pickup, how to call the customer, how to handle different parcels, how to confirm delivery, and how to behave politely at all times. Give each rider a branded jacket and helmet — it builds pride and makes your business recognisable on the road.

### Track your riders

Use a simple system to know where your riders are: regular WhatsApp check-ins, shared live locations during deliveries, or a basic tracking app. Clients will call you asking where their parcel is, and you should be able to answer. Knowing your riders' locations also protects you if a bike is stolen or a rider has an accident.

## Manage Money and Grow Your Fleet

### Track every delivery and every naira

Record your income and expenses daily. Know how much each bike earns per day, how much fuel each bike uses, and how much you spend on maintenance. A bike that earns ₦15,000 a day but costs ₦12,000 to run is barely profitable — you need to know that number before you add a second bike.

### Grow one bike at a time

Add a new bike only when your existing bike is fully busy most days and you are turning down deliveries. Each new bike should pay for itself: its fuel, its rider's pay, its maintenance and a share of your overheads, with profit left over. Growing too fast with loans is how many dispatch businesses collapse. Grow from profit, not from debt.

### Consider a dispatch app or booking form

When you have several riders, a simple booking form on Google Forms or a WhatsApp-based system may no longer be enough. There are affordable dispatch management tools that let customers book online and let you assign jobs to riders. You do not need this on day one, but plan for it as you grow.

### Expand your services

Once your delivery business is stable, consider adding related services: bulk delivery for supermarkets, errand services, pickup and drop-off for dry cleaners, or a small warehouse for vendors who need storage. Each new service should serve your existing clients first — they are the easiest people to sell to.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Buying a cheap, unreliable bike** — breakdowns cost you clients and repair money.
- **Operating without papers** — one impounded bike can wipe out a week's profit.
- **Pricing below cost** — you cannot build a business on deliveries that lose money.
- **No proof of delivery** — disputes over "I never received it" will eat your time and money.
- **Hiring without checks** — a dishonest rider can steal parcels and destroy your reputation.
- **Ignoring maintenance** — small problems become expensive breakdowns.
- **No records** — if you do not track deliveries and payments, you will not know if you are making money.
- **Taking every job** — deliveries far outside your area waste fuel and time; know your coverage and stick to it.

## Final Thoughts

A dispatch rider and delivery business in Nigeria is a practical way to earn from the growth of online selling. The demand is already there — vendors, restaurants, pharmacies and offices need reliable riders every day. Your job is to be the rider they can count on: on time, careful with parcels, easy to reach, and honest about prices.

Start with one reliable bike, learn your area, win your first ten vendor clients with excellent service, and keep your records straight. From there, growth is a matter of adding riders one at a time and never letting your standards drop. The road is busy — and for a good dispatch business, that is exactly the point.
