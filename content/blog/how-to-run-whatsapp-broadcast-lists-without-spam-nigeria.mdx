Tunde sells sneakers from a shop in Ikeja and a broadcast list of 412 contacts. Every Tuesday and Friday he sends a photo of new stock, a price, and "Reply YES to order." Every Tuesday and Friday his phone buzzes a few times, he sells a pair or two, and he tells himself the broadcasts are working. Then one quiet week he sat down and asked a question he had never asked in two years of sending: of the 412 people on his list, how many had actually bought anything because of a broadcast in the last three months? He went through his chats, counted, and the answer was eleven. Eleven people out of 412. The rest had either never replied, or had replied once to ask a price and vanished. He had been shouting into a room where almost nobody was listening, and calling it marketing.

Tunde's list is not unusual. Most Nigerian sellers build a broadcast list, send messages faithfully, and never measure anything beyond a feeling. A busy phone feels like success. A quiet phone feels like failure. Both feelings are unreliable.

## Count replies, not views

WhatsApp does not show you who read a broadcast. The blue ticks tell you almost nothing, because they mix together people who read carefully, people who swiped past, and people whose phones downloaded the message while they were asleep. So stop watching ticks. Watch replies.

The first number that matters is the reply rate. Take one broadcast, count how many people it was sent to, and count how many replied within 24 hours, even with a single word. Divide replies by people sent, and multiply by a hundred. If Tunde sends to 200 people and 24 reply, his reply rate is 12%. That is a healthy number for a Nigerian seller. Between 8% and 15% means the list is alive and the message was relevant. Below 5% means something is wrong: the list is stale, the message was wrong for the people who got it, or it went out at a time when nobody was looking. Above 20% means the message hit exactly the right people, and you should send more like it.

Replies and orders tell you different things, so count them separately: replies show whether people are listening, orders show whether listening turns into money.

## Trace every order back to the broadcast that caused it

An order counts as a broadcast order if the customer saw a broadcast and then bought within a week. You will not always know, so ask. When a customer orders on WhatsApp, add one short question to your normal reply: "Did you see this on my broadcast, or somewhere else?" Most people will answer. Write it down. Over a month, the pattern becomes obvious.

There is a neater way to trace orders, and it costs nothing. Give each broadcast a code word. Tuesday's broadcast ends with "Reply SNEAKERS to order." Friday's ends with "Reply FRIDAY to order." The customer who replies with the code word is telling you exactly which broadcast brought her. A fashion seller in Lekki uses a different code word every week, and by the end of the month she knows that her Ankara broadcasts bring three times as many orders as her shoe broadcasts, even though the shoe broadcasts get more replies. She now sends Ankara twice a week and shoes once, and her sales went up without her list growing at all.

The third number is the naira value. Add up what broadcast orders were worth in a week. If Tunde's two broadcasts brought six orders worth ₦168,000 in a week, that is real money, and it tells him the list earns its keep. If the same two broadcasts brought one order worth ₦18,000, the list is a hobby, not a sales channel, and he should spend his time fixing the list instead of writing more messages. Compare the weekly total against the time the broadcasts take you. If you spend four hours a week on broadcasts and they bring ₦20,000 in orders, you are paying yourself ₦5,000 an hour before costs. If they bring ₦150,000, the same four hours are well spent.

## A tracking sheet that takes five minutes a week

You do not need software. A notebook, or one sheet in Google Sheets, is enough. Make seven columns: date, which list you sent to, what the message was about, how many people received it, how many replied in 24 hours, how many ordered within a week, and the total naira value of those orders. Fill it in every time you send a broadcast, and fill in the orders column at the end of the week.

After four weeks, read the sheet like a story. Which list replies most? Tunde's sheet showed that his "Lekki regulars" list of 80 people replied at 18%, while his "everyone" list of 240 people replied at 3%. The big list was nearly dead, and the small list was doing all the work. Which message type orders best? His new-arrival photos ordered twice as well as his discount messages, because his customers wanted the new stock before it sold out, not a small cut on old stock. Which day works? Friday evening beat Tuesday morning by a wide margin, because people were planning their weekend. None of this was a guess. It was four weeks of counting.

## Cleaning the list: the quiet work that doubles your reply rate

A broadcast list gets worse with age unless you clean it. People change numbers. People lose interest. People save your number, buy once, and never want another message, but they never say so. They just stop reading. Every one of those people drags your reply rate down and raises the chance that WhatsApp sees your number as a sender people ignore.

Once a month, on the first Saturday, do a clean. Open your sheet and look at the last 90 days. Anyone who has not replied to anything, bought anything, or even asked a question in 90 days goes on a shortlist. Check their number first: does it still show a profile picture? Do messages show delivered ticks? A number that no longer shows any picture and never shows delivery is probably dead, changed, or blocked. Remove those without a second thought. The rest of the shortlist, the people who are real but silent, get one chance, in the form of a warm message.

## One warm message to the dormant, then stop

Write to the dormant contacts once, personally, and make it about them, not about your stock. "Good afternoon, Mrs. Balogun. It has been a while since we spoke. I hope you are well. I am clearing my broadcast list this week and I wanted to check — would you still like to receive my updates on new arrivals? Reply YES if you would, and I will keep you on. No worries at all if not." That is the whole message. No photo, no price, no offer, no guilt.

The replies will sort the list for you. The people who reply YES were not dead, they were busy, or your messages had been arriving at the wrong time. Keep them. The people who reply NO, or "please remove me," should be removed that same day, with a polite "Thank you, done." The people who say nothing at all have given you their answer by silence. Remove them after a week. Do not send a second message.

Tunde ran this exercise on his list of 412. He removed 61 dead numbers, sent the warm message to 148 dormant contacts, kept the 44 who replied YES, and removed the 104 who stayed silent. His list fell from 412 to 213, and his reply rate doubled, from 6% to 13%, because the people left were the people who actually wanted to hear from him.

## What to do with the people who never open anything

There is a group every seller recognises: the contacts who never reply, never buy, and never leave, and who also never block you. They are not dead numbers. They read, or they used to. What do you do with them?

First, check whether the problem is the message or the person. If one segment of your list never replies and another replies happily to the same message, the problem is the segment. A customer who bought baby clothes two years ago will never reply to a message about men's sneakers, and that is not her fault. Move her to a list that matches what she actually bought, or stop sending her the wrong messages. If nobody on a list replies to any message for two months, the list itself is the problem, and it is time to rebuild it from your recent buyers instead of patching it.

Second, try one change before you give up on them. Change the time. Tunde's dormant contacts were mostly civil servants who never looked at their phones before 6pm. His 10am broadcasts arrived while they were at work, sank under a pile of office messages, and were never seen. He moved one broadcast a week to 6.30pm and his reply rate on that list rose from 4% to 11%. The people had not stopped caring. They had stopped seeing him.

Third, accept the ones who will never come back. A customer who bought once in 2024 and has ignored fifteen messages since then is not a lead. She is a line in a notebook. Remove her, and use the space to add the customer who bought yesterday and actually wants your updates. A list of 150 people who reply is worth more than a list of 500 who do not, every single time, in orders, in reply rate, and in the safety of your number.

## A monthly routine for a list that stays alive

The work is small if you do it regularly and painful if you leave it for a year. Once a month, first Saturday, one hour. Update the tracking sheet and read the last four weeks. Remove the dead numbers. Send the warm message to anyone silent for 90 days, and remove the ones who stay silent a week later. Add your new buyers from the month, because a list that never gains new names is a list that is slowly dying. Check your three numbers: reply rate, broadcast orders, naira value.

Tunde still sends twice a week, to a list half the size it used to be. His phone is not busier. It is quieter, and the messages that do arrive are orders, not questions from strangers. He knows which broadcast brought each order, he knows his Friday evening message earns more than his Tuesday morning one, and he knows that his list is alive because he counts it. That is the difference between a seller who sends broadcasts and a seller whose broadcasts sell.
