## Why Pricing Is Where Many Nigerian Businesses Lose Money

Many small businesses in Nigeria are busy but not profitable. They sell every day, their WhatsApp is full of orders, their shop is never empty — yet at the end of the month there is little money left. In most cases the problem is not sales. It is pricing.

Pricing feels simple: buy low, sell higher, keep the difference. In practice, the difference disappears into costs that were never counted — transport from the market, packaging, data, bank charges, delivery, a damaged item, a customer who never pays, or a discount given too easily. If you do not count those costs before you set a price, you can sell a lot and still lose money.

This guide shows you how to work out your true cost, choose a profit margin that fits your business, check what competitors charge without copying them blindly, and raise prices when costs rise — without losing your customers.

## Step 1: Know Your True Cost Per Product

Your true cost is not just what you paid the supplier. It is everything you spend to get one unit into a customer's hands in good condition. Write it down for each product, because guessing is how profit disappears.

Start with the purchase cost. If you buy a dress for ₦7,000 at Balogun Market, that is your starting figure. Add transport to bring it to your shop or home — say ₦500 if you shared a bus or a bike. Add packaging: a poly bag, a paper bag, or a box, perhaps ₦300. Add any market levy, loading fee, or transfer charge you paid, even if it was only ₦100.

Then add the cost of selling it. If you sell on Jumia or another marketplace, add the commission for that category. If you deliver it yourself, add fuel or rider fees. If you pay for advertising to sell it, add a share of that cost. A product that costs ₦7,000 to buy can easily cost ₦8,500 to sell once all of these are added.

Do this calculation on paper or in a simple spreadsheet. List the product, the purchase cost, transport, packaging, commission or delivery, and the total cost. When you can see the total cost clearly, you will stop setting prices by feeling and start setting them by fact.

## Step 2: Decide How Much Profit You Need

Profit is what is left after all costs are paid. If your total cost is ₦8,500 and you sell at ₦10,000, your profit is ₦1,500. That is your gross profit per unit. To judge whether that is enough, look at it as a margin — profit divided by selling price. In this example, ₦1,500 divided by ₦10,000 is 15 percent.

Different products need different margins. A low-priced item that sells in large numbers, like a phone case at ₦3,000, may work on a smaller margin because you sell many each day. A high-priced item that sells slowly, like a sofa at ₦250,000, needs a larger margin because you may sell only a few each month and you still have rent and other costs to cover.

As a starting point, many Nigerian small businesses aim for a gross margin of 25 to 40 percent on retail products, depending on the category. Food and fast-moving goods often work on thinner margins, while fashion, beauty, and handmade goods often need wider margins. Use those ranges as a guide, not a rule. The right margin for you is the one that covers your costs, pays you for your time, and still leaves money to grow the business.

Also count your own time. If you spend two hours sewing a dress, that time has value. If you price only for materials and forget your labour, you are paying yourself nothing. Add a labour or handling amount to your cost, even if it is modest, so your price reflects the work involved.

## Step 3: Check Competitor Prices — Then Think

Before you fix a price, look at what similar products sell for in your area and online. Visit two or three shops in your market, check Jumia and Instagram sellers, and note the range. If similar Ankara dresses sell for ₦15,000 to ₦20,000 in Lekki, pricing yours at ₦35,000 without a clear reason will push buyers away. Pricing yours at ₦8,000 may bring buyers, but it may also leave you with no profit.

Do not copy the cheapest seller. That seller may be buying in bulk, selling old stock, or cutting corners on quality. Ask instead: what does my customer get from me that they do not get elsewhere? Better fabric, neater finishing, faster delivery, a shop they can visit, a guarantee, or a brand they trust? If you can answer that clearly, you can justify a price in the middle or upper part of the range.

Also check the price of the alternative. A customer who buys a homemade cake for ₦25,000 is comparing it not only with other bakers, but with buying a cake from a supermarket or skipping the cake altogether. Your price must make sense against that alternative, not just against other sellers of the same item.

## Step 4: Choose a Pricing Method That Fits

There are three simple pricing methods that work for most Nigerian small businesses. You do not need to use all three. Pick the one that fits your product and your customers.

Cost-plus pricing is the simplest. You add up your total cost and add your chosen margin. If your total cost is ₦8,500 and you want a 30 percent margin, you divide the cost by 0.7 to get a selling price of about ₦12,150. You can round that to ₦12,000 or ₦12,500 for a clean price. This method is reliable because it guarantees a profit on every sale, as long as your cost figure is honest.

Market pricing sets your price based on what customers in your area expect to pay. If everyone sells a similar wig for ₦45,000, you price yours in that range and compete on quality, service, or delivery. This works well when customers know the usual price and will not pay much more, even for a better product.

Value pricing sets your price based on what the product is worth to the customer. A custom wedding dress, a branded gift box, or a tailored suit can command a higher price because the customer is paying for the result, the occasion, and the experience, not just the material. This method needs strong branding and clear communication of quality, but it can give the best margins.

Many successful sellers combine these methods: they use cost-plus to set a floor below which they will not sell, market pricing to stay competitive, and value pricing for their best or most unique products.

## Step 5: Use Clean, Honest Prices

Customers in Nigeria are used to bargaining, but they also appreciate clarity. A clear price saves time for both sides. If you sell on WhatsApp, Instagram, or a website, state the price or give a clear starting price. "DM for price" on every product slows down buyers and makes many of them move on to a seller who states the price openly.

Use round numbers where it helps. ₦10,000, ₦15,500, and ₦25,000 are easier to remember and to pay than ₦9,873 or ₦15,432. If you use a price ending in 500 or 900, do it deliberately — for example, ₦9,500 can feel cheaper than ₦10,000 — but do not make your prices so complicated that customers need a calculator.

Be clear about what is included. Does the price include delivery, or is delivery charged separately? Does a food tray serve four people or six? Does a phone come with a charger? State it in the description. Hidden extras are one of the fastest ways to lose a customer, while clear terms build trust even when the price is not the lowest.

## Step 6: Handle Discounts Without Destroying Your Profit

Discounts can bring customers, but they can also destroy your margin if you use them carelessly. Before you offer a discount, work out the new profit. If your normal price is ₦12,000, your cost is ₦8,500, and you offer 20 percent off, the new price is ₦9,600 and your profit is only ₦1,100. If you offer that discount to everyone, every day, you are working hard for very little.

Use discounts with a purpose and a limit. A discount for a first order, a bulk purchase, a public holiday sale, or a clearance of old stock can work well. A permanent discount with no end date simply becomes your new price, and customers will expect it forever.

Avoid the common trap of raising the price first and then discounting it. Customers compare prices, and they notice when a product that was ₦15,000 yesterday is suddenly ₦25,000 with a 40 percent discount. That kind of pricing erodes trust and can damage your reputation, especially on marketplaces where price history is visible.

Instead, offer value rather than a price cut where you can. Free delivery within Lekki for orders above ₦30,000, a small free gift with a first order, or a loyalty reward for repeat customers can be more profitable than a discount, and customers often value them more.

## Step 7: Plan for Rising Costs and Price Reviews

Prices in Nigeria change often. Fuel, transport, the exchange rate, and supplier prices can all rise within a few months. A price that was profitable in January may lose money by June if you never review it.

Set a simple routine: review your prices once a month, or whenever a major cost changes. Check your top ten selling products first. Has the supplier price gone up? Has transport gone up? Has your packaging cost changed? If your cost has risen by ₦1,000, your selling price needs to rise too, unless you are willing to accept a smaller profit.

When you need to raise prices, do it clearly and calmly. Tell regular customers in advance where possible, explain briefly that costs have gone up, and keep the increase modest and fair. Most customers understand that prices rise. What they do not accept is a sudden, unexplained jump, or a different price every time they ask.

Keep a price list, even if it is only in a notebook or on your phone. Update it when prices change, and share the current list with your team so everyone quotes the same price. Nothing damages trust faster than two staff members giving two different prices for the same product on the same day.

## Step 8: Price Services Differently From Products

Services are priced differently because the main cost is your time and skill, not stock you bought. A tailor in Yaba, a makeup artist in Abuja, or a digital marketer in Lekki should start by working out an hourly or daily rate that covers living costs, business costs, and profit, then use that rate to price each job.

First, add up your monthly business costs: rent, transport, data, electricity, and materials. Add the monthly income you need to live on. Divide that total by the number of billable hours you can realistically work in a month. If your total is ₦300,000 and you can bill 100 hours, your rate needs to be at least ₦3,000 per hour before profit. A job that takes five hours should therefore cost at least ₦15,000, plus materials.

Do not price a service by copying a beginner. An experienced photographer who delivers edited photos in 48 hours, a caterer who cooks for 200 guests without stress, or a web designer who builds a site that brings in customers can charge more than someone starting out, because the customer is paying for reliability and results. State what is included — number of revisions, delivery time, or number of guests — so the customer can compare fairly.

For services, also ask for a deposit. A 50 percent deposit before work starts protects you from customers who change their minds after you have bought materials or blocked your calendar. Put the deposit, the balance, and the delivery date in writing on WhatsApp or on an invoice, so both sides are clear from the start.

## Common Pricing Mistakes to Avoid

The first mistake is pricing by copying a competitor without knowing your own costs. Their costs may be lower because they buy in bulk or have no rent to pay. Your price must work for your business, not theirs.

The second mistake is forgetting small costs. Bank transfer charges, POS charges, packaging, data, and transport all count. A business that forgets ₦500 of costs on every sale will lose ₦50,000 after only a hundred sales.

The third mistake is underpricing to win customers, then being unable to sustain the business. A low price brings a rush of orders, but if each order loses money, the rush only speeds up the loss. It is better to have fewer customers at a profitable price than many customers at a loss.

The fourth mistake is never raising prices. Some sellers keep the same price for a year while costs rise, hoping customers will stay loyal. In the end they either close or start cutting quality to survive. A small, regular price review protects both your business and your quality.

## A Worked Example: Pricing a Skincare Set

Imagine you sell a skincare set in Abuja. You buy the set for ₦12,000, pay ₦800 for transport, ₦400 for packaging, and ₦1,200 in marketplace commission if you sell online. Your total cost is ₦14,400.

If you want a 30 percent gross margin, divide ₦14,400 by 0.7. That gives about ₦20,570, which you can round to ₦20,500 or ₦21,000. At ₦21,000, your profit is ₦6,600 per set. If you sell ten sets a week, that is ₦66,000 in gross profit before rent and other overheads.

If a competitor sells a similar set for ₦18,000, you need to decide whether your set is worth the extra ₦3,000. If it is a trusted brand, well packaged, and delivered quickly, many customers will pay the difference. If it is identical and you offer nothing extra, you may need to reduce your margin, find a cheaper supplier, or sell a different product.

## Your Pricing Checklist

Write down the true cost of each product, including transport, packaging, commission, and delivery. Choose a target margin and calculate a selling price from your cost, not from a guess. Check competitor prices and decide where you fit in the range.

State your prices clearly, say what is included, and keep one price list for your whole team. Use discounts sparingly, with a clear purpose and end date. Review your prices monthly, and raise them calmly when costs rise.

## Final Thoughts

Good pricing is not about charging the highest price you can get away with. It is about charging a fair price that covers your costs, pays you for your work, and leaves enough to keep the business running and growing.

Start with your best-selling product today. Work out its true cost, check its margin, and see whether the price still makes sense. If it does not, adjust it. That one exercise can change how much money your business keeps at the end of the month — and it is the foundation for every other decision you make as you grow.
