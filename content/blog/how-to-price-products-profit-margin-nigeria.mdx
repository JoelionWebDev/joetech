Ngozi sells handbags, wigs and sneakers from a shop in Onitsha Main Market, and for two years she kept all her prices in her head. She knew the handbag cost ₦14,500 and sold for ₦21,000. She knew the wig was expensive to buy and expensive to sell. What she did not know, until a slow month forced her to sit down with a notebook, was which of her products actually paid her — and which ones only looked profitable.

The answer was on one page. Not in a business course, not in an app she had to pay for. A table with six columns: the product, what it cost her, what she sold it for, the profit in naira, and the margin as a percentage. Once the numbers sat next to each other, the truth was hard to miss. Her phone case, the cheapest thing in the shop, had the best margin in the building. Her wig, the grandest item on the shelf, earned her the most naira per sale by a distance. And one perfume set she had been pushing all month was quietly earning less than the small chops pack her sister sold from a cooler by the door.

This article is that table. You can copy it, fill it with your own numbers, and keep it on your phone. It does the job that memory cannot: it shows you, side by side, what each product gives you back.

## The Table, Filled In

Here is Ngozi's table after she did the exercise properly, with every cost counted — supplier price, transport, packaging, delivery, payment charges, data, and a share of rent and market dues folded into the total cost of each unit.

| Product | Total cost | Selling price | Profit | Margin % | Markup % |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Leather handbag | ₦14,500 | ₦21,000 | ₦6,500 | 31.0% | 44.8% |
| Human-hair wig | ₦38,000 | ₦58,000 | ₦20,000 | 34.5% | 52.6% |
| Sneakers | ₦22,000 | ₦34,000 | ₦12,000 | 35.3% | 54.5% |
| Small chops pack | ₦1,800 | ₦3,000 | ₦1,200 | 40.0% | 66.7% |
| Phone case | ₦1,200 | ₦2,500 | ₦1,300 | 52.0% | 108.3% |
| Perfume oil set | ₦6,500 | ₦9,500 | ₦3,000 | 31.6% | 46.2% |

Two columns in that table need a plain explanation, because mixing them up is how sellers talk themselves into a bad price. Profit is the selling price minus the total cost, in naira. Margin percent is that profit divided by the selling price, times a hundred — it tells you how much of each naira the customer pays you actually keep. Markup percent is the profit divided by the cost, times a hundred — it tells you how much you added on top of what you paid. The handbag's profit is ₦6,500 either way. As a margin it is 31.0 percent of the ₦21,000 price. As a markup it is 44.8 percent on top of the ₦14,500 cost. Same profit, two different percentages, and you need to know which one you are looking at before you quote it.

## How to Read Your Own Table

Read the table in three passes, and each pass answers a different question.

The first pass is the profit column, in naira. This is the money question: how much does one sale put in your pocket? Ngozi's wig wins by a mile at ₦20,000 a sale. Her sneakers are next at ₦12,000. The phone case, for all its fine percentages, puts ₦1,300 in her pocket — real money, but she would need to sell fifteen phone cases to match one wig. If you need ₦150,000 this month to cover rent and food, the profit column tells you how many sales of each product get you there. Seven and a half wigs. Twelve and a half pairs of sneakers. Twenty-three handbags. One hundred and fifteen phone cases. That is the table doing arithmetic your head will not do reliably at 9 p.m. after a long day.

The second pass is the margin column. This is the health question: for every ₦100 a customer hands you, how much stays with you after costs? The phone case keeps ₦52 of every ₦100, the best in the shop. The small chops pack keeps ₦40. The handbag keeps ₦31, the perfume set ₦31.60, the wig ₦34.50, the sneakers ₦35.30. A high margin means the product forgives mistakes. If delivery costs jump by ₦500, the phone case still earns. A low margin means the product does not forgive anything — a discount, a damaged item, a rider who charges more than usual, and the profit is gone.

The third pass is the comparison between the two. The product with the best margin is rarely the product with the best profit, and that gap is where sellers make their real decisions. Ngozi's phone case has a 52 percent margin and earns ₦1,300. Her wig has a 34.5 percent margin and earns ₦20,000. If she only looked at margin, she would push phone cases all day and wonder why the month ended short. The table lets her do both: push the wig when a customer is ready to spend, and keep the phone cases by the till where they sell themselves.

## What a Small Price Change Does

The most useful thing a table does is show you what happens before you change a price, instead of after. Take the handbag. Its total cost is ₦14,500, and Ngozi sells it at ₦21,000 for a profit of ₦6,500 and a margin of 31.0 percent. Watch what happens as the price moves in ₦1,000 steps.

| Handbag price | Profit | Margin % |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ₦19,000 | ₦4,500 | 23.7% |
| ₦20,000 | ₦5,500 | 27.5% |
| ₦21,000 | ₦6,500 | 31.0% |
| ₦22,000 | ₦7,500 | 34.1% |
| ₦23,000 | ₦8,500 | 37.0% |

Every ₦1,000 on the price is ₦1,000 straight to profit, because the cost has not moved. But the margin does not rise in a straight line — it climbs by about three to four points each step, and each step is harder to win from the customer than the last. The jump from ₦19,000 to ₦20,000 is easy. The jump from ₦22,000 to ₦23,000 is where a customer starts comparing you with the shop across the road. The table does not tell you where to stop. It tells you exactly what each step is worth, so the decision is yours with the numbers in front of you.

A discount shows up the same way, and it is usually uglier than sellers expect. Knock 10 percent off the handbag's ₦21,000 and the price becomes ₦18,900. The profit falls to ₦4,400 and the margin drops to 23.3 percent. A tenth off the price took nearly a third off the profit. Ngozi used to offer 10 percent to anyone who asked. She stopped when she saw that row, and started offering a free phone case with a handbag instead — a gift that costs her ₦1,200 and feels generous, rather than a discount that costs her ₦2,100 and feels expected.

A cost rise shows up too. When her supplier raised the handbag price and her total cost went from ₦14,500 to ₦16,000, the same ₦21,000 selling price left her ₦5,000 profit and a 23.8 percent margin. The table made the choice obvious: raise the price to ₦22,500, which restores the profit to ₦6,500, or find a cheaper supplier. She raised the price, told her regulars why, and kept the supplier she trusted.

## Copy This Template

You do not need a laptop or a paid app. A notebook works, and Google Sheets on your phone works better, because the formulas do the maths for you. Copy these columns exactly, one row per product.

| Product | Total cost ₦ | Selling price ₦ | Profit ₦ | Margin % | Markup % |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  |  |  |  |  |  |
|  |  |  |  |  |  |
|  |  |  |  |  |  |

Fill in the total cost first, and be honest about it. That column is where most tables fail, because it is where the small costs hide: the ₦500 transport, the ₦300 packaging, the POS charge, the data, the share of rent. If the cost column is wrong, every column after it is wrong too.

Then add three formulas. Profit is selling price minus total cost. Margin percent is profit divided by selling price, times a hundred. Markup percent is profit divided by total cost, times a hundred. In Sheets, if total cost sits in column B and selling price in column C, profit is `=C2-B2`, margin is `=(C2-B2)/C2*100`, and markup is `=(C2-B2)/B2*100`. Copy them down every row and the table fills itself.

Keep the table to your ten best sellers at most. A table with forty rows is a table nobody reads. Ten rows you actually look at will change your prices. Forty rows you never open will not.

## The Weekly Habit That Makes It Pay

Ngozi looks at her table every Sunday evening, and the habit takes her twenty minutes. She sorts the rows by profit in naira and asks which product deserves more of her attention next week — more photos, a better spot in the shop, a mention on her WhatsApp status. Then she sorts by margin percent and asks which product is fragile: which one would stop paying if a cost rose or a discount slipped in. Then she checks the costs. Has a supplier raised a price? Has the rider's fee gone up? If a cost moved, she changes the cost cell, and the profit and margin cells change with it. The decision follows the numbers instead of chasing them a month later.

She also watches the total, not just the rows. A product can have a fine margin and still fail you if it barely sells. Multiply the profit per unit by the units sold that week and you get the product's real contribution. Her wig earns ₦20,000 a sale but sells twice a week: ₦40,000. Her phone cases earn ₦1,300 and sell thirty times a week: ₦39,000. Nearly identical, from opposite ends of the table.

## What the Table Will Not Do

A table will not set your price for you. It will not tell you whether your customers will pay ₦23,000 for the handbag, or whether the woman across the road sells it for ₦19,500. It will not count the value of a customer who buys a handbag today and a wig at Christmas. What it does is smaller and more useful: it stops you guessing. It puts the cost, the price, the profit and the margin for every product on one page, in figures you can check, so that when you do decide — to raise a price, to run a discount, to drop a product that never pays — you decide with the numbers in front of you instead of a feeling in your head.

Start with three products tonight. Your best seller, the one you suspect is underpriced, and the one you sell most often. Fill in six columns each. The answers will surprise you. They surprised Ngozi, and she had been selling for two years.
