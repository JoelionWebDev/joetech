## Why Free Samples Work — and Why They Often Fail

Free samples are one of the oldest marketing tools in the world, and they work for a simple reason: people are more willing to try something new when it costs them nothing. A customer who would never spend ₦5,000 on a product they have never tasted will happily take a small free taste. If the taste is good, the sale often follows.

Nigerian markets have always understood this. The woman selling groundnuts at the market offers you a few to taste. The skincare vendor at a trade fair lets you rub cream on the back of your hand. The food vendor at a Lagos food festival gives out small cups of jollof. Sampling is built into how Nigerians shop, and customers expect it, especially for food, drinks, cosmetics and other products you experience with your senses.

But free samples also fail often. A business gives away hundreds of samples at an event and gets almost no sales. A shop owner hands out free products every day and watches their profit disappear. The difference between sampling that works and sampling that wastes money is not luck. It is strategy: who you sample to, what you sample, how much you give, and — most importantly — what you do after the sample.

This guide shows you how to run a free sample campaign that actually converts. You will learn which products to sample, how to calculate what sampling costs, where to offer samples, how to follow up with people who tried your product, and how to measure whether the whole exercise made you money.

## The Psychology: Why a Small Taste Leads to a Purchase

To sample well, you need to understand why sampling works. The first reason is risk removal. Buying an unknown product feels risky. What if the food tastes bad? What if the cream irritates my skin? What if the drink is too sweet? A free sample removes that risk completely. The customer tries before they buy, and the fear disappears.

The second reason is reciprocity. When someone gives us something for free, we feel a natural urge to give something back. A customer who accepts your sample and enjoys it feels a gentle pressure to buy, especially when you are standing there smiling. This is not manipulation — it is human nature, and it works best when the sample is genuinely good and the offer to buy is friendly, not pushy.

The third reason is experience. An advert can tell a customer your chin chin is crunchy, but a taste proves it. A picture can show your shea butter is smooth, but touching it proves it. Samples turn claims into experiences, and experiences are far more persuasive than words.

The fourth reason is habit. A customer who tries your product once and likes it has taken the hardest step: the first use. The second purchase is easier than the first, because the product is no longer unknown. Sampling is really about getting customers over that first-use hurdle.

Understanding these four reasons tells you when sampling will work and when it will not. It works when your product is genuinely good, when the customer can experience the difference immediately, and when buying is easy right after tasting. It fails when the product is mediocre, when the sample is too small to judge, or when there is no easy way to buy afterwards.

## Choose the Right Product to Sample

Not every product should be sampled. The best products for sampling share three qualities. First, the customer can judge the quality quickly — one taste, one touch, one use. Food, drinks, snacks, skincare, hair products, perfumes and cleaning products all qualify. Second, the product has a clear advantage over what the customer currently uses. If your groundnut oil tastes the same as every other groundnut oil, a sample proves nothing. Third, the product is affordable enough that the customer can buy it on the spot after tasting.

If you sell several products, sample your best one, not your cheapest one. Your best product is the one customers are most likely to love and buy again. Sampling a weak product to save money is false economy — a bad sample actively drives customers away.

Consider the sample size carefully. A sample should be big enough to judge the product and small enough to keep your costs low. For food, a small cup or a bite-sized piece is enough. For skincare, a small sachet or a dab on the hand is enough. For drinks, a small cup is enough. The sample should satisfy curiosity, not replace a meal. If your sample is so large that the customer feels full or fully supplied, they have no reason to buy.

Also think about packaging. A sample in a plain nylon bag tells the customer nothing. A sample in a small branded pack, with your business name, phone number and price, turns the sample into an advert that goes home with the customer. Even a simple sticker with your WhatsApp number on the sample pack can bring orders days later.

## Calculate What Sampling Will Cost You

Sampling is marketing, and marketing costs money. Before you give away a single item, calculate the cost so you can decide whether it is worth it.

The cost of a sample has three parts. The product cost is the cost of the ingredients or the item itself. If you make chin chin, calculate the cost of flour, sugar, oil and packaging for one sample portion. The labour cost is the time spent preparing and handing out samples. The distribution cost is transport to the sampling location, the cost of a table or stand, and any fee for the venue or event.

Add these up and multiply by the number of samples you plan to give. If each sample costs ₦150 to make and distribute, and you plan to give out 200 samples, your total cost is ₦30,000. That is your sampling budget. Now ask the key question: how many of those 200 people need to buy for you to recover ₦30,000? If your product sells for ₦2,000 with a profit of ₦800 per sale, you need about 38 buyers to break even. That means a conversion rate of about 19 percent. Is that realistic? For a good product sampled to the right people, yes. For a random crowd, maybe not.

Set a budget and a limit before you start. Decide in advance how many samples you will give and where. When the samples run out, stop. Giving away extra samples because you feel generous is how sampling budgets collapse. A limit also creates urgency — customers who see others tasting and buying are more likely to buy before the samples finish.

Track your spending as you go. Keep the receipts for ingredients, packaging and transport. At the end of the campaign, you should know exactly what sampling cost you, so you can compare it with the sales it produced.

## Where to Offer Samples for the Best Results

Location matters as much as the product. The best sampling locations have three qualities: the right people, high foot traffic, and a setting where buying is easy.

Your own shop is the easiest and cheapest place to sample. Every customer who walks in is already interested in what you sell. Offer a taste at the counter, and the path from sample to purchase is a few steps. This works particularly well for food businesses, bakeries, and shops selling snacks or drinks.

Markets and trade fairs are excellent for reaching new customers. A stall at a market in Ikeja or a trade fair in Lekki puts your product in front of hundreds of people who came to buy. The cost is the stall fee and your time, but the exposure is large. Choose events where your target customers actually go — a food festival for a food product, a beauty expo for a skincare product.

Churches, mosques, offices and schools can work well for targeted sampling, with permission. A skincare vendor who samples at a women's meeting reaches exactly the right audience. A snacks business that samples at an office during lunchtime reaches hungry workers with money to spend. Always get permission from the organisers first, and always leave the place clean.

Social media sampling is a growing option. Send small samples to micro-influencers or loyal customers and ask them to post an honest review. This costs the product and delivery, but one good review video on TikTok or Instagram can reach thousands of people. Choose reviewers whose followers match your customers, not just anyone with a large following.

Avoid sampling where your target customers are not present. Giving out baby food samples at a football viewing centre wastes your stock. Giving out luxury perfume samples in a market where people shop on tight budgets wastes your time. Match the location to the customer.

## The Follow-Up: Where Most Sampling Campaigns Fail

Here is the truth that most small businesses miss: the sample is not the sale. The follow-up is the sale. A customer who tastes your product and walks away without buying is not a lost customer — they are a warm lead. But they will forget you within days unless you follow up.

The follow-up starts at the moment of sampling. When someone tries your product, ask for their name and WhatsApp number. Offer a reason: "Let me send you our price list and our delivery details." Most people who enjoyed the sample will happily share their number. Write it down immediately, or have them send you a message on WhatsApp so their number is saved in your chat.

Then follow up within 24 to 48 hours, while the taste is still fresh in their mind. Send a short, friendly message: "Hello Ada, it was nice meeting you at the food fair yesterday. I hope you enjoyed the chin chin. Our 500g pack is ₦2,500 and we deliver in Lekki and Victoria Island. Would you like to order one?" This message is polite, specific, and makes buying easy.

Do not follow up once and stop. Many customers need two or three gentle reminders before they buy. Send a second message after a few days, perhaps with a small incentive: a discount for first-time buyers, or free delivery on their first order. If they still do not buy after three messages, stop. Continuing to message someone who is not interested is spam, and it damages your reputation.

Keep a simple record of your sampling leads. A notebook or a Google Sheet with columns for name, phone number, date sampled, product sampled, and follow-up status is enough. Each week, check the list and follow up with anyone you have not contacted. This list is one of the most valuable assets your sampling campaign produces, because it is a list of people who have already tried and liked your product.

## Turn One-Time Buyers Into Repeat Customers

A customer who buys once after a sample is a good result. A customer who buys every week is a great one. The real profit from sampling comes from repeat purchases, not the first sale.

Make the first purchase easy and pleasant. Deliver on time. Package the product well. Include a small thank-you note or a small extra item. First impressions after the sample matter, because the customer is still deciding whether your business is reliable.

Then stay in touch. Add the customer to your WhatsApp broadcast list, with their permission, and send them updates about new products, discounts and restocks. Do not message them every day. Once or twice a week is enough. A customer who hears from you occasionally will remember you when they need your product again.

Consider a simple loyalty offer. A buy-five-get-one-free card, a discount on the second purchase, or a referral reward for bringing a friend. These cost little and give customers a reason to come back and to bring others. Word of mouth from a happy customer is more powerful than any sample you could give.

Ask for feedback, and act on it. A week after the first purchase, ask the customer how they liked the product. If they loved it, ask for a short testimonial you can post. If they had a problem, fix it and tell them. Customers who feel heard become loyal customers, and loyal customers become your best marketers.

## Mistakes That Waste Your Samples

The first mistake is sampling to everyone instead of your target customer. A crowd of children at an event will happily eat your free snacks and buy nothing, because they have no money. Sample to people who can actually buy.

The second mistake is giving samples with no way to buy. If you sample at a market but have no stock to sell on the spot, the customer walks away with a nice taste and no product. Always bring stock to a sampling event, and always have your price list, your payment details and your delivery information ready.

The third mistake is a sample that is worse than the real product. If you make a special small batch for sampling that is lower quality than what you sell, customers will feel cheated when they buy the real thing. Your sample should be exactly the product you sell, at its best.

The fourth mistake is no follow-up, which we covered above. A sample without follow-up is a gift, not marketing. If you are not going to follow up, save your stock.

The fifth mistake is sampling too often in the same place. If you give free samples at the same market every week, people will come for the free food and never buy. Sampling should be an event, not a routine. Use it to launch a product, to enter a new location, or to win customers at a special occasion. Regular customers should be buying, not sampling.

## Measure Your Results Honestly

After every sampling campaign, sit down and measure it. How many samples did you give out? How much did it cost in total? How many people bought within a week? How many bought again within a month? How much revenue did those sales bring?

Calculate your conversion rate by dividing the number of buyers by the number of samples given. A conversion rate of 15 to 25 percent is a good result for most small businesses. Below 10 percent, something is wrong — either the product, the audience, or the follow-up. Above 30 percent, you have a winning product and a winning approach, and you should sample more.

Also calculate your return on investment. If sampling cost ₦30,000 and it brought in ₦90,000 in sales in the first month, that is a strong return. If it cost ₦30,000 and brought in ₦15,000, the campaign lost money, and you need to change something before you do it again.

Keep these numbers for every campaign. Over time, you will learn which locations, products and follow-up methods work best for your business. That knowledge is worth more than any single campaign, because it lets you spend your marketing money where it actually works. ## Final Thoughts

Free samples are not about giving things away. They are about giving the right people a reason to trust your product, and then making it easy for them to buy. The businesses that succeed with sampling are the ones that choose the right product, control the cost, sample in the right place, follow up quickly, and measure the results.

Start small. Pick your best product, prepare a limited number of samples, choose one good location, and follow up with every person who tries it. Measure what happens. If it works, do it again, bigger. If it does not, find out why before you spend more.

Your product deserves to be tasted. Give people the chance to taste it, and give yourself the systems to turn tasters into customers. That is how free samples build a paying customer base — one taste, one follow-up, and one repeat order at a time.
