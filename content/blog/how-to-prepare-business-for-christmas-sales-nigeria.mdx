Every year, the same thing happens in Nigeria. From the last week of November, money starts moving. Salaries come with 13th-month bonuses, people return from abroad for Detty December, families buy new clothes, shoes, foodstuff, phones and gifts. Markets in Lagos, Onitsha, Aba and Kano get crowded, and WhatsApp statuses fill up with "Christmas sales" flyers. For a small business, December can bring in more money than the previous three months combined — but only if you are ready for it.

The businesses that make the most money at Christmas are not always the biggest ones. They are the ones that planned early, stocked the right products, told their customers in advance, and made buying easy. This guide walks you through exactly how to prepare, step by step, with realistic timelines and Nigerian prices.

## Start Early: Build Your Christmas Timeline

The biggest mistake small businesses make is starting Christmas preparation in mid-December. By then, suppliers have raised prices, delivery companies are overwhelmed, and your customers have already spent their money elsewhere. Start in early November, or at the very latest, the last week of November.

A simple timeline works well. In the first two weeks of November, review last December's sales if you have records. Which products sold out? Which ones sat on the shelf? What did customers keep asking for? If this is your first Christmas in business, look at what similar shops around you sold last year and ask your regular customers what they plan to buy.

In the second half of November, place your orders with suppliers. Markets like Balogun, Onitsha Main Market, and Ariaria in Aba get extremely busy in December, and prices rise as demand grows. A carton of shoes or a bale of clothes that costs ₦180,000 in November can cost ₦230,000 by the second week of December. Ordering early protects your profit margin.

In the first week of December, launch your Christmas campaign. Put up your decorations, announce your offers, and start posting daily. The second and third weeks of December are your peak selling weeks. Plan to have your best stock, your best offers, and your full team available then. The last week of December, between Christmas and New Year, is for clearance: sell off remaining stock at a discount so you enter January with cash, not leftover goods.

## Know What Your Customers Actually Want

Christmas buying in Nigeria follows clear patterns. Parents buy clothes and shoes for children. Young people buy outfits for parties, concerts, and church programmes. Families buy rice, vegetable oil, chicken, drinks, and hampers. Offices buy gifts for staff and clients. People travelling home buy gifts for relatives in the village.

Look at your own business and ask which of these patterns fits you. A fashion seller in Lekki should stock party dresses, senator wears, and children's outfits. A foodstuff seller in Mile 12 or Bodija should stock bagged rice, oil, and hamper items. A phone accessories seller in Computer Village should stock phone cases, chargers, and earphones, because people buy gadgets as gifts.

Do not guess. Ask your customers directly. Send a short WhatsApp broadcast to your regulars: "Hello, as Christmas approaches, what would you like us to stock for you this year?" The answers will guide your buying better than any guesswork. You can also run a quick poll on your WhatsApp status or Instagram story with two or three options. Customers love being asked, and their answers tell you exactly where to put your money.

## Stock Smart: Buy Enough, Not Too Much

Stocking for Christmas is a balance. Buy too little and you lose sales when products run out during the busiest week. Buy too much and you are stuck with goods in January when nobody is buying. The solution is to stock in two rounds.

Your first round, in November, should cover your proven bestsellers — the products you know will sell. Buy about 70 percent of what you expect to sell. Your second round, in early December, should be a top-up based on what is actually moving. If a particular shoe design is selling fast, order more of that one. If another is slow, do not restock it.

Negotiate with your suppliers in November while they still have time. Ask for a better price on bulk orders, and ask about their return policy for unsold goods. Some wholesalers in Lagos and Onitsha will allow exchanges after Christmas for fast-moving items. Get this agreement in writing on WhatsApp, even if it is just a chat message confirming the terms.

Keep a simple stock sheet, even if it is just a notebook or a Google Sheet on your phone. Write down each product, how many you bought, the cost price, and the selling price. When December gets busy, this sheet stops you from selling at a loss or forgetting what you paid. It also makes your January stock count easy.

## Price Your Christmas Offers Honestly

Christmas shoppers in Nigeria are price-sensitive and promotion-savvy. They compare prices across shops and on Instagram before buying. Your offers need to be real, not tricks. A fake discount — raising the price first and then "reducing" it — is quickly noticed, and customers will call it out publicly on social media.

Simple offers work best. Buy two, get one free works well for clothes, shoes, and cosmetics. A percentage discount of 10 to 20 percent is easy to understand and easy to calculate. Bundles work very well at Christmas: a hamper of rice, oil, and drinks at ₦45,000 feels like better value than buying each item separately, even if the total is similar. Free delivery within Lagos or Abuja for orders above ₦50,000 can push a hesitant customer to buy more.

Set your prices before December starts and write them down. When the rush begins, you will be tempted to change prices on the spot. A fixed price list keeps you consistent and protects your reputation. If your cost price goes up because a supplier raised prices, adjust once, announce it, and stick to it.

Also, decide your Christmas closing and opening dates early. Will you deliver on Christmas Day? Will your shop open on Boxing Day? Tell your customers in advance so they can plan. Many businesses in Lagos close on 25 December and reopen on 26 or 27 December. Whatever you choose, communicate it clearly on your WhatsApp status and shop notice board.

## Make Your Shop and Online Pages Look Like Christmas

People buy with their eyes, especially at Christmas. A shop that looks festive attracts walk-in customers. You do not need to spend a lot of money. A small Christmas tree, some red and gold ribbons, fairy lights, and a hand-painted "Merry Christmas" sign can transform a small shop in Surulere or Enugu for less than ₦20,000.

Your online pages matter even more, because many customers will see your WhatsApp catalogue or Instagram page before they ever visit your shop. Update your WhatsApp Business catalogue with Christmas products and clear prices. Change your profile picture or status to something festive. Create a simple Christmas flyer with your best offers and your phone number in large text. You can design one on your phone with free apps like Canva, and it will look professional enough for WhatsApp and Instagram.

Post every day in December, but keep your posts useful. Show the actual products, show the prices, show customers wearing or using them, and show your shop. A short video of you arranging new Christmas stock gets more attention than a plain flyer. Post in the morning when people check their phones, and again in the evening when they are free.

## Plan Delivery Before the Rush Breaks It

Delivery is where many Nigerian businesses fail at Christmas. Riders are booked solid, traffic in Lagos doubles, and inter-state buses are full. A customer in Abuja who orders on 20 December expects the goods before Christmas, and if they arrive on 27 December, you have lost that customer.

Start by setting a clear last-order date for Christmas delivery and announcing it early. For example, tell customers that orders outside Lagos must be placed by 18 December, and orders within Lagos by 22 December. This manages expectations and pushes people to order early.

Talk to your dispatch riders and logistics companies now, in November, before they are overwhelmed. Agree on rates and confirm they will be available during your peak weeks. If you use a logistics company, ask about their Christmas schedule and cut-off dates. Some stop accepting new parcels a few days before Christmas.

Package your goods properly. Christmas orders are often gifts, so neat packaging matters. A simple branded nylon bag, tissue paper, or a small gift wrap costs very little and makes the customer feel the purchase was special. For fragile items like perfumes or electronics, add extra protection. A broken gift on Christmas morning is a refund and a bad review.

If you deliver yourself within your area, plan your routes. Group deliveries by neighbourhood — all the Lekki deliveries together, all the Ikeja deliveries together — instead of crisscrossing the city. This saves fuel, time, and stress. Fuel prices are high, so route planning directly protects your profit.

## Make Payment Easy and Safe

At Christmas, customers pay in many ways: bank transfer, POS, cash, and sometimes instalments. Make every option available and make it smooth. If your POS machine has been unreliable, get it serviced or get a backup before December. A failed POS transaction in a crowded shop costs you the sale and embarrasses the customer.

For online orders, confirm payment quickly. When a customer sends a transfer, reply within minutes to confirm you received it. At Christmas, people are anxious about being scammed, especially when buying from Instagram vendors. Quick confirmation builds trust. Share your account name clearly so customers can verify it matches your business name.

Consider accepting a deposit for large orders. If a customer orders a ₦150,000 hamper package or a set of outfits, a 50 percent deposit secures the order and protects you if they change their mind. State the deposit policy clearly before taking the order, and give a receipt, even if it is a WhatsApp message confirming the amount received and the balance due.

Keep your cash safe. December brings more cash into shops, and that attracts attention. Deposit cash in the bank regularly instead of keeping large amounts in the shop. If you use a POS, reconcile your sales at the end of each day so you can spot errors quickly.

## Get Your Team Ready

If you have staff, Christmas is not the time to figure out roles. Hold a short meeting in late November and explain the plan: opening hours, who handles walk-in customers, who handles WhatsApp orders, who packs deliveries, and who handles complaints. Give everyone a copy of the price list so customers get the same price from every staff member.

Consider hiring temporary help for the peak weeks. A student on holiday or a trusted neighbour can handle packing and deliveries while your main staff focus on selling. Pay them fairly and train them for a day before the rush starts. A temporary worker who does not know your prices or your products will slow you down, not help you.

Take care of your staff too. December is long hours and high pressure. A small Christmas bonus, even ₦10,000 or ₦20,000, or a gift of rice and chicken, goes a long way. Staff who feel appreciated serve customers better, and customers notice the difference.

If you run the business alone, be honest with yourself about your limits. You cannot answer WhatsApp at 2 a.m., pack orders, and serve walk-in customers all day. Set your working hours, use WhatsApp Business auto-replies for messages outside those hours, and tell customers when to expect a reply. A clear "I will reply within 2 hours" is better than silence.

## Handle Complaints Fast and Gracefully

Christmas brings more sales and more complaints. A wrong size, a late delivery, a product that looks different from the photo — these happen even to careful businesses. What matters is how fast and how gracefully you respond.

Reply to complaints the same day. Apologise, even if the fault is partly the customer's. Offer a clear solution: exchange, refund, or redelivery. Do not argue on WhatsApp or in public comments. Take the conversation to a private chat or a phone call, solve it there, and then ask the customer to confirm they are satisfied.

Keep a small budget for Christmas refunds and exchanges. Setting aside 2 to 3 percent of your expected December revenue for this purpose means a refund never feels like a disaster. A customer whose complaint was handled well often becomes a loyal customer who recommends you to others.

## Watch Your Money: Track Christmas Cash Flow

December brings in a lot of money, but it also takes a lot out. Stock, decorations, extra staff, delivery costs, and fuel all add up. If you are not tracking both sides, you can finish December busy and broke. Keep a simple daily record: money in, money out, and what it was for. A notebook works, and so does a free phone app or a Google Sheet.

Separate your Christmas profit from your Christmas revenue. If you sold ₦2,000,000 worth of goods in December but spent ₦1,600,000 on stock, transport, and staff, your real profit is ₦400,000, not ₦2,000,000. Knowing this stops you from overspending in January on the excitement of a big December.

Set aside money for January as December progresses. January is traditionally the slowest month for Nigerian businesses — school fees, rent, and post-Christmas caution squeeze spending. Move a fixed portion of each week's profit, even 20 percent, into a separate savings account or a locked savings plan on your banking app. When January arrives, you will have rent, salaries, and restocking covered without panic.

Also watch out for December fraud. Fake transfer alerts and doctored screenshots increase during the festive rush, when staff are hurried and distracted. Train your staff to confirm every transfer in your bank app or by SMS alert before releasing goods, no matter how busy the shop is. A two-minute check is cheaper than losing a ₦80,000 item to a fake alert.

## After Christmas: Clear Stock and Learn

The week between Christmas and New Year is your clearance window. Offer discounts on remaining stock — 20 to 30 percent off, or bundle slow items with popular ones. Post clearance offers on your status daily. The goal is to convert leftover stock into cash before January, when spending drops sharply.

In the first week of January, sit down and review the season. How much did you sell? Which products sold out? Which ones are still on the shelf? What did customers complain about? What would you do differently? Write these answers down in a notebook or a document on your phone. Next Christmas, this review will be the most valuable thing you own, because it is based on your real business, not guesswork.

Finally, thank your customers. A short WhatsApp broadcast on New Year's Day — "Thank you for shopping with us this Christmas. We appreciate you and look forward to serving you in the new year" — costs nothing and keeps the relationship warm. Your Christmas customers are your January customers too, if you treat them well.

Christmas sales reward preparation, not luck. Start early, stock what your customers actually want, price honestly, make buying and delivery easy, and take care of the people who serve your customers. Do that, and December can be the month that funds your business for the first quarter of the new year.
