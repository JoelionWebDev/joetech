Every week in Nigeria, somebody loses money trying to source products. A WhatsApp supplier collects ₦250,000 for sneakers and disappears. A carton of "original" perfumes arrives from Lagos Trade Fair and half the bottles are water. A first-time importer pays a Chinese factory in full, and the goods that arrive look nothing like the pictures.

Sourcing is the quiet part of selling. Customers only see your Instagram page, your prices and your delivery speed. They never see the three weeks you spent finding a supplier who actually delivers what they promised. But that invisible work decides whether your business makes profit or bleeds cash.

## Start With Demand, Not Supply

The biggest sourcing mistake is buying what is cheap instead of what people want. A carton of phone pouches at ₦400 each sounds like a bargain until you realise ten thousand other sellers have the same pouches and nobody is searching for them.

Before you spend one naira on stock, answer four questions:

- **Who exactly will buy this?** "Everybody" is not an answer. "Working-class women in Lekki aged 25 to 40 who buy hair monthly" is an answer.
- **How often will they buy it again?** Products people repurchase — foodstuffs, skincare, baby items, phone accessories — build steadier income than one-off items like wedding gowns.
- **What price will they happily pay?** Ask ten real people, not your friends who will say yes to anything. Check what the same product sells for on Jumia, Konga, Instagram shops and in Balogun Market.
- **What problem does it solve?** A power bank sells because NEPA supply is unreliable and people need their phones alive. A waist trainer sells because of a beauty standard. Know the reason, because that reason becomes your advert.

Do a small test before a big order. Buy ten pieces, sell them, and watch what happens. If they sell out in three days, you have demand. If they sit for three weeks, you have learnt a cheap lesson instead of an expensive one. Many successful sellers in Lagos started with ₦20,000 or ₦50,000 of test stock, not ₦500,000 of hope.

## Where Nigerians Actually Source Products

You do not need to travel to China on your first order. Plenty of reliable supply sits inside Nigeria already. Here are the main options, with honest pros and cons.

### Onitsha Main Market, Anambra State

Onitsha is the largest market in West Africa and the backbone of wholesale trade in Nigeria. If it is sold in Nigeria, somebody in Onitsha probably stocks it: electronics, building materials, shoes, clothes, provisions, plastics, auto parts.

Prices are among the lowest in the country because most importers are based there. The trade-off is that you need to know what you are doing. Go with someone experienced on your first trip, buy from shops with a physical address and a reputation, and never pay a tout who approaches you at the park promising a cheaper deal. Budget for transport and a night in a hotel if you are coming from Lagos or Abuja — a rushed one-day trip leads to rushed decisions.

### Lagos: Balogun, Trade Fair Complex and Computer Village

Balogun Market on Lagos Island is the go-to for fabrics, lace, shoes, bags and fashion items. The Trade Fair Complex in Ojo is strong for electronics, furniture, building materials and imported household goods. Computer Village in Ikeja remains the centre for phones, laptops and accessories.

Lagos markets reward relationships. A trader who knows you will call you when new stock lands and may give you a better price than a stranger. Visit the same shops repeatedly, pay promptly, and keep their numbers saved properly — name, shop address, what they sell. Your phone contact list is a business asset.

### Aba, Abia State

Aba is Nigeria's manufacturing heartland, especially for shoes, belts, bags and clothing. The famous "Aba made" products range from rough to excellent, and the difference is the workshop you choose. If you sell fashion, Aba lets you order custom designs in your own sizes and colours, often at prices that beat imported goods. Order a sample first, check the stitching and finishing yourself, and agree the exact specification in writing on WhatsApp before paying for a bulk run.

### Kano and Kantin Kwari Market

For fabrics, especially Ankara, lace and textiles, Kantin Kwari in Kano is unmatched. Traders from across West Africa buy there. If your business is fabric or fashion, a Kano trip can cut your cost price by 20 to 30 percent compared with buying the same fabric in Lagos. The catch is distance and logistics — factor in waybill costs and delivery time to your base.

### Local manufacturers and farmers

Do not overlook producers in your own state. Nigeria has factories making soap, plastics, packaged food, drinks, furniture and building materials. Buying direct from a manufacturer cuts out the middleman and gives you consistent quality. For foodstuffs — garri, rice, beans, palm oil, yam flour — buying at harvest from farming communities in Benue, Niger, Kogi or Oyo states can be far cheaper than buying from a Lagos retailer. You need storage and transport, but the margins reward the effort.

### Importing from China, Turkey and Dubai

When local supply cannot give you the price or the product you need, importing is the next step. China, through platforms like Alibaba and 1688, supplies most of the world's small goods. Turkey is strong for fashion, shoes, home textiles and furniture. Dubai is popular for electronics, perfumes and phones.

Importing has real costs beyond the product price: shipping, customs duty, clearing agent fees, port charges and delivery from the port to your shop. A product that costs ₦1,000 in Guangzhou can land in Lagos at ₦2,800 after all charges. Always calculate the landed cost before you order — never the factory price alone.

For beginners, use a trusted cargo agent or a procurement agent who consolidates shipments. They handle shipping and clearing for a fee, and you avoid the confusion of dealing with the port yourself. Ask for referrals from sellers who already import, and compare at least three agents before choosing one.

## How to Vet a Supplier Before You Pay a Kobo

Most sourcing losses are not bad luck. They are skipped checks. Run through this list every time you deal with a new supplier, whether they are in Onitsha or Guangzhou.

1. **Confirm they are real.** A real supplier has a physical shop, warehouse or factory you or someone you trust can visit. Ask for a video call inside the shop, showing the goods on the shelves — not a video they downloaded. Ask for their shop address and business name, then check it exists.
2. **Check their track record.** Ask for the names of two or three customers you can call. A genuine supplier will have repeat buyers. Search their phone number and business name on Google and social media. Complaints and scam warnings usually surface quickly.
3. **Start small.** Your first order with any supplier should be small enough that losing it hurts but does not kill the business. ₦50,000 to ₦100,000 is a sensible test range for most small sellers. If the first small order goes well, scale up gradually.
4. **Get a written agreement.** On WhatsApp or email, confirm the product name, quantity, quality, size or colour breakdown, unit price, total price, delivery date and who pays for delivery. Screenshot everything. A supplier who refuses to put terms in writing is telling you something.
5. **Inspect a sample.** For orders above ₦200,000, pay for a sample first. Check the material, stitching, packaging and labelling. If the sample is poor, the bulk order will be worse — samples are usually the best piece they have.
6. **Watch for red flags.** Prices far below every other seller, pressure to pay immediately "before the price changes", refusal to do video calls, only accepting payment to a personal account with a different name, and no address anywhere — any two of these together mean walk away.

## The Payment Rules That Protect Your Money

How you pay matters as much as who you pay. Follow these rules and you will avoid most payment scams in Nigerian trade.

- **Never pay 100 percent upfront to a new supplier.** The standard is a deposit — often 30 to 50 percent — with the balance paid after you confirm the goods are ready or after inspection. For local market purchases, pay on collection where possible.
- **Pay into a business account, not a random personal account.** A supplier with a registered business account, POS or a verifiable company name is more accountable than one who only accepts transfers to a personal account in a different name.
- **Use payment methods with a trail.** Bank transfers leave records. Keep every receipt, chat and invoice. If a dispute ever goes to the police or a market association, your paper trail is your evidence.
- **For imports, use trade assurance or an escrow-style arrangement.** On Alibaba, Trade Assurance gives you a refund path if goods do not ship or do not match. Paying a factory directly by wire transfer to a personal account is the fastest way to lose money.
- **Confirm the account details by voice call.** Scammers hack WhatsApp accounts and send fake account numbers. Before a large transfer, call the supplier on a number you already have and read the account number back to them.
- **Keep business money separate.** Open a separate account for the business, even if it is just a Moniepoint or Kuda business account. Mixing business cash with personal spending is why many sellers cannot tell whether they made profit or loss.

## Shipping, Clearing and Hidden Costs

The price you pay the supplier is only the beginning. Work out the full landed cost before you commit:

**Landed cost = product cost + shipping + customs duty + clearing fees + transport to your shop + packaging**

For goods coming from China by sea, shipping takes 35 to 60 days. By air, 7 to 14 days but far more expensive. If you need goods for December sales, order by September at the latest. Every year, sellers who order in November discover their goods are still at sea on Christmas Day.

Customs duty varies by product category. Some items attract high duty; others are low. Ask your clearing agent for the duty estimate before you ship, not after the goods arrive. A surprise duty bill at the port has forced many small importers to abandon their goods.

For local sourcing, factor in waybill costs. Sending a carton from Onitsha to Lagos by road transport costs a few thousand naira and takes one to two days. Fragile goods need proper packing — budget for it, or you will pay in broken stock instead. Always insure high-value shipments where possible, and photograph goods at the point of dispatch and on arrival.

## Quality Control: Check Before You Sell

Your reputation is built on the quality of what you deliver. One batch of fake or faulty products can destroy months of marketing. Build quality checks into your process:

- **Inspect on arrival.** Open cartons and check a sample from each batch before you list products for sale. Count everything against the waybill or invoice.
- **Test electronics.** Charge phones and power banks, switch on gadgets, test every function. A faulty item discovered by your customer costs you a refund, a bad review and a lost buyer.
- **Check expiry dates.** For food, cosmetics and drugs, check expiry dates on arrival and rotate stock so the oldest sells first. Selling expired goods is not just bad business — it is dangerous and illegal.
- **Keep records of batches.** Note which supplier and which date each batch came from. If customers complain about one batch, you can trace it and stop selling it without guessing.
- **Ask customers for feedback.** A simple WhatsApp message a week after delivery — "Hope the product is serving you well?" — surfaces problems early and builds loyalty at the same time.

If a supplier sends substandard goods, complain immediately with photos and video. Good suppliers replace or refund. If they refuse, stop buying from them. Never sell goods you know are defective at full price and hope nobody notices. Somebody always notices.

## Pricing for Profit, Not Just Sales

Many Nigerian sellers price by copying competitors. That is a mistake — you do not know their cost price, their rent or whether they are even making profit. Price from your own numbers:

1. **Add up your true cost.** Product cost, transport, packaging, delivery, data and airtime used for marketing, and your time. A product that costs ₦2,000 and sells for ₦2,500 is not a ₦500 profit if delivery and packaging cost ₦400.
2. **Set a margin target.** For most retail products, aim for 30 to 50 percent gross margin. Fast-moving consumables can survive on thinner margins because they sell in volume. Slow, high-value items need fatter margins.
3. **Watch the naira.** Exchange rate movements change your cost price overnight. If you import, review prices monthly and adjust quickly. Selling at last month's price with this month's exchange rate is how importers quietly go broke.
4. **Offer clear price tiers.** A basic, standard and premium option helps customers choose and raises your average sale. A skincare seller might offer a single cream, a cream-plus-soap set, and a full routine kit.
5. **Do not compete on price alone.** Somebody will always be cheaper, often because their quality is lower. Compete on trust, packaging, delivery speed and after-sales care — things a price-cutter cannot copy in a day.

Keep a simple price list and update it when costs change. Customers respect a seller whose prices are stable and explained.

## Common Scams and How to Dodge Them

- **The hacked WhatsApp supplier.** A supplier's account is hacked and "they" send a new account number urgently. Always confirm payment details by voice call first.
- **The too-cheap Instagram vendor.** Prices far below market rate, no physical address, only a WhatsApp number, and pressure to pay now. The goods do not exist.
- **The bait-and-switch.** You order original sneakers and receive fakes in original-looking boxes. This is why samples and inspection matter.
- **The clearing agent who vanishes.** An agent collects your duty money and disappears. Use agents recommended by people you trust, and pay duty through traceable channels where possible.
- **The fake waybill.** A seller sends a waybill number that does not exist. Track the waybill with the transport company directly before you confirm receipt to anyone.

If you are scammed, report it to the market association, the police, and your bank immediately. You may not recover the money, but reports protect the next seller. And tell other sellers in your network — silence is what keeps scammers in business.

## Build a Supplier Shortlist You Can Rely On

Sourcing is not a one-time task. It is a system. Keep a simple record — a notebook or Google Sheet — with these columns for every supplier: name, what they sell, phone number, shop address, unit prices, minimum order, delivery time, and a rating from your experience. Update it after every order.

Aim to have at least two suppliers for your bestselling products. If one raises prices suddenly or runs out of stock, the other keeps your business running. Single-supplier dependence is a risk most small businesses do not notice until it bites.

## Your First 30 Days of Sourcing

If you are starting from zero, here is a simple plan:

- **Days 1 to 7:** Pick one product line and research demand. Talk to buyers, check prices online and in a local market, and list five potential suppliers.
- **Days 8 to 14:** Contact the suppliers, compare prices, request samples and verify at least two of them with a visit or video call.
- **Days 15 to 21:** Place a small test order with your best supplier. Inspect on arrival, photograph everything, and list the products for sale.
- **Days 22 to 30:** Sell, collect feedback, note your true costs and profit per item, and decide whether to reorder, adjust the price or switch products.

Sourcing well is a skill, and like any skill it improves with practice. Your first order will feel stressful. Your tenth will feel routine. The sellers who last in Nigeria are rarely the ones with the biggest starting capital — they are the ones who learned to buy right, check carefully and protect their cash.
