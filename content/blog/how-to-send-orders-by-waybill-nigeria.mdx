If you sell anything physical in Nigeria — clothes, shoes, foodstuff, electronics, hair, skincare, building materials — you will eventually need to send goods to a customer in another city. That is where waybill comes in. A waybill is simply the system of sending goods through a transport company, bus park, or logistics firm so that a customer in Lagos, Abuja, Onitsha, Port Harcourt, or Kano can receive what they ordered from you without you travelling there yourself.

For thousands of Nigerian small businesses, waybill is the backbone of interstate sales. Instagram vendors in Lagos send wigs to customers in Enugu. Traders in Onitsha send goods to buyers in Abuja. A foodstuff seller in Ibadan supplies a customer in Port Harcourt. It works, and it works every single day. But it also goes wrong every single day. Goods get lost. Goods arrive damaged. A driver denies receiving the parcel. A customer claims they never got it. Money paid for delivery disappears. The difference between a business that grows through waybill and one that bleeds money through it is almost always the same thing: process.

This guide walks you through the whole process, step by step, the way experienced Nigerian traders actually do it.

## What Waybill Really Means in Nigeria

In formal logistics, a waybill is a document that lists the goods being transported, the sender, the receiver, and the terms of carriage. In everyday Nigerian business, the word means something broader: the entire practice of handing your goods to a transporter — a bus company, a haulage firm, a bike or van logistics company — so they carry it to another town or city where your customer collects it.

There are three main ways Nigerians send goods by waybill:

- **Bus parks and transport companies.** Companies like ABC Transport, God Is Good Motors (GIGM), Peace Mass Transit, GUO Transport, and Chisco run scheduled buses between major cities, and most of them carry parcels. You take your goods to their park, pay the waybill fee, and the customer collects it at the destination park with the waybill number.
- **Dedicated logistics and courier companies.** Companies like GIG Logistics, DHL, Kwik Delivery, Fez Delivery, and many smaller inter-state logistics firms move parcels door to door or park to park. They are usually more expensive than bus parks but offer tracking and better handling.
- **Independent drivers and waybill agents.** At every major park — Ojota, Iyana Ipaja, Jibowu, Onitsha Main Market, Upper Iweka, Wuse — there are agents who load goods onto buses and trucks. This is the cheapest route and the riskiest, because documentation is often informal.

Each route has its place. A ₦8,000 dress going to Abuja can go by bus park. A ₦250,000 laptop going to Port Harcourt probably deserves a tracked courier. A bulk order of 50 cartons going to Onitsha needs a haulage arrangement, not a bus boot. The first skill is matching the value and fragility of the goods to the right channel.

## Step 1: Agree the Delivery Terms With Your Customer Before You Send Anything

Most waybill problems start before the goods ever leave your shop. They start with an agreement that was never made. Before you package anything, settle these five things with the customer on WhatsApp, in writing, so there is a record:

1. **Who pays the waybill fee?** In Nigeria, the buyer usually pays, but not always. Some sellers include delivery in the price. State it clearly: "Waybill to Abuja is ₦5,000, to be paid by you on collection" or "Delivery is included in the price." Never leave it vague.
2. **How much is the waybill fee?** Confirm the exact amount with the transporter before you quote the customer. Prices change. A Lagos to Abuja waybill for a small carton that cost ₦4,000 last month may cost ₦6,000 today because of fuel prices. Quote after you confirm, not before.
3. **When will it arrive?** Give a realistic window, not a promise. "It leaves Lagos tomorrow morning and should arrive in Abuja by evening" is honest. "It will arrive tomorrow by 10am" is a promise you cannot keep, because traffic on the Lagos–Ibadan expressway, a bus breakdown, or a park delay can ruin it.
4. **Who collects it, and with what?** The receiver should collect with a valid ID and the waybill number. If someone else is collecting on their behalf, the customer must tell you the person's name in advance.
5. **What happens if the goods are damaged or lost?** State your policy before payment. Many sellers state that goods are checked and confirmed before dispatch, and that the transporter bears responsibility for damage in transit. Whatever your policy is, say it before money changes hands, not after a problem appears.

Screenshot or save this conversation. If a dispute comes up later, your WhatsApp chat is your evidence.

## Step 2: Confirm Payment Before You Dispatch

This is the rule that saves more Nigerian businesses than any other: do not send goods on trust to a first-time customer. Confirm payment before the goods leave your hands.

For most small businesses, payment comes through bank transfer, Moniepoint, Opay, Palmpay, or a Paystack or Flutterwave payment link. Whichever channel you use, verify the money in your own account before you go to the park. A screenshot of a transfer is not confirmation — screenshots can be faked in minutes. Open your bank app and check the balance yourself.

For high-value orders, consider these safer arrangements:

- **Full payment before dispatch.** This is the standard for most online vendors, and customers who are serious will accept it. Your reputation, reviews, and past deliveries are what make customers comfortable paying first.
- **Payment on delivery through the transporter.** Some transport companies collect payment from the receiver before releasing the goods. Ask your transporter if they offer this. Not all do, and those that do usually charge a fee.
- **Escrow-style arrangement.** For very large orders, a trusted third party or a formal escrow arrangement through a payment provider can protect both sides. This is uncommon for small orders but worth it when the order is ₦500,000 or more.
- **Part payment.** For bulk orders, take a deposit of 50 to 70 percent before dispatch and the balance before the goods are released at the destination. Put this in writing.

Never send goods because a customer says "I will transfer when it arrives" unless you know that customer well and have done business with them before. The goods are your money in physical form. Once they are on a bus to Kano, your leverage is gone.

## Step 3: Package the Goods Like the Road Will Be Rough — Because It Will

Nigerian roads are not gentle on parcels. Your goods will be loaded, unloaded, stacked under heavier cartons, squeezed into a bus boot, and possibly rained on. Package for the worst journey, not the best one.

- **Use a strong carton or bag, not a flimsy nylon.** Double-wall cartons are worth the extra ₦500 to ₦1,500. For clothes and soft goods, a thick poly mailer inside a carton is good protection against rain and dust.
- **Wrap each item separately.** If you are sending three bottles of cream, wrap each one in bubble wrap or old newspaper. Items rubbing against each other in transit is one of the commonest causes of damage.
- **Fill empty space.** A half-empty carton collapses under weight. Fill gaps with paper, foam, or air pillows so the box keeps its shape.
- **Seal with strong tape, all round.** Use proper packing tape, not cello tape. Tape every seam. Then tape round the box in both directions. A well-taped box is harder to tamper with and less likely to burst open.
- **Label clearly on the outside.** Write the receiver's full name, phone number, destination city, and your own name and phone number on the carton with a permanent marker. Then stick a printed label over it too, in case the marker fades. If the carton is going to Onitsha, write "ONITSHA" big and bold.
- **Take photos and a short video before sealing.** Photograph the items, photograph them inside the box, and record a short video as you seal it. Send the photos to the customer on WhatsApp. This takes two minutes and ends most "the item was not complete" arguments before they start.

For foodstuff — garri, crayfish, dried fish, palm oil — use airtight containers inside the carton, and double-bag anything that can leak. Palm oil in a leaking container will ruin everything else in the bus boot, and the transporter will remember you for it.

## Step 4: Choose the Right Transporter for the Route

Not every transporter is good for every route. A company that is excellent on Lagos to Abuja may be poor on Lagos to Makurdi. Ask other traders who use the same route. In markets like Balogun, Onitsha Main Market, and Ariaria, traders know exactly which park and which company delivers reliably to each destination, and which ones lose goods.

When choosing, check these things:

- **Does the company give a receipt with a waybill number?** If the answer is no, walk away. A receipt is the single most important document in waybill. Without it, you have no proof you sent anything.
- **Is there a tracking option?** Bigger companies offer SMS updates or a tracking number you can check. For high-value goods, this is worth paying extra for.
- **What time does the vehicle leave?** If you arrive at the park at 4pm and the last bus left at 2pm, your goods will sit overnight. Ask for departure times and plan around them. Morning dispatch is almost always better than evening dispatch.
- **How is the goods stored?** Ask where the parcel will be kept — in the bus boot, on the roof, or in a separate cargo van. Goods on the roof are exposed to rain and theft. Goods in the boot are safer.
- **What is the claims process?** Ask, before you pay: "If this parcel is lost or damaged, what happens?" A company that has a clear answer is more trustworthy than one that waves the question away. Some companies insure goods up to a value; ask what the limit is.

Build a relationship with one or two reliable transporters on your busiest routes. When the park staff know you, your goods get better treatment and your complaints get taken seriously.

## Step 5: Get the Waybill Receipt and Send the Details to the Customer Immediately

Once you have paid and the goods are loaded, you will receive a waybill receipt. This small piece of paper is your most valuable document for this transaction. Check it before you leave the park:

- Is the receiver's name spelled correctly?
- Is the phone number correct? One wrong digit means the park cannot reach your customer.
- Is the destination correct — Abuja, not Bauchi?
- Is the number of pieces correct? If you sent three cartons, the receipt must say three.
- Is the amount paid written on it?

If anything is wrong, correct it there and then. Once you leave the park, corrections become a long argument.

Then, immediately — not later in the day, immediately — send the customer a WhatsApp message with:

- The waybill number
- The name of the transport company and the park
- The amount paid (if they are paying on collection, state the amount)
- The expected arrival day and time
- The phone number of the park or driver, if you have it
- A photo of the receipt

This message does two things. It tells the customer exactly what to expect, and it creates a dated record that you sent the goods. Customers who have the waybill number rarely panic, and customers who do not have it will call you every hour.

## Step 6: Follow Up Until the Customer Confirms Receipt

Your job is not finished when the bus leaves. It is finished when the customer tells you they have the goods in their hands. Follow up:

- **On the expected arrival day,** message the customer in the morning: "Your goods should arrive today. The waybill number is X. Please let me know when you collect it."
- **If they have not confirmed by evening,** call them. Then call the park. Find out whether the bus arrived and whether the goods were collected. Do not assume.
- **If the customer says the goods have not arrived,** go back to the park with your receipt the next morning. Ask to see the manifest. Ask which bus carried it. Be polite but firm, and be persistent. Goods are found far more often when the sender shows up in person than when they only call.
- **When the customer confirms receipt,** ask them to confirm the items are complete and in good condition. Save that confirmation. That message closes the transaction cleanly.

## What to Do When Goods Go Missing or Arrive Damaged

Even with the best process, things go wrong. A bus is robbed. A carton falls off a roof rack. A park attendant gives your parcel to the wrong person. Here is how to handle it:

1. **Act fast.** Report to the park the same day you discover the problem. The longer you wait, the harder it is to trace the goods. Take your receipt, your photos, and your WhatsApp chat with the customer.
2. **Write a formal complaint.** Ask for the manager or the operations officer. Write down what was sent, the waybill number, the date, and the value. Get the name of the person you spoke to and ask for a reference or complaint number.
3. **Know your rights.** The transporter took your money to deliver goods. If they lost them, they owe you either the goods or compensation. Many transport companies have a claims process and a compensation limit, often tied to the declared value of the goods. This is why declaring the true value matters — if you declare ₦10,000 for goods worth ₦150,000 to save on fees, your compensation will be based on ₦10,000.
4. **Escalate in writing.** If the park office does not resolve it in a few days, write to the company's head office or customer care line on WhatsApp and email. Keep copies of everything.
5. **Involve the customer honestly.** Tell your customer what happened and what you are doing about it. Most reasonable customers will give you time if they see you are fighting for them. Hiding the problem is what destroys trust.
6. **Decide on refund or resend.** If the goods cannot be recovered, you will usually need to refund the customer or send a replacement. This hurts, but it protects your reputation. One angry customer with a viral post can cost you far more than one refund.

## Common Waybill Mistakes Nigerian Sellers Make

- **Sending goods without a receipt.** No receipt, no proof, no claim. Never do it, no matter how well you know the driver.
- **Writing the wrong phone number.** One wrong digit and the park cannot call the receiver. Always read the number back to the customer.
- **Under-declaring value.** It saves a little on fees and costs you everything if the goods are lost.
- **Using a nylon bag for fragile goods.** A black nylon bag is not packaging. It tears, it leaks, and it invites rough handling.
- **Sending on Friday evening for a Monday need.** Weekend schedules are thinner. Goods sent late on Friday may not move until Monday. Plan dispatch days around your customer's deadline.
- **Not telling the customer the collection deadline.** Parks charge demurrage — a storage fee — if goods are not collected within a day or two. Tell your customer to collect promptly, or the fee eats your profit or annoys your customer.
- **Mixing orders.** When sending several customers' goods on the same day, label each carton clearly and keep a written list. Sending Mrs Ade's order to Mr Obi is an avoidable disaster.

## Final Checklist Before You Send Your Next Order

- [ ] Delivery terms agreed with the customer in writing on WhatsApp
- [ ] Payment confirmed in your own bank app
- [ ] Goods packed strongly, labelled clearly, photographed before sealing
- [ ] Transporter chosen and fee confirmed
- [ ] Waybill receipt collected and checked for correct name, number, destination, and pieces
- [ ] Waybill number and receipt photo sent to the customer immediately
- [ ] Follow-up message sent on arrival day
- [ ] Delivery confirmed by the customer and saved

Waybill is not complicated. Send with a receipt, communicate at every step, and treat your customer's trust as the asset it is.
