Walk through any busy market in Lagos, Onitsha, or Kano today and you will see the change. Customers who used to pay only in cash now ask, "Do you have POS?" or "Can I transfer?" A trader who can only take cash loses sales every day, because the customer who has no cash in their pocket will simply walk to the next stall.

The good news is that accepting card and transfer payments is no longer something only big shops can do. A market trader with a small stall can now take payments with a handheld POS terminal or a simple payment link sent on WhatsApp, often with no monthly rent and no bank account from a big commercial bank. The hard part is choosing the right option, because the wrong one will eat your profit in charges, fail when the network is bad, or leave you waiting days for your money.

This guide compares POS terminals and payment links honestly, with real fee examples, so you can pick the setup that fits your stall, your customers, and your pocket.

## Why Cash Alone Is Costing You Sales

Cash feels simple, but it has hidden costs. The first is lost sales. A customer who wants to buy a ₦45,000 bag but has only ₦10,000 in cash and no ATM nearby will not buy today. They may promise to come back tomorrow, but many never do. A POS terminal or a transfer option turns that almost-sale into a sale.

The second cost is safety. Keeping large amounts of cash in a market stall is risky. Traders have been robbed on their way home, and cash can be stolen from a stall during a busy rush. Money received by transfer or card goes straight into your account, where it is far harder to steal.

The third cost is record keeping. Cash leaves no trace. At the end of the week, many traders cannot say exactly how much they sold, because the money mixed with transport fare, food, and family expenses. Payments that pass through a POS or a payment link leave a record, and that record helps you know whether your business is growing or shrinking.

The fourth cost is trust. Customers trust a trader who gives them a printed receipt or a transfer confirmation with the business name on it. It shows that the business is organised and that the customer can come back if there is a problem. Cash-only stalls look less serious, especially to new customers who do not know you yet.

None of this means cash will disappear. Many customers will always pay cash, especially for small items. The goal is not to replace cash, but to add card and transfer options so that no customer ever leaves because they could not pay.

## How a POS Terminal Works at a Market Stall

A POS terminal is a small handheld machine that reads a customer's ATM card and moves money from their bank account to yours. The customer inserts or taps their card, enters their PIN, and the machine prints or shows a receipt. The whole process usually takes less than a minute when the network is good.

Most POS terminals used by Nigerian market traders today come from fintech companies rather than traditional banks. Opay, Palmpay, Moniepoint, and similar providers give traders a terminal after a small deposit or a one-time payment, and some offer the machine free if you meet a transaction target. Traditional banks also offer POS terminals, but their process is often slower, with more paperwork and stricter requirements.

The terminal needs a network connection to work. Most use a SIM card inside the machine, which means they depend on mobile network coverage in your market. Some markets have strong coverage, and some have dead spots. Before you commit to a provider, test the network in your exact stall location with a phone on the same network the terminal will use.

You do not need a shop or a registered company to get a POS terminal from most fintech providers. A business name registration helps, and it builds trust with customers, but many traders start with just their national ID, a BVN, and a passport photograph. The provider opens a wallet or an account in your name, and your settlements go into it.

## How a Payment Link Works

A payment link is a web address that takes your customer to a secure payment page, where they pay with their card, bank transfer, or USSD. You create the link on your phone, send it to the customer on WhatsApp, and they pay from wherever they are. You do not need a machine at all.

Payment links are offered by Paystack, Flutterwave, and other payment companies. You sign up, create a link for a product or a fixed amount, and share it. Some providers let you create a link for a single amount, like ₦15,000 for a pair of shoes, and others let you build a simple storefront page where customers pick from a list of products.

A payment link is ideal for customers who are not standing in front of you. A customer in Abuja who saw your Ankara on WhatsApp status can pay through the link and you send the goods by dispatch. It also works well for pre-orders, deposits, and customers who want to pay before coming to collect.

The main weakness of a payment link is that it needs the customer to have internet on their phone and to be comfortable paying online. Many older customers and customers in a hurry prefer to tap a card on a machine. A payment link also takes longer than a POS transaction at a busy stall, because the customer must open the link, enter their details, and wait for confirmation while other customers wait behind them.

## POS Terminal or Payment Link: Which Fits Your Stall?

The honest answer is that most market traders need both, but they need them in the right proportions. The choice depends on how your customers buy.

Choose a POS terminal as your main option if most of your customers stand in front of you and pay on the spot. This covers foodstuff sellers, fabric sellers, shoe sellers, and phone accessory traders. Speed matters at a busy stall, and a card tap is the fastest way to take money from a person who is already holding the goods.

Choose a payment link as your main option if many of your customers order from a distance, pay deposits in advance, or buy after seeing your products on WhatsApp status. This covers sellers of aso-ebi, custom cakes, furniture, and wholesale goods. It also suits traders who sell at several markets on different days, because a link travels with you while a machine needs charging and network.

Many successful traders carry both. The POS terminal handles walk-in customers at the stall. The payment link handles WhatsApp customers, deposits, and deliveries. The two together mean you almost never have to say, "I cannot collect that payment."

Think about your average sale too. A trader selling items of ₦500 to ₦2,000, like pepper, tomatoes, or small provisions, may find a POS terminal slow for the volume of tiny sales, and the charges on each small transaction add up. Such a trader may be better off with transfers to a bank account for bigger purchases and cash for the small ones. A trader selling items of ₦10,000 and above, like fabrics, electronics, or bags, will recover the charges easily, because one lost sale costs far more than the fee.

## Understanding the Fees Before You Sign Up

Every provider charges something, and the differences matter when you add them up over a month. Read the charges before you collect a terminal or create a link, and do not rely on what an agent tells you verbally.

For POS terminals, the main charge is a percentage of each transaction. Many fintech providers charge around 0.5 percent, often with a cap of about ₦1,000 per transaction. That means on a ₦20,000 sale, you pay about ₦100. On a ₦200,000 sale, you pay the capped ₦1,000 rather than a full percentage. Some bank terminals charge more, and some charge a daily or monthly rent on top of the percentage. Ask for the full list of charges in writing.

For payment links, the standard charge in Nigeria is around 1.5 percent of the transaction, plus a flat fee of about ₦100, and that flat fee is often waived for smaller transactions under ₦2,500. On a ₦15,000 payment, that works out to about ₦325. That is higher than a POS charge, which is why payment links are best used for remote orders and deposits, not for every small sale at the stall.

Do a simple calculation for your own business. Take your average daily sales through card and link, multiply by the percentage, and see what it costs you in a month. Then compare that with the sales you would lose without the service. For most traders selling goods above ₦5,000, the fees are far smaller than the lost sales, and the choice is easy.

## Network, Power, and Other Real Market Problems

A POS terminal is only useful if it works when a customer is ready to pay. Market conditions in Nigeria make this harder than it sounds.

Network is the biggest problem. Many markets have poor mobile coverage, especially inside large concrete buildings or in crowded areas where thousands of phones compete for the same signal. Before you choose a provider, stand in your stall and test the network. Send a WhatsApp message, load a web page, and make a test transaction if possible. If one network fails in your stall, choose a provider whose terminal uses a different network, or ask whether the terminal can connect to Wi-Fi from a nearby shop.

Power is the second problem. A POS terminal has a battery, but it needs charging. Charge it fully every night, and carry a power bank in your stall bag for long market days. A dead terminal at 4pm, when the evening rush begins, is as useless as no terminal at all. Some traders keep two terminals from different providers, so that if one has no network or no battery, the other can still take the payment.

Always give a receipt or a clear confirmation. A printed slip from the terminal, or a screenshot of the successful transfer, protects both you and the customer. If there is a dispute later, that record is your evidence. Traders who give receipts are trusted more, and they lose fewer arguments.

## Choosing a Provider You Can Rely On

There are many POS and payment providers in Nigeria, and new ones appear regularly. Choosing well matters, because switching providers later means new paperwork and a new learning curve.

Look first at the provider's presence in your area. A provider with agents, an office, or a support team near your market can help you faster when a terminal breaks or money is stuck. A provider that only exists online may be cheaper, but help can be slow.

Look at the settlement speed. Some providers settle the same day, some the next working day. If you restock daily, same-day or next-morning settlement matters. Ask other traders in your market which provider pays fastest, because their experience is more reliable than an advert.

Look at the support. Save the provider's support number in your phone before you need it. Test it once with a small question. If nobody answers, or the answer takes days, that is a warning. You will need support one day, probably at the worst possible time, and a provider with no support is a provider you cannot rely on.

Do not choose a provider only because an agent offered you a free terminal or a cash bonus. A free machine from a provider with high charges and slow settlement will cost you more in a month than a paid machine from a good provider. Compare the total cost, not the sign-up gift.

## Staying Safe from Fraud

Accepting electronic payments brings new risks, and market traders are a target. A few habits will protect you.

Never hand over goods before the payment is confirmed. A customer who shows you a transfer alert on their phone may be showing you a fake alert, and this is a common trick. Wait for your own alert, or check your balance on your provider's app, before you give the goods. Your own notification is the only proof that counts.

Be careful with card transactions where the customer claims the machine is faulty and asks you to try again and again. Multiple attempts can lead to multiple debits, and the argument that follows will cost you time and customers. One attempt, then a check of your balance, then a decision.

Keep your terminal and your phone safe. Do not lend your terminal to another trader, and do not let a customer hold it and walk away with it. Set a PIN on the terminal if the provider allows it, and keep the provider's support number saved so you can block a stolen terminal quickly.

Separate your business money from your personal money. Use the provider's wallet or a separate bank account for business settlements, and move your personal spending out of it deliberately, rather than spending from the same pot. This simple habit makes it possible to know whether your business is making a profit, and it protects you if your account is ever questioned.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What happens if a customer's card is debited but my POS shows failed?

Do not ask the customer to pay again straight away. Check your transaction history and balance on the provider's app. In most cases the money reverses to the customer within 24 hours. If it does not, report it to the provider with the transaction reference, the date, the amount, and the customer's bank. Keep the receipt.

### Can I use a payment link if I do not have a website?

Yes. A payment link does not need a website. You create the link in the Paystack or Flutterwave dashboard on your phone, and share it on WhatsApp. Some providers also give you a simple storefront page, but a single link for a single amount is enough to start.

### How much does a POS terminal cost a market trader?

It varies. Some fintech providers give the terminal free after a refundable deposit of around ₦20,000 to ₦30,000, or after you reach a transaction target. Others sell it outright for ₦35,000 to ₦60,000. Always add the transaction charges to the cost of the machine when you compare providers, because the charges matter far more over time than the price of the machine.

## What to Do Next

1. Test the mobile network in your exact stall today, and ask three traders near you which POS provider they use and whether the money settles fast.
2. Compare two providers on paper: the percentage charge, any cap, the settlement time, and the cost of the terminal. Pick the one with the lowest total cost, not the biggest sign-up gift.
3. Get your terminal or create your first payment link this week, and tell your customers: put a small sign on your stall that says you accept cards and transfers, and add it to your WhatsApp Business profile.
