A woman standing on Admiralty Way in Lekki Phase 1, phone in hand, types "braids near me" into Google. Her phone knows where she is. Google shows her three salons on a little map, with star ratings, photos, and how many minutes away each one is. She taps the first one, sees braids from ₦15,000, reads two reviews, and calls. She never saw the salon two streets away with better braids and cheaper prices, because that salon is not on Google Maps at all. As far as her phone is concerned, it does not exist.

That is the situation for thousands of Nigerian businesses. The work is good, the shop is open, but the customer searching on her phone cannot find them, so she finds someone else. This guide is about fixing that — the simple work of making Google show your business when a nearby customer searches for what you sell, and making her pick you over the two other names on the map.

## What "near me" actually means

When a customer types "pharmacy near me", Google is really asking: which pharmacies are close to this phone, open right now, and trusted enough to show first? The answer is a map pack — three businesses at the top of the results, above every website. Those three get the calls. The fourth business, just below the map, gets almost nothing.

Three things decide who lands in that pack: relevance, distance, and prominence. Relevance is whether your profile matches the search. Distance is how close you are to the person searching — you cannot change that one. Prominence is how well known and trusted Google thinks you are. You can change relevance and prominence, and the changes are mostly small, specific things: the exact category you pick, the words in your description, the photos you upload, the reviews you collect. None of it is difficult. All of it is easy to skip, which is why most listings in Lagos are half-finished and most map packs are won by the businesses that bothered to finish theirs.

## The category is the single most important choice

If you take one thing from this guide, take this: your primary category decides which searches you appear for, and most Nigerian businesses pick the wrong one. A woman who sells wigs, braids hair, and does makeup picks "Beauty Salon" because it covers everything — and Google shows her for almost nothing, because a thousand other businesses picked it too. The woman who picks "Hair Salon" first, with "Wig Shop" as an extra, shows up when someone searches "hair salon near me".

The fix is to look at what your customers type, not what your business card says. Search the way a customer would — "phone repair in Computer Village", "cake shop in Surulere" — and read the category under each of the three businesses in the map pack. Those categories are your answer. Pick the category that describes the service that brings in the most money, not the one you enjoy most. You can add up to nine extra categories afterwards, but the first one carries the most weight. A tailor in Yaba changed his primary category from "Clothing Store" to "Fashion Designer" and his profile views doubled within a month. Same shop, same phone number, same photos. One dropdown.

## Your address is a ranking decision, not an admin detail

Google uses your address to decide how close you are to the searcher, so it has to be exact — street number, street name, area, city. "14 Admiralty Way, Lekki Phase 1, Lagos" is an address Google can place on a map. "Lekki, Lagos" is a guess, and Google does not rank guesses. If your shop sits inside a plaza with a known name, add that too — customers navigate by landmarks in Lagos, and Google's map pin will sit in the right place.

If you run your business from home and do not want your house address public, do not invent an address. Google suspends profiles with fake addresses, and a suspension takes weeks to fix. Use the service-area option instead: hide the street address and list the areas you actually serve — Lekki, Victoria Island, Ajah. But be honest: list only the areas you can reach in under an hour. A makeup artist in Surulere who lists the whole of Lagos will get calls from Epe she cannot honour, and the disappointed customers leave one-star reviews.

## Photos that rank are photos that prove you are real

Google ranks profiles with good photos higher, and customers choose with their eyes before they read a single word. But the photos that rank are not the polished ones. They are the real ones. A photo of your actual shopfront, taken in daylight, with your signboard readable, tells Google and the customer the same thing: this business exists, at this address, and it is open.

Upload at least ten photos, and make them specific. Your exterior, so a customer driving down the road recognises the building. Your interior, so she knows what to expect when she walks in. Your team at work — a barber cutting hair, a baker pulling trays from the oven, a mechanic under a bonnet. Your best-selling items, photographed in daylight. Never use stock photos downloaded from the internet — they destroy trust the moment a customer walks in and sees something different.

Photograph things the way they actually look. A restaurant in Ikate should photograph its jollof rice the way it leaves the kitchen, not a dish it has never served. A fashion designer should photograph a dress on a real customer, with permission, because a dress on a person sells the fit in a way a dress on a hanger never will.

Add new photos every month. Fresh photos tell Google the business is alive. A profile whose newest photo is two years old looks closed, even when the gate is open. Ten minutes with a phone camera, once a month, is enough.

## Reviews are the ranking engine

Here is the uncomfortable truth about the map pack: the business with 60 genuine reviews and 4.7 stars will beat the business with no reviews almost every time, even if the second business is cheaper, closer, and better. Google reads reviews as proof that real customers used the business and left happy. A stranger will call a salon with 40 reviews before she calls one with none.

The way to get reviews is to ask at the exact moment the customer is happiest, and to make it easy. A tailor hands over a finished dress and the customer is smiling at the mirror — that is the moment. A phone repair shop hands back a working phone — that is the moment. One sentence, with the link ready: "Thank you for choosing us. If you have a minute, please leave us a review on Google — it helps our small business a lot." The review link lives in your profile dashboard under "Ask for reviews". Send it by WhatsApp while the customer is still in front of you.

Aim for a steady flow, not a flood. Five genuine reviews a week for two months beats 40 reviews in one day, which looks suspicious to Google. Never pay for reviews, never ask staff or family to write fake ones. Google removes fake reviews and can suspend the profile.

And reply to every review, good and bad, by name, mentioning the service. "Thank you, Mrs Adeyemi, we are glad the braids came out well" tells the next reader that a real person runs this business and remembers her customers. A calm, helpful reply to a bad review — an apology, an invitation to call you directly — builds more trust than ten five-star reviews.

## Your first week after setup

Most profiles fail in the first week, not the first year. The owner sets it up, gets verified, and forgets it exists. A profile that has not been touched since setup looks abandoned, and Google slowly stops showing it.

On day one, complete every section. Not most of them — every one. The description, in plain language, saying what you do, who you serve, and what makes you different: "Fresh cakes, pastries and small chops baked daily in Lekki Phase 1. We deliver across Lagos for birthdays, weddings and office events. Order on WhatsApp and get same-day delivery within Lekki." The services, with prices where you can give them — "Braids from ₦15,000, wig installation ₦10,000, pedicure ₦8,000" — because prices answer the first question every customer asks. The attributes too: wheelchair accessible, free Wi-Fi, accepts card payments, women-led. These appear in search filters.

On day two, upload your ten photos and your logo. On day three, ask your five happiest customers for reviews — the regulars, the ones who already tell their friends about you. On day four, answer the questions customers always ask before they ask them: post "Do you deliver to Abuja?" and "What time do you close on Sundays?" in your profile's Questions and Answers section, and answer them from your owner account. On day five, check your insights: how many people found you, what they searched for, whether they called or asked for directions.

That is the whole first week. No adverts, no tricks. A complete profile, ten real photos, five review requests, a few answered questions, and one look at the numbers. Do that, and your business is no longer invisible. It is findable.

## The searches your customers actually type

It helps to think in the customer's words, not yours. Nobody types "establishment providing comprehensive hair solutions in the Lekki axis". They type "braids near me", "cheap braids Lekki", "salon open now", "best tailor in Yaba", "phone repair Computer Village", "cake shop Surulere", "hotel in Ikeja with pool". Short phrases, an area name, sometimes "near me", sometimes "open now".

Your profile has to contain those words in the places Google looks: the category, the services list, the description, and the reviews. If you want to rank for "wedding cakes in Abuja", your profile must say wedding cakes, list wedding cakes as a service, and have reviews that mention wedding cakes. A customer who writes "the wedding cake was beautiful" in her review has just done your ranking work for you.

## What to do when you are still not showing up

If a month passes and you still do not appear, check four things. First, search your exact business name. If Google cannot find you by your own name, the problem is verification or a duplicate listing — claim the existing profile if one already exists, and never create a second one. Duplicates split your reviews and confuse Google.

Second, check your map pin. Open Google Maps and look at where it sits. A pin two streets away means Google thinks you are two streets away, and the customer near you is being shown your competitor instead.

Third, check your hours. If Google thinks you are closed when a customer searches at 10am on Saturday, you will not appear in "open now" results. Update holiday hours too — Christmas, Sallah, Easter.

Fourth, check your phone number. Call it yourself. If it rings on a phone nobody answers, or if it is an old number you stopped using, fix it everywhere the same day: Google, WhatsApp Business, Instagram, your website. A customer who calls a dead number does not try again. She calls the next name on the map.
