## The Savings System Older Than the Banks

Long before bank loans and fintech apps, Nigerian traders had a system that worked. Every market day, traders in Balogun, Onitsha Main Market, Bodija, and Mile 12 would set aside a fixed amount of money, hand it to a trusted collector, and wait for their turn to collect the lump sum. That system is ajo, also called esusu, adashe, or contribution, depending on where you live. It still works today, and for many small businesses, it is still the most reliable way to raise a lump sum.

Funke sells fabric in Balogun Market. She earns a modest profit each day, but her money disappears into food, transport, and small daily needs before she can save enough to buy a new bale of lace. Two years ago, she joined an ajo group of ten traders, each contributing ₦20,000 every week. When her turn came in the tenth week, she collected ₦200,000 at once. That single payout bought her a bale she could never have saved for on her own, and the bale sold out in three weeks. The system did not give her free money. It gave her discipline and a lump sum at the right time.

## What Ajo and Esusu Really Mean

Ajo and esusu describe the same idea: a group of people agree to contribute a fixed amount at a fixed interval, and each member takes the full collection in turn until everyone has collected once. The group can be small, with five members, or large, with fifty. Contributions can be daily, weekly, or monthly. The interval and the amount are agreed at the start, and they do not change until the cycle ends.

In a daily ajo, a market trader might contribute ₦2,000 every day for 30 days, collecting ₦60,000 at the end of her turn. In a weekly group, ten shop owners might contribute ₦10,000 each week, so each member collects ₦100,000 when their turn comes. In a monthly group, salary earners and business owners might contribute ₦50,000 a month for ten months, collecting ₦500,000 in their month. The maths is simple, and that simplicity is part of the appeal. There is no interest, no form, and no collateral.

There are two main forms. In the first, members contribute to each other in rotation, and the person who collects is simply taking the turn that belongs to them. In the second, a collector, sometimes called an ajo agent or alajo, collects a daily amount from a trader and returns the total at the end of the month, keeping one day's contribution as a fee. Both forms are common in Nigerian markets, and both can work well when the people involved are honest and the rules are clear.

The names change by region and language. Yoruba traders say ajo or esusu. Hausa traders say adashe. Igbo traders say isusu. In the south-south, people say contribution. The names differ, but the discipline is the same: small, regular contributions that become a useful lump sum.

## Why Ajo Works for Small Businesses

The biggest advantage of ajo is forced discipline. Saving alone is hard when daily needs keep calling. A trader who plans to save ₦5,000 a day often ends the day with ₦1,500 left, because transport, food, and a family request took the rest. In an ajo group, the contribution is not optional. The collector arrives, the group expects the money, and the trader pays first and manages the rest of the day with what is left. That pressure, applied gently and regularly, is what turns small daily earnings into real capital.

Ajo also gives you a lump sum at a known date. That matters for business planning. If you know your turn falls in the third week of December, you can plan to buy Christmas stock with it. If your turn falls in March, you can plan to buy a freezer before the hot season. A bank savings account gives you access to your money any time, which sounds good until you realise it also means you can spend it any time. Ajo locks the money away until your turn, and that lock is a feature, not a problem.

There is a social advantage too. A good ajo group is a network of business people who share information. Traders in the same group tell each other which supplier has the best price this week, which road is blocked, and which product is selling fast. A new trader who joins a group of experienced sellers gains advice worth more than the money itself. Emeka, who sells phone accessories in Ikeja Computer Village, says his ajo group taught him more about pricing than any course he ever attended.

## How to Choose a Group You Can Trust

Trust is the whole system. If the people in your group are honest and committed, ajo works beautifully. If one person defaults, the whole cycle suffers. Choosing the right group is the most important decision you will make, and it deserves more care than choosing the amount.

Start with people you know. The safest groups are made of people whose businesses you can see: fellow traders in your market, shop owners on your street, or members of your trade association. You can see whether their shops are open, whether they sell steadily, and whether they keep their word in small things. A group of strangers who met online is riskier than a group of traders who have shared the same market for five years.

Ask questions before you join. How long has the group been running? Has anyone ever defaulted, and what happened? Who keeps the money between collection and payout? Is there a written list of members, amounts, and turn order? A group that cannot answer these questions clearly is not ready for your money. A group that has run three full cycles without a problem is a group worth joining.

Check the collector if there is one. A good alajo is known in the market, has an office or a fixed stall, and can name traders who have saved with them for years. Be careful of a collector who promises very high returns, who has no fixed address, or who pressures you to join today. A genuine collector earns a small, agreed fee and is happy for you to ask questions first.

## How Much Should You Contribute?

The right contribution is one you can pay every time, even on a slow week, without borrowing to meet it. That sounds simple, but many traders get it wrong by choosing an amount based on a good week instead of an average week.

Begin by looking at your real daily profit, not your sales. If you sell ₦30,000 worth of goods in a day but your profit after buying stock, transport, and shop costs is ₦6,000, your contribution must come from that ₦6,000. A good rule is to contribute no more than one-third of your average daily profit to a daily ajo, or no more than one-quarter of your weekly profit to a weekly ajo. That leaves room for slow days, for NEPA bills, for generator fuel, and for the unexpected.

Work out the numbers on paper before you commit. Suppose your shop makes an average profit of ₦35,000 a week. A weekly contribution of ₦10,000 is about a quarter of that profit, and it is manageable. In a group of ten, your payout will be ₦100,000 when your turn comes. That is enough to buy a useful amount of stock, a small freezer, or a batch of packaging materials. If you contribute ₦20,000 a week from the same profit, you will struggle in slow weeks, and one missed payment can damage your standing in the group.

## Setting the Rules Before the First Contribution

Every successful ajo group has rules that everyone knows and agrees to before the first naira is collected. Write them down, even if the group is informal. A written list, shared on WhatsApp and kept by the group leader, prevents most disputes.

The rules should cover the amount, the interval, the turn order, the start and end dates, and what happens if someone misses a payment. Decide how turns are chosen: by ballot, by seniority, or by need, with the person who needs the money most taking an early turn by agreement. Decide whether the first turn goes to the organiser, as is common in some groups, or is drawn by lot like every other turn. State it clearly so nobody feels cheated later.

## How to Use Your Payout to Grow the Business

The payout is the moment the whole system pays off, and it is also the moment most people waste it. A lump sum of ₦100,000 or ₦500,000 feels like a windfall, and the temptation is to spend it on a celebration, a new phone, or a family event. That is the fastest way to turn a year of discipline into nothing. Decide what the payout is for before your turn arrives.

The best uses are the ones that earn money back. Buying stock in bulk is the most common and often the most profitable. A trader who buys a bale of fabric for ₦200,000 instead of buying pieces for ₦20,000 at a time will usually pay less per piece and earn more per sale. A food seller who buys a bag of rice and a carton of oil at wholesale prices will cut costs for weeks. Bulk buying only works if you buy goods you already sell well, so do not use your payout to try a product you have never sold.

Equipment is the second best use. A freezer for a drinks seller, a sewing machine for a tailor, a generator for a shop in an area with poor power supply, or a good phone for taking product photos can all pay for themselves. Choose equipment that saves you money or earns you money every week. A freezer that lets you sell cold drinks all day in Lagos heat is an investment. A television for the shop is not.

Paying off expensive debt is another strong use. If you owe a supplier or a lender who charges high interest, using your payout to clear that debt frees your future profits. It is less exciting than new stock, but it is often the smartest move, because it removes a cost that was eating your business every week.

## Risks, Warning Signs, and How to Protect Yourself

Ajo works on trust, and trust can be broken. Knowing the risks does not mean you should avoid ajo. It means you should join with your eyes open and protect yourself with simple habits.

The biggest risk is default. A member takes an early payout, then stops contributing, leaving the rest of the group to cover the gap. This is why turn order matters: the first person to collect has the least incentive to keep paying. Groups handle this in different ways. Some require the first collectors to be the most trusted members. Some ask early collectors to provide a guarantor, often a fellow trader who will cover a missed payment. Some require a small deposit before the cycle starts. Any of these measures is better than none.

Watch for warning signs. A group that changes its rules mid-cycle, a leader who refuses to show the ledger, a member who always pays late but still expects an early turn, or a collector who promises returns far above what the contributions can produce are all reasons to pause. If something feels wrong, ask questions. If the answers are vague or hostile, leave the group before the next contribution, and collect what you have already paid if the rules allow it.

## Digital Ajo and Modern Alternatives

Ajo is no longer only a market-day activity. Several Nigerian fintech apps and cooperatives now offer digital ajo, where you join a group on your phone, contribute by bank transfer or card, and receive your payout into your account. The system is the same, but the record-keeping is automatic and the money is held by the platform rather than by one person.

Digital ajo suits sellers who work online, who live far from a market, or who want a clear digital trail of their savings. It also suits salary earners who want to save with colleagues without carrying cash. Before joining a digital platform, check that it is a registered company, read reviews from real users, and confirm how payouts are made and what fees are charged. A platform that is vague about fees or about who holds the money deserves the same caution as a collector with no fixed address.

Cooperative societies are another modern form. Many trade associations and market associations run cooperatives where members save regularly and can borrow at low interest. Joining your market association's cooperative can give you access to both savings and loans, and to a community that understands your business. Ask your association leaders what is available; many traders never ask, and miss a benefit that is already there.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the difference between ajo and esusu?

They describe the same savings system. Ajo and esusu are Yoruba terms, adashe is the Hausa term, and isusu is the Igbo term. In all cases, a group contributes a fixed amount at a fixed interval, and each member takes the full collection in turn. The name changes by region, but the rules and the discipline are the same.

### How much should I contribute to an ajo group?

Contribute no more than one-quarter of your average weekly profit to a weekly group, or one-third of your average daily profit to a daily group. Choose an amount you can pay even in a slow week without borrowing. Start small in a new group, and increase the amount only after a full cycle runs smoothly.

### Is ajo safe?

Ajo is safe when the group is honest and the rules are clear. The main risks are a member defaulting after an early payout and a dishonest collector. Reduce the risk by joining a group of people you know, checking the group's history, keeping your own records, and starting with a small amount. Digital platforms and registered cooperatives can add an extra layer of safety.

### What happens if someone in my group stops paying?

The group should follow the rule it agreed at the start. Common approaches are for the defaulter's guarantor to cover the payment, for the group to replace the member, or for the remaining members to share the shortfall. This is why written rules and a guarantor for early collectors matter so much. Discuss default before the cycle starts, not after it happens.

### Can I join more than one ajo group at the same time?

Yes, but be careful. Two groups are manageable for most small businesses. Three or more can strain your cash flow, especially in slow weeks. Make sure the total of all your contributions still leaves enough profit for restocking, transport, and daily needs. If you must choose, one reliable group is better than three shaky ones.

### Are digital ajo apps better than traditional market ajo?

Digital ajo offers automatic records, bank-held funds, and convenience for online sellers. Traditional market ajo offers a personal network, flexible timing, and advice from fellow traders. Neither is better for everyone. Choose the form that fits how you work, and check any platform's registration, fees, and reviews before you join.

## What to Do Next

1. Work out your average weekly profit after all costs, and choose a contribution that is no more than a quarter of it.
2. Ask three trusted traders or colleagues about the ajo groups they belong to, and note which groups have run several smooth cycles.
3. Before joining, ask for the written rules: amount, interval, turn order, start and end dates, and what happens on default.
4. Start small in your first cycle, keep your own record of every payment, and save all transfer receipts and confirmations.
5. Decide in writing what your payout is for, such as stock, equipment, or clearing a debt, before your turn arrives.
6. When your turn comes, follow the plan, keep personal spending to a small planned amount, and record what the payout earned.
7. Review the cycle when it ends, and only then decide whether to increase your contribution or join a second group.
8. Consider a registered cooperative or a reputable digital ajo platform if you want a digital record or a larger, more formal group.
