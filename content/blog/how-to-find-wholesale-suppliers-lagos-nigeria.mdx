Every profitable small business in Lagos has one thing in common: a good supplier. The price you buy at decides the price you can sell at, and the price you sell at decides whether you make a profit or slowly bleed money. Two shops on the same street can sell the same rice, the same shoes, or the same phone accessories, and one will thrive while the other closes — often because one found a supplier who sells cheaper, delivers on time, and stocks genuine goods.

Lagos is the best place in Nigeria to find wholesale suppliers, because the biggest markets in West Africa are here. But the size of the opportunity is also the problem. There are thousands of traders, agents, and middlemen, and telling a genuine wholesaler from a person who will take your money and disappear takes knowledge. This guide shows you where to find wholesale suppliers in Lagos, how to check that they are genuine, how to negotiate prices, and how to build a supply relationship that keeps your business stocked and profitable.

## Start by Knowing Exactly What You Need

Before you visit any market or contact any supplier, write down exactly what you need. Which products, which brands, which sizes, which quantities, and how often will you reorder? A supplier who sells Ankara fabric is not the same as one who sells lace, and a supplier who sells iPhone accessories may not sell Samsung parts. The more specific you are, the faster you will find the right person and the less time you will waste talking to traders who cannot help you.

Work out your target buying price too. If you sell a handbag for ₦15,000 and you want a 40 percent margin after delivery and other costs, you need to buy it for around ₦8,000 or less. Knowing your target price before you negotiate stops you from agreeing to a price that feels cheap in the market but leaves you no profit after transport, packaging, and the occasional damaged item.

Also decide your minimum order. Some wholesalers in Lagos will sell you as few as six pieces, while others only deal in bales, cartons, or dozens. If you are starting with ₦100,000, a supplier who insists on a ₦500,000 minimum order is not your supplier yet — find one whose minimum matches your capital, and move up as your business grows.

## The Big Wholesale Markets in Lagos

The fastest way to find suppliers is to walk the markets where they already trade. Each major Lagos market specialises in different goods, and knowing which market to visit saves you days of wandering.

Balogun Market on Lagos Island is the biggest market for fabrics, lace, Ankara, shoes, bags, and fashion accessories in West Africa. Traders come from all over Nigeria and neighbouring countries to buy here. Prices vary from shop to shop on the same street, so never buy from the first shop you enter. Walk the whole row, compare prices for the same item in at least five shops, and then negotiate.

Idumota Market, also on Lagos Island, is the centre for wholesale provisions, foodstuffs, and household goods. If you sell rice, vegetable oil, noodles, drinks, or toiletries, this is where your suppliers are. Many of the big distributors here supply shops across Lagos and the South-West, and they offer better prices for bulk purchases paid in cash.

Computer Village in Ikeja is the place for phones, phone accessories, laptops, and electronics. It is also the place where fake products are most common, so the verification steps in this guide matter most here. Buy from shops with a fixed address and a reputation, not from hawkers carrying goods in their hands, and always test electronics before you pay.

Mile 12 Market is the main wholesale market for foodstuffs — yams, tomatoes, peppers, onions, and grains — supplied from farms across the country. If you run a food business, a restaurant, or a provision store, building a relationship with a Mile 12 trader can cut your costs sharply, especially if you buy early in the morning when the fresh stock arrives.

Oyingbo Market is strong for foodstuffs and provisions too, and is a good alternative if Mile 12 is too far from you. Trade Fair Complex in Ojo is the hub for imported goods — building materials, electronics, and household items brought in by importers. Alaba International Market, also in Ojo, is the largest electronics market in West Africa and the source for many of the gadgets sold across Nigeria.

Tejuosho Market in Yaba is a good source for fabrics, clothing, and fashion items, and is often less crowded than Balogun. Yaba also has a busy market for fairly used clothes, known as okrika, which many fashion sellers buy, sort, and resell at a profit.

## How to Find Suppliers Online

You do not have to visit a market for every purchase, especially once you know what you want. Several online platforms connect Nigerian retailers with wholesalers. Jumia and Konga have wholesale sections and business seller programmes, and their prices are useful as a benchmark even if you eventually buy in a physical market. Instagram is also a major sourcing channel — many Lagos wholesalers run Instagram pages showing their stock, prices, and delivery terms. Search for terms like "wholesale Lagos", "wholesale bags Lagos", or "Ankara wholesale" and check the accounts carefully before you pay anyone.

WhatsApp groups and Telegram channels for traders exist for almost every product category, from hair to shoes to foodstuffs. These can be useful, but they are also full of scammers, so never pay a supplier you found in a group until you have verified them using the steps below. Facebook groups for Nigerian traders work the same way.

For imported goods, platforms like Alibaba connect you directly with manufacturers in China and Turkey. The prices look attractive, but remember to add shipping, customs duty, and clearing costs before you compare with a Lagos market price. A product that costs ₦2,000 on Alibaba can cost ₦5,000 by the time it reaches your shop in Lekki. For most small businesses, buying from an importer at Trade Fair or Alaba is cheaper and faster than importing a small quantity yourself.

## How to Verify a Supplier Before You Pay

This is the step most new business owners skip, and it is the step that costs them the most money. Never send money to a supplier you have not verified, no matter how good the price looks.

First, visit their shop or warehouse in person if you can. A genuine wholesaler has a fixed address, a shop or warehouse with stock on the shelves, and staff. Take note of the shop number, the market, and the name on the signboard. If the supplier only meets you in a car park, a hotel, or a roadside, be careful.

Second, check their business registration. Ask for their CAC registration number and the business name, and confirm that the bank account you are paying into carries that business name or the owner's real name. A supplier who insists you pay into a different person's account, a POS agent, or a betting wallet is a red flag.

Third, start small. Place a small test order first — six pieces, one carton, one bale — and check the quality, the delivery time, and the supplier's communication. If the first order goes well, increase your orders gradually. A supplier who is genuine will understand a test order. A scammer will pressure you to place a big order immediately.

Fourth, ask for references. A good supplier has regular customers. Ask for two or three shop owners who buy from them, and call those people. You can also ask in your trader WhatsApp groups whether anyone has dealt with the supplier. The Lagos trading community is small, and a supplier's reputation travels fast.

Fifth, inspect the goods before you pay in full. For fabrics, check the material and the length. For electronics, test them. For foodstuffs, check the expiry dates and the packaging. If you are buying in bulk, open a random sample of cartons — not just the ones the seller shows you, because the top layer can be genuine while the bottom is fake or damaged.

## How to Negotiate Prices Like a Trader

Negotiation is expected in Lagos markets. The first price you hear is almost never the final price, and paying it without negotiating means you are leaving profit on the table. But good negotiation is not about fighting — it is about information and relationships.

The most powerful negotiating tool is comparison. When you know that five shops on the same row sell the same Ankara for ₦12,000, ₦11,500, ₦11,000, ₦10,500, and ₦10,000, you can negotiate from a position of knowledge. Walk the market, collect prices, and then go back to the shop with the best combination of price and reliability. Tell them what others are charging, politely, and ask them to match or beat it.

Volume is the second tool. Suppliers give better prices to buyers who buy more and buy regularly. If you can commit to buying twenty cartons a month instead of five, say so, and ask for the bulk price. If you cannot afford a big order alone, consider joining with another small business owner to buy together and share the discount.

Cash is the third tool. Many Lagos wholesalers prefer cash or instant bank transfer, and they will give a discount for immediate payment because it improves their own cash flow. If you pay cash, ask for a discount — even 3 to 5 percent adds up over a year.

Timing is the fourth tool. Prices in Lagos markets change with the season, the exchange rate, and the day of the week. Fabrics are often cheaper just after a big import lands, foodstuffs are cheaper at harvest time, and electronics prices move with the dollar. If you can buy when supply is high, you will pay less.

Finally, negotiate the whole deal, not just the price. Ask about free delivery for large orders, replacement for damaged goods, credit terms after you have built trust, and whether they can hold stock for you. A supplier who replaces a damaged carton without argument is worth more than one who is ₦200 cheaper per carton but refuses to take responsibility.

## Building a Relationship with Your Supplier

The cheapest supplier is not always the best supplier. A supplier who delivers on time, answers your calls, replaces faulty goods, and warns you when prices are about to rise is worth keeping, even if another trader is slightly cheaper. Reliability is what keeps your shop stocked and your customers happy.

Treat your supplier like a business partner. Pay on time, every time. Communicate early if you will be late with a payment or if you need to change an order. Refer other buyers to them when they do a good job. Suppliers remember the customers who pay promptly and bring them business, and they give those customers the best prices, the first pick of new stock, and credit when it matters.

Keep more than one supplier for your most important products. If your only supplier runs out of stock, raises prices suddenly, or travels, your business stops. Having a second supplier — even one you buy from less often — gives you a backup and also gives you a price comparison to use in negotiation.

Keep records of every supplier: their name, shop address, phone number, the products you buy, the prices you paid, and how each order went. A simple notebook or a spreadsheet on your phone is enough. Over time, this record shows you which suppliers are getting more expensive, which ones are reliable, and where you can save money by switching.

## Delivery, Transport, and Hidden Costs

The price in the market is not the final cost of your goods. You must add transport, and in Lagos, transport can change your profit completely. A carton of shoes that costs ₦50,000 in Balogun can cost ₦55,000 by the time you pay for a bus, a keke, and a dispatch rider to Lekki. Always calculate the landed cost — the market price plus transport, plus any market levies, plus packaging — before you decide whether a deal is profitable.

For large orders, ask the supplier whether delivery is included or can be arranged. Many big wholesalers have their own delivery vans or work with trusted transporters, and their delivery price is often cheaper than hiring a vehicle yourself. For smaller orders, use a dispatch rider service you trust, and agree the delivery fee with the rider before they pick up the goods.

If you are buying from a market far from your shop, consider the time cost too. A trip to Balogun from Ajah can take three hours in traffic. Sometimes paying a trusted agent or a runner a small fee to buy on your behalf is cheaper than closing your shop for half a day. But only use an agent you know well, and always confirm the prices and quantities with the supplier directly.

Watch out for hidden costs: market levies, loading fees, and the cost of replacing damaged goods. Build a small margin into your pricing for breakage and loss, because in any bulk purchase, a small percentage of items will be damaged, faulty, or unsellable. If you price as if every single item will sell perfectly, your profit will disappear.

## Common Mistakes When Sourcing in Lagos

The first mistake is buying on credit before you have built trust. Some suppliers offer goods on credit to new customers, then use the debt to control your prices. Until you have traded with a supplier for several months, pay cash or transfer, and keep your independence.

The second mistake is buying what is cheap instead of what your customers want. A bale of clothes at a very low price is not a bargain if the styles do not sell in your area. Buy what your customers ask for, not what the supplier is pushing.

The third mistake is failing to check for fakes. Counterfeit products are common in Lagos markets, especially for electronics, perfumes, and branded fashion. Learn the signs of a genuine product — the packaging, the serial number, the weight, the smell — and buy from suppliers whose goods you have tested and confirmed.

The fourth mistake is putting all your money into one product from one supplier. Spread your risk. Buy a range of products, keep a cash reserve for emergencies, and never spend your entire capital on a single order, no matter how good the deal looks.

The fifth mistake is not keeping records. If you do not write down what you bought, at what price, and from whom, you cannot tell whether your business is making a profit or not. You also cannot negotiate properly, because you will not remember what you paid last time.

## Your Sourcing Checklist

Before you place any order, run through this list. Do you know your target buying price and your minimum order? Have you compared prices in at least three shops or from three suppliers? Have you visited the supplier's shop or verified their business name and bank account? Have you placed a small test order first? Have you inspected the goods and checked the quality? Have you calculated the landed cost, including transport? And do you have a backup supplier for this product? If the answer to any of these is no, slow down and fix it before you pay. The supplier who is genuine today will still be genuine tomorrow, and the money you save by checking is real profit in your pocket.
