Your video call freezes. A page takes ten seconds to load. A file upload fails halfway. You restart your phone, and nothing changes. Slow internet is one of the most frustrating problems in a modern business, because everything depends on it — and it is never clear whether the problem is your Wi-Fi, your internet provider, or something else entirely.

## How to Test Your Internet Speed

Testing your speed takes less than a minute, and it tells you whether the problem is real and how bad it is. Two free tools do the job well:

- **fast.com** — run by Netflix, very simple. Open the site and it starts testing immediately. It shows your download speed in big numbers.
- **speedtest.net** — run by Ookla. It shows download speed, upload speed, and ping, and it lets you choose a test server.

Before you test, close apps that use the internet — video streaming, downloads, and cloud backups — because they will steal speed from your test and give you a lower number than your connection can really do. If you can, stand in the same room as your router for the first test, so you are testing your internet, not your Wi-Fi signal through two walls.

Run the test two or three times and take the average. A single test can be thrown off by a brief glitch. Also test at different times of day. Internet in many Nigerian neighbourhoods is slower in the evening, when everyone is at home streaming video, than it is in the morning.

## What the Numbers Mean

A speed test gives you three numbers. Here is what each one means in plain English.

**Download speed, measured in Mbps (megabits per second).** This is how fast data comes to you — how quickly pages load, videos play, and files download. This is the number that matters most for everyday use. A higher number is better.

**Upload speed, also in Mbps.** This is how fast data goes from you to the internet — how quickly your files upload, your video appears on a video call, and your photos back up to the cloud. Upload speed is usually much lower than download speed on home internet, and that is normal.

**Ping, measured in ms (milliseconds).** This is how quickly your device gets a response when it asks for something. Low ping is better. High ping makes video calls lag and online games stutter, even when your download speed looks fine. Anything under 50 ms is good. Over 100 ms, you will notice delays.

You do not need a huge number to work well. Browsing and email work fine on a few Mbps. A video call needs only a few Mbps in each direction. Streaming video in high quality needs more, and several people using the internet at once need more still. The right question is not "is my speed high?" but "is my speed enough for what I do, and is it stable?"

## The Quick Fixes: Try These First

Most slow Wi-Fi is fixed by one of these. Work through them in order.

### 1. Restart your router

Switch the router off, wait thirty seconds, and switch it back on. This clears temporary glitches and gives the router a fresh connection to your internet provider. It fixes more problems than any other single step, and it costs nothing. Do this first, every time.

### 2. Move closer to the router

Wi-Fi signal gets weaker through walls, floors, and metal objects. If you are two rooms away from the router, your speed can drop sharply. Move closer and test again. If the speed jumps up, the problem is distance, not your internet.

### 3. Move the router itself

A router hidden in a corner, behind a television, or inside a cabinet gives a poor signal. Put it in a central spot, out in the open, at about chest height — on a shelf, not on the floor. Keep it away from thick walls, mirrors, and large metal objects. A router in the middle of your space serves the whole space. A router in one corner serves one corner.

### 4. Check how many devices are connected

Every phone, laptop, television, and smart device on your Wi-Fi shares the same speed. Ten devices streaming, downloading, and backing up at once will make everything slow. Check what is connected: a smart TV updating itself, a phone backing up photos, a laptop downloading a large update — any of these can eat your speed quietly. Pause the big downloads and test again.

### 5. Switch between 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz

Most modern routers broadcast two Wi-Fi networks: 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz. The 5 GHz network is faster but reaches a shorter distance. The 2.4 GHz network is slower but reaches further through walls. If you are close to the router, connect to 5 GHz for the best speed. If you are far away, 2.4 GHz may actually work better. Try both and see which is faster where you sit.

### 6. Check for background updates

Your phone and laptop download updates in the background without asking you. A Windows update, an app update, or a cloud photo backup can use your full connection for a long time. On your phone, check your data usage to see which app is using the most. On your computer, pause updates while you work, and let them run overnight instead.

## Is It Your Wi-Fi or Your Internet Provider?

If the quick fixes do not help, you need to find out where the problem actually is. Here is how:

**Test with a cable.** If your laptop has an ethernet port, plug it directly into the router with a cable and test again. If the speed is good on the cable but bad on Wi-Fi, the problem is your Wi-Fi, not your internet. If it is slow on the cable too, the problem is your internet connection or your provider.

**Test on mobile data.** Turn off your Wi-Fi and test your speed on mobile data. If mobile data is fast and your Wi-Fi is slow, the problem is your Wi-Fi or your provider. If both are slow, the problem may be your device or the area.

**Test at different times.** If your internet is fast in the morning and slow in the evening, the network in your area is congested at peak hours. That is a provider problem, and there is little you can do except complain to the provider or consider a different one.

## When to Call Your Provider — and What to Say

Call your provider when your speed is consistently far below what you pay for, when the connection drops often, or when a cable test shows the problem is on their side. Before you call, write down your test results: the download and upload speeds, the time of day, and whether you tested on Wi-Fi or with a cable.

Say exactly what you found: "I pay for 20 Mbps, I tested with a cable at 9 am and got 3 Mbps." Specific numbers get a faster response than "my internet is slow". Ask them to check your line, and ask for a reference number for the complaint.

## When the Router Itself Is the Problem

Routers wear out. A router that is five or more years old may not keep up with a modern connection, especially if you have many devices. Signs that the router is the problem include: the Wi-Fi drops even when you are close to it, the router feels very hot, it needs restarting every day, or a new phone gets good speed on the same network while an old device struggles.

If you rent your router from your provider, ask them to replace it. If you bought it yourself, a modern dual-band router is a worthwhile investment for a business that depends on the internet. You do not need the most expensive model — a reliable mid-range router from a known brand is enough for most homes and small offices.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What speed do I actually need?

It depends on what you do. Browsing, email, and messaging work on a few Mbps. Video calls need a few Mbps in both directions. Streaming video in high quality needs more, and a household where several people stream at once needs more still. The number that matters is not the maximum speed, but whether your connection stays steady while you work.

### Why is my Wi-Fi fast in the morning and slow at night?

Evening is peak time. Everyone in your area is home, streaming video, making calls, and downloading. Networks slow down when many people use them at once. If the evening slowdown is severe, it is a provider capacity problem in your area, and you should report it.

### Is 5 GHz always better than 2.4 GHz?

No. 5 GHz is faster but has a shorter range and passes through walls poorly. 2.4 GHz is slower but reaches further. Close to the router, use 5 GHz. Far from the router, 2.4 GHz may actually be faster. Try both where you normally sit.

### Should I restart my router every day?

You do not need to restart it daily, but restarting it once a week, or whenever things feel slow, is good practice. If you have to restart it every day for the internet to work, the router is probably failing and should be replaced.

## Work Through the List, Then Decide

Slow Wi-Fi is annoying, but it is usually fixable. Test your speed, restart your router, move closer, check what is using your connection, and try the other frequency band. Most problems are solved by one of those steps. If the speed is still bad on a cable, the problem is your provider, and a call with specific numbers is your next move.
