Most website projects that go wrong did not fail at the building stage. They failed at the planning stage — when decisions were skipped, assumptions went unspoken, and scope kept drifting until the budget broke.

The good news is that planning is free. You can save yourself weeks of confusion and a large chunk of money by thinking through a few things before you contact a developer. This guide walks through the planning process we wish every client did before their first call with us.

## Start With the Goal, Not the Design

The first question is not "what colours do I like?" It is "what is this website for?" Every page and every feature should trace back to a clear goal.

Ask yourself:

- What is the main action I want visitors to take?
- Who are these visitors, and what are they trying to achieve?
- What am I doing today that the website should make easier or faster?
- How will I know the website is working — what will I measure?

A website with a clear goal — get bookings, generate enquiries, sell products — is far easier to design well. A website built around aesthetics without a goal looks nice and delivers nothing.

## Know What the Website Must Do

Scope is the difference between a smooth project and a nightmare. Write down every feature you need, then mark each one as "must have," "should have," or "nice to have."

- **Must have:** essential to the goal, the project is incomplete without them
- **Should have:** important but the website works without them at launch
- **Nice to have:** could add later without rework

Developers price projects on scope. When you are clear about your tiers, you can decide realistically what fits your budget and what waits for phase two. This is exactly the approach we recommend in our [app discovery calls guide](/blog/app-discovery-calls-guide), and it applies just as much to websites.

## Map Out the Pages and Content

List every page you expect to need — home, about, services, portfolio, contact, and so on. Then think about what content each page will hold.

Content is the most underestimated part of a website project. Copywriting, images, videos, and product descriptions take real time. If you leave content until the last week, the launch date will slip.

Gather before you build:

- Text for every page, written in your own words
- Professional photos or approved stock images
- Logos, certifications, and client testimonials
- Pricing and service descriptions that are accurate

Our [copywriting for business](/blog/copywriting-for-business) guide can help if writing feels like the hardest part.

## Set a Realistic Budget

Decide your budget range before you shop for developers. A clear budget lets developers tell you honestly what they can deliver for that amount, rather than quoting the lowest number to win the job.

Remember that a website is an investment with running costs:

- **Design and build:** the one-time project cost
- **Domain and hosting:** annual fees
- **Maintenance and updates:** ongoing, whether you do it or outsource it
- **Content and marketing:** images, copy, and promotion after launch

## Choose the Right Partner

Whether you hire a freelancer or an agency, the selection process is the same. Look at their portfolio for work like yours, ask for references, and talk to them before committing.

Red flags to watch for:

- No portfolio or only design mockups without live sites
- Vague answers about cost and timelines
- No discussion of what happens after launch
- Unusually low quotes that ignore your requirements

## Agree on the Process and Timeline

A good project has clear phases. Confirm with your developer how the work will proceed and what you need to provide at each stage:

1. **Discovery:** finalising requirements and scope
2. **Design:** wireframes and visual design for your approval
3. **Development:** building the pages and features
4. **Content and review:** your feedback and revisions
5. **Testing and launch:** checking everything works and going live

Agree on how many revision rounds are included, how changes are priced, and what the launch checklist contains. Written clarity at this stage prevents disputes later. A written timeline with named milestones also helps you track progress without nagging, and it gives both sides a single shared reference when a deadline moves. If the timeline slips, you will see it early enough to adjust the budget or scope before the problem compounds.

## What to Prepare for Your First Developer Meeting

When you finally sit down with a developer or agency, the quality of the conversation decides the quality of the quotes you receive. Here is what to bring so the meeting is productive.

**Your goal and target audience.** Write down the main purpose of the website and describe who it serves. A developer who knows your goal can recommend the right structure and features.

**A list of pages and features.** Even a rough list — home, about, services, contact, a quote form — saves enormous back-and-forth. Mark what is essential versus what can wait.

**Examples you like.** Collect two or three websites in your industry (or outside it) that you admire. Point to what works: the layout, the feel, the booking flow. Examples communicate taste faster than descriptions.

**Your content situation.** Be honest about whether you have copy and images ready or still need help creating them. Developers plan timelines around content, and surprises here cause most delays.

**Your budget range.** You do not have to reveal your exact figure, but sharing a range helps the developer tell you what is achievable. A vague budget produces vague proposals.

**Your questions.** Come with a written list of questions — timelines, ownership, revisions, maintenance, and support. The best developers are happy to answer them, and their answers tell you a lot about how they work.

Preparation like this signals that you are a serious client, and it tends to attract serious, honest developers. The same discipline that plans your project also selects your partner.

**Before you go live, run a launch checklist.** The essentials are simple: the site works on phones, tablets, and computers; all forms send enquiries to the right email; every link works with no broken pages; any payment is tested end to end; analytics is installed so you can measure results; and the site is submitted to Google. A short pre-launch audit catches small problems that would otherwise embarrass you in front of customers.

Website projects succeed when the planning is done before the building starts. Define your goal, scope your features, prepare your content, set a real budget, choose a capable partner, and agree on a clear process.

It takes a few hours of thinking now and saves weeks of confusion later. The best developers will appreciate that you arrived prepared — it makes their job easier and your project better.

A final reminder: planning is not a delay, it is the fastest route to launching. The businesses that skip planning to save a week usually spend an extra month fixing what the plan would have caught. Invest the few hours now, and the build, launch, and first year will all be calmer for it.
