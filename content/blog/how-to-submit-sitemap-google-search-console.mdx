You built a website, it looks great, and you are waiting for customers to find it on Google. Days pass. You search for your business name, and nothing shows up. The problem is often simple: Google does not know your pages exist yet. A sitemap fixes that. It is a file that lists every page on your website and tells Google where to find them.

Submitting your sitemap to Google Search Console takes about five minutes, and it is one of the most useful things you can do for a new website. This guide walks you through it, step by step, with no technical background needed.

## What Is a Sitemap, Really?

A sitemap is a simple file, usually called sitemap.xml, that lives on your website. It lists your pages — your homepage, your services page, your blog posts, your contact page — along with information about when each page was last updated. Search engines read it to discover pages they might otherwise miss, especially new pages and pages that are not linked from your homepage.

A sitemap does not guarantee that Google will show your pages in search results, and it does not push you to the top of the rankings. What it does is make sure Google knows your pages exist, which is the first step before any ranking can happen.

## What Is Google Search Console?

Google Search Console is a free tool from Google that shows you how your website appears in Google search. It tells you which searches bring people to your site, which pages Google has indexed, and whether Google has found any problems with your site, such as pages it cannot read or pages that load with errors.

Every website owner should have it set up. It costs nothing, and it is the most direct line of communication between you and Google about your website. If you have not set it up yet, this guide covers that too, because you cannot submit a sitemap without it.

## Step 1: Create Your Sitemap

Before you can submit a sitemap, you need one. How you get it depends on how your website was built.

If your website is built with WordPress, most SEO plugins create a sitemap automatically. Check for a file at yourwebsite.com/sitemap.xml or yourwebsite.com/sitemap_index.xml. If it loads and shows a list of your pages, your sitemap already exists.

If your website was built with a modern framework or a custom setup, your developer may have generated a sitemap for you, or it may be available at yourwebsite.com/sitemap.xml. Ask your developer if you are not sure.

If you do not have a sitemap at all, you can generate one for free. Whatever method you use, the result should be a sitemap.xml file that is reachable at your website address. Open it in your browser to check — you should see a structured list of your page addresses.

## Step 2: Open Google Search Console

Go to search.google.com/search-console and sign in with a Google account. If you have never used Search Console before, click "Add property" and enter your website address. Google will ask you to verify that you own the website, usually by adding a small piece of code to your site or a DNS record with your domain provider. Verification sounds technical, but the instructions are clear, and most website owners complete it in a few minutes. If your developer manages your site, ask them to verify it for you.

Once verified, you will see your website's dashboard. This is where you will submit the sitemap.

## Step 3: Submit the Sitemap

In the left-hand menu of Search Console, click on "Sitemaps". You will see a box labelled "Add a new sitemap". Type the address of your sitemap file — usually just "sitemap.xml" — into the box and click "Submit".

Google will then fetch your sitemap. The status will first show as "Pending" or "Couldn't fetch" if something is wrong, and then change to "Success" once Google has read it. A successful submission shows the number of pages Google discovered from your sitemap. That number should roughly match the number of pages on your website.

If the status shows an error, the most common causes are a wrong address, a sitemap file that is not reachable, or a sitemap that lists pages that no longer exist. Check the address in your browser first — if the file loads there, the address is right, and the issue is usually temporary. Wait a few hours and check again.

## Step 4: Check That Google Is Indexing Your Pages

Submitting a sitemap is the start, not the end. After a few days, come back to Search Console and look at the "Pages" report under "Indexing". This shows how many of your pages Google has indexed — meaning stored and made eligible to appear in search results — and how many it has not, with reasons.

New websites take time. It is normal for only a few pages to be indexed in the first week, with more appearing over the following weeks. If a page you care about is not indexed after a few weeks, open it in Search Console's URL Inspection tool, which tells you exactly what Google sees when it visits that page and whether anything is blocking it.

Also check the "Performance" report after a few weeks. It shows the searches people used to find your site, how often your pages appeared, and how often people clicked. This is the real feedback loop for your website's visibility, and it only works once Google knows your pages exist.

## Common Sitemap Mistakes to Avoid

- **Submitting a sitemap that lists broken pages.** If a page in your sitemap returns an error or redirects, remove it or fix the page first. A clean sitemap builds trust with Google.
- **Forgetting to update the sitemap.** When you add new pages, make sure your sitemap includes them. Automatic sitemaps handle this for you; manual ones need to be regenerated.
- **Submitting the wrong file.** A sitemap is an XML file, not a list of links on a web page. If you submit a normal page address, Google will reject it.
- **Having no sitemap at all.** Many small business websites have never had a sitemap submitted. If that is you, doing this today puts you ahead of most of your competitors.

## What to Do After Submitting

A submitted sitemap is a foundation, not a finished job. Keep adding useful pages to your website — service pages, blog posts, and answers to the questions your customers actually ask. Every new page gives Google another reason to send people your way, and a good sitemap makes sure Google finds it.

Check Search Console once a month. Look for pages that are not indexed, errors Google has found, and the searches that bring you visitors. Fix what is broken, and write more of what is working.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does it take Google to index my pages after I submit a sitemap?

Usually a few days to a few weeks. New websites take longer than established ones. If a page is still not indexed after a month, use the URL Inspection tool in Search Console to check whether Google can read the page at all.

### Do I need to resubmit my sitemap when I add new pages?

No, if your sitemap updates automatically. Most WordPress SEO plugins and modern frameworks regenerate the sitemap when you publish a new page. If your sitemap is a manual file, regenerate it and upload the new version.

### What if Google Search Console says "Couldn't fetch" my sitemap?

First, open the sitemap address in your browser. If it loads, the file is fine and the problem is usually temporary — try submitting again in a few hours. If it does not load, the address is wrong or the file is not on your server, and you need to fix that first.

### Is a sitemap enough to rank on Google?

No. A sitemap helps Google find your pages, but ranking depends on the quality and usefulness of your content, how fast your site loads, and whether other reputable sites link to you. Think of the sitemap as opening the door — your content still has to earn its place inside.
