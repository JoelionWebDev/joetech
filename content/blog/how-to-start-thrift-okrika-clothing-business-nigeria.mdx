Okrika — second-hand clothing sold in bales — is one of the most popular businesses in Nigeria. From the big markets of Lagos to small towns across the country, millions of Nigerians buy okrika every week because it offers quality clothes at prices far below new ones. A good shirt that costs ₦15,000 new can sell for ₦3,000 as okrika. That value is why the market never dries up.

The business is straightforward: you buy bales of used clothing from importers or wholesalers, open them, sort the clothes by quality and type, and sell them piece by piece at a profit. A single bale can contain hundreds of items, and if you grade well and price well, one bale can return far more than it cost. This guide walks you through choosing your niche, buying bales, grading, pricing, selling and growing.

## Why Okrika Sells So Well in Nigeria

Nigerians love good clothes, and okrika delivers quality that new clothes at the same price often cannot match. Many bales contain clothes from the UK, USA, Canada and China, including strong brands and durable fabrics. A buyer who knows okrika can find a quality jacket, a good pair of jeans or a fine office shirt for a fraction of the boutique price.

The market is also huge and varied. Students buy okrika for campus fashion. Workers buy office wear. Parents buy children's clothes. Traders buy bales and resell. Fashion vendors on Instagram and TikTok buy selected pieces and resell them at higher prices to their followers. Every group is a potential customer for you.

Okrika also fits every budget. You can start with one bale and a small table in a market, or from your home with a phone and a WhatsApp group. There is no expensive machine to buy and no shop you must rent before your first sale. What you need is an eye for quality, honest grading and the patience to sort carefully.

## Step 1: Choose Your Niche

Okrika covers everything from baby clothes to wedding gowns, so choosing a niche is the first smart decision. A niche helps you learn prices faster, build a reputation, and avoid spreading your money across too many bale types.

Men's wear is a strong starting point. Men's shirts, trousers, polo shirts, jeans and jackets sell steadily and are easy to grade. Ladies' wear is bigger but more competitive — dresses, tops, skirts, blouses and jeans all move fast, especially trendy pieces. Children's wear sells quickly because parents buy often as children grow, and the pieces are small and cheap to stock.

Other profitable niches include shoes, handbags and sportswear. Shoes can be very profitable, but they need more capital per bale and more knowledge to grade. For a beginner, clothes are the safest place to start because they are the easiest to understand and the fastest to sell.


## Step 2: Learn Where Bales Come From

Bales arrive in Nigeria from the UK, USA, Canada, China and other countries. They are compressed bundles of used clothing, usually weighing 45kg or 100kg, and they are graded by the factory that packed them. A "first grade" bale contains mostly clean, wearable clothes with little damage. A "second grade" bale contains more worn items and more pieces that need repair or cannot be sold.

The biggest bale markets in Lagos are at Yaba, Katangua in Abule Egba, and parts of Balogun and Oshodi. In other cities, there are wholesale points in Onitsha, Aba, Kano and Ibadan. Many sellers also buy directly from importers who bring containers in through the ports.

Bale prices vary widely by type and grade. A 45kg bale of mixed children's wear might cost ₦120,000 to ₦200,000, while a bale of men's shirts can cost ₦180,000 to ₦350,000. A 100kg bale of ladies' dresses can cost ₦350,000 to ₦700,000 depending on grade and origin. Prices move with the exchange rate, so always confirm the current price before you buy.

Never buy your first bale from a seller you have not checked. Visit the market, ask around, and buy from a dealer with a good reputation. Some dealers let you see the bale opened or show you a sample from the same batch. If a deal looks too cheap to be true, it usually is — the bale may be mostly rags.

## Step 3: Plan Your Startup Costs

You can start an okrika business with as little as ₦150,000 to ₦250,000 if you buy a small bale and sell from home or online. A more comfortable start is ₦400,000 to ₦800,000, which covers one or two good bales, basic equipment and marketing.

Your first bale is the biggest cost. Budget ₦180,000 to ₦350,000 for a good 45kg bale in your chosen niche. Add a steaming iron or pressing iron (₦25,000 to ₦60,000), hangers (₦10,000 to ₦20,000 for a hundred), a clothes rack or two (₦20,000 to ₦50,000), and packaging bags (₦5,000 to ₦15,000). A table and a chair for a market stall cost ₦15,000 to ₦30,000 if you need them.

A phone with a good camera is essential if you plan to sell online — most of your customers will judge your clothes by the photos you post. If your current phone takes poor pictures, budget for a better one or borrow one for your product photos until you can afford your own.

Keep at least ₦30,000 to ₦50,000 as working capital for transport, market levies and small expenses in your first month. If you rent a shop, budget ₦200,000 to ₦600,000 a year in many areas, though many beginners start from home or a market stall and add a shop later.

## Step 4: Buy Your First Bale

Buying well is the skill that decides your profit. A good bale can double your money. A bad bale can eat it. Take your time with this step and never rush a purchase.

Go to the market early in the morning when the fresh bales arrive. Talk to several dealers and compare prices for the same type of bale. Ask which countries the bales came from and what grade they are. UK and USA bales are often considered higher quality for shirts and dresses, while China bales can be cheaper and good for children's wear.

Check the bale before you pay. Look at the packaging: a well-compressed, tightly bound bale with clear factory markings is a better sign than a loose or damaged one. Ask the dealer what other buyers say about that batch. Experienced dealers will tell you honestly if a batch is strong or weak, because they want you to come back.

Start with one bale. Open it at home or in your stall, and count every piece. Note how many are excellent, how many are good, and how many are damaged or unsellable. This count tells you whether the bale was worth the price and whether you should buy from that dealer again. Write it down — your records are how you learn which suppliers are honest.

## Step 5: Grade and Sort Like a Professional

Grading is the heart of the okrika business. When you open a bale, you will find a mix: some pieces look almost new, some are good but slightly worn, and some are stained, torn or faded. Your job is to sort them into grades and price each grade fairly.

First grade pieces are clean, undamaged and fashionable. These are your best sellers and your highest prices — a first-grade men's shirt can sell for ₦4,000 to ₦8,000, and a first-grade ladies' dress for ₦5,000 to ₦12,000, depending on brand and style. Second grade pieces are wearable but have small faults: slight fading, a missing button, a loose hem. Price these lower, from ₦1,500 to ₦4,000, and be honest about the fault.

Third grade pieces are heavily worn, stained or damaged. Some can be repaired — a missing button, a small tear — and sold cheaply. Others are only fit for rags, and you should remove them from your stock entirely. Selling a damaged item without telling the customer destroys trust fast.

Wash and iron your best pieces before you display them. A clean, pressed shirt sells far faster than the same shirt wrinkled and dusty from the bale. This is where a steaming iron earns its money. Hang first-grade pieces on a rack where customers can see them clearly, and fold second-grade pieces neatly on a table.

## Step 6: Set Prices That Sell and Earn

Pricing okrika is a balance. Price too high and pieces sit unsold. Price too low and you give away your profit. Start by checking what similar pieces sell for in your market and online, then set your prices in that range.

A useful rule is to aim for your bale to return at least double what you paid for it. If a bale cost ₦250,000 and contains 200 sellable pieces, your average selling price needs to be above ₦2,500 per piece to double your money after costs. First-grade pieces will sell above the average and second-grade pieces below it, and that balance is normal.

Price by piece, not by weight, when you sell to walk-in customers. A beautiful dress is worth more than a plain T-shirt even if they weigh the same. Learn to recognise brands and quality fabrics — a piece with a known label or a strong fabric can sell for much more than a generic one.

Offer bundle deals to move stock faster. Three T-shirts for ₦5,000 or five children's items for ₦6,000 encourages customers to buy more and clears your second-grade stock. End-of-week discounts on pieces that have been sitting for a while keep your cash flowing and your rack fresh.

Always allow a little room to negotiate, because bargaining is part of Nigerian market culture. Set your asking price slightly above the lowest price you will accept, so a customer who bargains still leaves you a profit. But know your floor price and do not go below it — a sale that loses money is worse than no sale.

## Step 7: Sell Offline in Markets and From Home

Markets are the traditional home of okrika, and they still move the most volume. A stall in a busy market puts your clothes in front of hundreds of buyers every day. Market days — usually the busiest day of the week in each market — are when the most money changes hands, so make sure your stall is full and your best pieces are displayed on those days.

Display matters. Hang your best clothes where people can see them from the walkway. Keep your stall clean and tidy. A customer who has to dig through a messy pile will walk to the next stall. Greet people warmly, let them touch the clothes, and help them find their size without pressure.

If you sell from home, make your space welcoming. A clean room with racks, good light and a mirror is enough. Tell your neighbours, your church or mosque community and your estate group that you sell okrika, and let them come and browse. Home selling works best when your customers trust you and can reach you easily by phone.

## Step 8: Sell Online With WhatsApp and Instagram

Online selling has changed okrika completely. A vendor with a phone can now sell to customers across Nigeria without a market stall. WhatsApp and Instagram are the two platforms that matter most.

On WhatsApp, create a business profile with your shop name, your location and your main products. Post your best pieces on your status every day, with the price and the size written clearly on each photo. Create a broadcast list of customers who have bought from you, and send them new arrivals first — loyal buyers love getting first pick.

On Instagram, open a business account and post clean, well-lit photos of your clothes. Use a plain background and natural light, and show the front and back of each piece. Write the price, size and condition in the caption, and reply to comments and direct messages quickly. Customers buy from vendors who answer fast.

Take good photos. Lay each item flat or hang it against a plain wall, and make sure the picture is bright and sharp. A blurry photo hides the quality of the clothes and makes buyers suspicious. You do not need a professional camera — a phone with a clean lens and good daylight is enough.

For deliveries, use trusted dispatch riders within your city and interstate buses or courier services for other states. Wrap each item neatly, include a small thank-you note, and always confirm the customer's address and phone number before you send anything. For new customers, collect payment before dispatch. For trusted repeat customers, you can agree payment on delivery if it suits you both.

## Step 9: Manage Your Money and Grow

Okrika money is easy to spend and hard to track if you are not careful, so keep records from day one. Write down what each bale cost, how many pieces you sold, and how much you made. At the end of each bale, calculate whether you made a profit and how much. This record tells you which bale types and which suppliers make you money.

Reinvest your profit into the next bale before you spend it on anything else. A business that buys one bale, sells it, and spends all the money has to start again from zero. A business that reinvests grows its stock, its range and its customer base every cycle.

As you grow, add a second niche. If you started with men's wear, add ladies' tops. If you started with children's wear, add shoes. Each new niche brings new customers, but only add one at a time so you can learn its prices and grading before you invest heavily.


You can also train one or two assistants to help with sorting, washing and selling on busy market days. Pay them fairly and teach them your grading standards, because a helper who mixes grades or misprices pieces can quietly damage your business.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

The most expensive mistake is buying a bale without checking the dealer or the batch. A bad bale is the fastest way to lose your capital. Always buy from dealers with a good reputation, and start small with a new supplier until you trust them.

The second mistake is poor grading. Mixing damaged pieces with good ones, or pricing everything the same, confuses customers and leaves money on the table. Grade carefully, price each grade honestly, and remove unsellable pieces from your stock.

The third mistake is neglecting presentation. Wrinkled, dirty clothes on a messy table sell slowly at low prices. Clean, ironed clothes on a neat rack sell fast at better prices. The iron and the hanger are small investments that pay for themselves many times over.

The fourth mistake is selling on credit to people you do not know well. Okrika customers sometimes ask to take clothes and pay later, and a few will never pay. Sell for cash or transfer, and only give credit to customers with a long, proven record of paying you back.

Finally, do not spend your bale money. The cash from today's sales is tomorrow's bale. Keep your business money separate from your personal money, even if it is just a separate envelope or a separate bank account, and review your numbers every week.

## How Much Can You Earn?

A realistic example helps you plan. Suppose you buy a bale for ₦250,000 and it contains 200 sellable pieces. If your average selling price is ₦3,000, your revenue is ₦600,000. After transport, ironing, packaging and market costs of about ₦50,000, your profit is roughly ₦300,000 per bale. A good seller can turn over a bale every one to two weeks, which means a monthly profit of ₦300,000 to ₦600,000 is achievable once you have steady customers — though your first bales will be slower while you learn grading and build your buyer list.

December and festive seasons are the strongest periods for okrika, as people buy new clothes for Christmas, weddings and parties. Stock up ahead of these periods, display your best pieces, and keep enough cash to buy an extra bale when demand spikes.

## Conclusion

An okrika business rewards the seller who buys carefully, grades honestly and presents clothes well. Start with one niche and one bale from a trusted dealer, sort every piece by quality, price fairly, and sell both offline and online with clear photos and honest descriptions. Keep your records, reinvest your profit into the next bale, and grow one category at a time. Nigerians will always need good clothes at fair prices, and the vendor they trust is the one they return to week after week.
