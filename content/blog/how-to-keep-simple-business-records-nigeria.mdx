Ask a typical small business owner in Lagos, Onitsha or Port Harcourt how much profit they made last month, and you will often get a shrug. "Business is moving," they will say. Or: "Money is coming in." But when you ask how much they spent on stock, how much they owe suppliers, or which product makes the most money, they do not know. Their money lives in their head, in their pocket, and in three different bank accounts — and none of it is written down.

That is not a small problem. It is the reason many Nigerian small businesses work hard for years and never grow. Without records, you cannot tell whether you are making a profit or slowly losing money. You cannot prove your income to a bank when you need a loan. You cannot spot theft, waste or a product that is quietly draining you. And when tax time comes, you are guessing.

The good news is that record keeping does not need to be complicated. You need a simple system, used every day, that records four things: money coming in, money going out, what you own, and what you owe. This guide shows you how to build that system in a weekend and keep it up in 10 minutes a day.

## Why Records Save You Money

Good records save money in at least five direct ways.

First, they stop leaks. When you write down every expense, you notice the small costs that add up: the ₦5,000 for transport here, the ₦8,000 for airtime there, the daily lunch that comes from the business account. Many business owners discover they are spending ₦50,000 or more a month on things they never noticed.

Second, they show you your real profit. Sales are not profit. If you sold ₦800,000 worth of goods in a month and spent ₦700,000 on stock, rent, transport and data, your profit is ₦100,000, not ₦800,000. Owners who confuse sales with profit spend money they have not actually made.

Third, they help you price correctly. When you know your true costs — including delivery, packaging, bank charges and the cost of goods that spoil or get damaged — you can set prices that actually leave you a profit. Many vendors in Balogun Market and Computer Village underprice because they forget half their costs.

Fourth, they protect you from theft and mistakes. If your stock records say you should have 40 bags of rice and you have 32, something is wrong. Without a record, you would never know. A simple stock count, done weekly, catches problems while they are small.

Fifth, they open doors. Banks, microfinance institutions, grant programmes and investors all ask for records. A business with six months of clean sales and expense records can get a loan. A business with no records cannot, no matter how busy it looks. The Bank of Industry, Lagos State Employment Trust Fund and many cooperative societies ask for the same thing: show us your books.

## The Four Records Every Small Business Needs

You do not need 20 different books. You need four, and you can keep all of them in one notebook or one spreadsheet.

### 1. The sales record

Every sale, every day. Date, customer name or description, what was sold, quantity, amount, and how it was paid — cash, transfer, or POS. If a customer pays a deposit, record the deposit, and record the balance when it arrives. Never record a sale as complete until the money is in.

### 2. The expense record

Every naira that leaves the business, no matter how small. Date, what it was for, amount, and who received it. Stock purchases, transport, fuel, data, packaging, rent, salaries, repairs, bank charges. If you buy Gala and a bottle of water with business money, write it down. Small expenses are the ones that hide.

### 3. The stock record

What you bought, what you sold, and what is left. For a shop, this is your inventory list. For a service business, it is your materials: a tailor records fabric and thread, a caterer records rice and chicken, a phone repairer records screens and batteries. Update it when stock arrives and when stock is sold.

### 4. The cash and bank record

How much money you have, and where it is. Cash in the drawer, money in each bank account, money in your Moniepoint or Opay account. Record money moving between them. At the end of each day, your cash record should match the cash you actually have. If it does not match, find out why that same day.

These four records answer almost every question that matters: How much did I sell? How much did I spend? What is left? What do I own? What do I owe?

## Choose Your System: Notebook, Spreadsheet or App

Pick the system you will actually use every day. The best system is the one you will not abandon after two weeks.

### The notebook system

A hardcover ledger book, divided into sections with a ruler and a pen. It costs less than ₦3,000, needs no electricity, no data and no phone battery, and it works during a power cut. Many successful traders in Onitsha Main Market have run their businesses on notebooks for decades.

The weakness is that you must add up the numbers yourself, and finding an old sale means flipping pages. Still, for a very small business — a food vendor, a hairdresser, a tailor with a handful of daily sales — a notebook is a perfectly good start.

### The spreadsheet system

A Google Sheet on your phone, free, with one tab for sales, one for expenses, one for stock and one for cash. Google Sheets adds up your totals automatically, and you can search for any sale in seconds. It also backs up to the cloud, so you do not lose everything if your phone is stolen.

This is the best option for most small businesses. Set it up once, and updating it takes a few minutes a day. You can share it with your accountant or your business partner without handing over a physical book.

## Set Up Your System in a Weekend

### Day 1: Gather your starting numbers

Before you record a single new sale, write down where your business stands today. Count your cash. Check each bank balance. Count your stock. List what customers owe you and what you owe suppliers. This is your opening balance — the starting point everything else is measured against. Without it, your later records will never add up.

### Day 2: Build your sheets or your notebook

If you are using Google Sheets, create four tabs: Sales, Expenses, Stock, Cash. Give each tab clear columns. For Sales: Date, Customer, Item, Quantity, Amount, Payment Method, Paid? For Expenses: Date, Description, Category, Amount, Paid To. For Stock: Item, Opening Quantity, Bought, Sold, Remaining. For Cash: Date, Description, Money In, Money Out, Balance.

If you are using a notebook, rule the same columns on the pages. Keep it neat. A messy book is a book you will stop using.

### Day 3: Record one full day, then review

Record every transaction for one full day. At night, add up the day's sales and expenses. Does the cash you have match the cash your record says you should have? If it matches, your system works. If it does not, find the missing amount before it grows. Do this daily check for the first two weeks until it becomes a habit.

## The 10-Minute Daily Routine

Record keeping fails when it becomes a big weekly chore. It succeeds when it is a small daily habit. Here is a routine that takes about 10 minutes:

- **During the day:** Write down each sale and each expense as it happens, on paper or in your phone's notes app. Do not trust your memory at night.
- **Evening, 5 minutes:** Transfer the day's notes into your book or spreadsheet. Add up the day's sales and expenses.
- **Evening, 2 minutes:** Count your cash. Compare it with your record. If it does not match, note the difference and look for the cause.
- **Evening, 3 minutes:** Check tomorrow: Do you need to buy stock? Is a supplier expecting payment? Is a customer expecting delivery?

That is it. Ten minutes. The businesses that keep records are not smarter than the ones that do not. They are simply more consistent.

### The weekly review, 30 minutes every Sunday or Monday

Once a week, sit down and look at the numbers:

- Total sales this week, compared with last week. Up or down, and why?
- Total expenses this week. Anything unusual?
- Your best-selling product or service this week.
- Customers who owe you money, and how long they have owed it.
- Suppliers you owe, and when payment is due.
- Stock that is running low, and stock that is not moving.

This weekly review is where the money is saved. It is where you notice that you spent ₦40,000 on transport this week because you made five separate trips to the market, and decide to combine them into one trip next week. It is where you notice that a product you thought was profitable is actually losing money after delivery costs.

### The monthly close, one hour at month end

At the end of each month, do a proper close:

1. Add up total sales for the month.
2. Add up total expenses for the month, split into stock, rent, salaries, transport, and other costs.
3. Subtract expenses from sales. That is your profit or loss for the month.
4. Count your stock physically and compare it with your stock record.
5. List everyone who owes you and everyone you owe.
6. Write one paragraph: What went well this month? What went wrong? What will I change next month?

## Separate Your Business Money from Your Personal Money

This is the single most important habit in this guide, and the hardest for many Nigerian business owners. Your business money is not your money until you have paid your costs and set aside your profit. When you treat the business account like your personal wallet, you will spend the money meant for restocking on personal needs, and then wonder why you cannot buy stock next week.

Do these three things:

- **Open a separate account for the business.** It can be a Moniepoint, Opay, Kuda or traditional bank business account. All business income goes in there. All business expenses come out of there.
- **Pay yourself a salary.** Decide a fixed amount you take from the business each month, for example ₦100,000 or ₦150,000, on a fixed date. That is your personal money. Everything else stays in the business.
- **Never borrow from the business without recording it.** If you take ₦20,000 from the business for a family emergency, write it down as a personal withdrawal. That way your records stay honest, and you can see how much you are really taking.

This separation is also what makes your business look serious to a bank. A loan officer who sees a clean business account with regular sales and a fixed owner salary is far more likely to approve a loan than one who sees a personal account mixing school fees, food money and customer payments.

## Track Who Owes You — And Chase It

Many Nigerian small businesses fail not because they have no customers, but because their customers owe them too much money. A tailor who sews on credit, a supplier who delivers on credit, a caterer who cooks for an event and is still chasing payment three months later — they are all lending money without interest and without a repayment date.

Keep a simple debtors list: customer name, amount owed, date, and promised payment date. Review it every week. Follow up politely but firmly:

- The day after the promised date, send a friendly reminder on WhatsApp.
- A week later, call them.
- Two weeks later, visit or send a formal message.

Do not be shy about chasing your money. You did the work. You deserve to be paid. And set a credit policy: decide in advance who gets credit and how much. New customers pay upfront or pay a deposit. Only trusted, repeat customers get credit, and only up to a limit you can afford to lose. A credit limit of ₦50,000 for a regular customer is reasonable. Unlimited credit for anyone is not.

Also track what you owe suppliers. Paying suppliers on time keeps your supply chain alive and your reputation clean. A supplier who trusts you will give you stock on credit when you need it most. A supplier you owe and ignore will stop answering your calls.

## Use Your Records to Make Better Decisions

Records are not for the tax office alone. They are for you. Here is what they can tell you:

**Your most profitable product.** Your sales record might show that your cheapest product sells the most, but your expense record shows that it also costs the most to deliver. Your real profit might be in a product you barely promote. Shift your energy to the profitable one.

**Your best customers.** Your records show who buys often, who pays on time, and who spends the most. Spend more time serving those customers, and less time chasing customers who order once and disappear.

**Your wasteful costs.** Your weekly review might show that you spend ₦25,000 a month on data for the business, or ₦60,000 on transport. Can you negotiate a better data plan? Can you combine market trips? Small savings, repeated every month, become big money.

## Common Record-Keeping Mistakes

- **Recording sales but not expenses.** You will think you are profitable when you are not.
- **Mixing personal and business spending.** Your profit figure will be wrong, and you will not know it.
- **Writing records from memory at the end of the week.** You will forget sales, forget expenses, and invent numbers.
- **Keeping records only when a loan is needed.** By then it is too late to reconstruct six months of history accurately.
- **Letting one person handle all the money with no checks.** Even in a family business, somebody else should review the records weekly. Trust, but verify.
- **Throwing away receipts.** Keep your receipts in an envelope or a folder, sorted by month. Photograph them with your phone as a backup. Receipts are your proof if a supplier disputes a payment or a tax officer asks questions.

## What About Tax?

Keeping records makes tax simpler, not harder. Nigeria's tax system for small businesses has become more structured in recent years, and the Federal Inland Revenue Service expects businesses to keep records of income and expenses. Good records let you calculate your tax correctly, claim the expenses you are entitled to, and avoid penalties for guessing.

You do not need to become a tax expert. Keep your records clean, keep your receipts, and visit a tax consultant or an accountant once a year to review your books and file correctly. The cost of that visit is small compared to the cost of a tax problem.

If your business is registered with the Corporate Affairs Commission, your records also support your annual filings. If it is not registered yet, keeping records now will make registration easier later, because you will already know your turnover and your structure.

## Your First Month Action Plan

**Week 1:** Open a separate business account if you do not have one. Buy a ledger book or set up your Google Sheet. Write down your opening balances: cash, bank, stock, debts owed to you, debts you owe.

**Week 2:** Record every sale and every expense, the same day it happens. Count your cash every evening. Fix any difference immediately.

**Week 3:** Do your first weekly review. Look at your best seller, your biggest expense, and your debtors list. Send reminders to anyone who owes you.

**Week 4:** Do your first monthly close. Calculate your profit for the month. Write your one-paragraph summary. Decide your salary for next month and one cost you will cut.

Then repeat. Month after month. The system will feel slow at first, and then it will become automatic, like brushing your teeth. And one day, when a bank asks for your records, or a supplier asks for your order history, or you simply want to know whether your business is truly growing, you will have the answer in front of you — written down, in black and white.

Your business deserves to be measured. Start today, with one sale and one expense, written down properly. That single habit, repeated daily, is what separates the businesses that survive from the ones that grow.
