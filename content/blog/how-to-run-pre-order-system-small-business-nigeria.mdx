# How to Run a Pre-Order System That Brings Cash In Before You Buy Stock

A fashion vendor in Lekki sees a new shoe design trending on Instagram. She orders 40 pairs at ₦18,000 each, spending ₦720,000 of her savings. Only 12 pairs sell. The rest sit in her shop for months, and her money is trapped in boxes she cannot move.

A second vendor sees the same trend. She posts the shoe, collects a ₦10,000 deposit from 35 customers, and uses that ₦350,000 plus a small top-up to order exactly 35 pairs. Every pair is already sold before it arrives. She risks almost nothing.

The difference is a pre-order system. Instead of buying stock and hoping it sells, you sell first and buy only what customers have already paid for. Cash comes in before cash goes out, which is the single biggest advantage a small business in Nigeria can have.

This guide explains how to set up a pre-order system that works in Nigeria: how to price deposits, where to take orders, how to manage the money, and how to deliver on time so customers order again.

## What a Pre-Order System Is

A pre-order is a sale made before the product is in your hands. The customer pays a deposit, or the full price, in advance. You use that money to order, make, or import the goods, then deliver them on an agreed date.

Pre-orders are common in fashion, where a vendor collects orders for a new collection before the tailor starts sewing. They are common in gadgets, where a phone dealer collects deposits for a model that is not yet in the country. They work for food too: a baker takes cake orders for the weekend and buys ingredients only for the cakes already booked.

The system has three parts:

1. **An offer with a clear deadline.** The customer knows what they are ordering, how much the deposit is, and when the order window closes.
2. **A payment and tracking method.** Every deposit is recorded against a name, a product, a size or variant, and a phone number.
3. **A delivery promise you can keep.** The customer knows the expected delivery date, and you have a realistic plan to meet it.

Get those three parts right and pre-orders become a reliable way to run your business. Get any of them wrong and you get complaints, refund requests, and a damaged reputation.

## Why Pre-Orders Work So Well in Nigeria

Nigerian small businesses face a specific cash problem. Stock is expensive, suppliers often require payment upfront, and unsold stock is hard to shift. Many vendors fund their stock from personal savings or from loans, and a bad buying decision can hurt for months.

Pre-orders change the maths:

- **You buy only what is sold.** There is no dead stock, no clearance sale at a loss, and no money trapped on your shelves.
- **Your customers fund your stock.** The deposits you collect pay the supplier. You may only need a small top-up, or none at all.
- **You test demand before spending.** If only five people order a new bag design, you order five bags, not fifty. You learn what sells without paying for the lesson.
- **You can negotiate better prices.** Ordering 35 pairs at once because 35 customers already paid is a stronger position with a supplier than ordering 10 and hoping.
- **You build a waiting list.** Customers who miss one pre-order window will ask to join the next one. That list is a ready-made audience for your next launch.

## Setting Your Deposit and Price

The deposit is the heart of the system. It must be high enough to prove the customer is serious, and low enough that customers will actually pay it.

### How much deposit to ask

A common range is 50 to 70 percent of the selling price. A ₦40,000 dress with a 50 percent deposit means ₦20,000 upfront and ₦20,000 on delivery. A 70 percent deposit means ₦28,000 upfront and ₦12,000 on delivery.

Full payment upfront is also possible, and some vendors prefer it, because it removes the risk of a customer refusing to pay the balance. But full payment asks for more trust, so it can reduce the number of orders. For a first pre-order with a new audience, a 50 percent deposit usually converts better.

Low deposits, such as 20 percent, create a different problem: customers who pay ₦8,000 on a ₦40,000 dress are more likely to disappear when the balance is due, because they have little at stake. A deposit that is too small does not filter out casual buyers.

Set your deposit as a percentage, not a fixed naira amount, when you sell items at different prices. A fixed ₦10,000 deposit works for a ₦20,000 item but not for a ₦150,000 item.

### Price the product before you open orders

Calculate your landed cost before you announce the price. Landed cost means the supplier price, plus shipping, plus clearing or delivery to you, plus packaging. Add your profit margin on top of that. Then announce the price and the deposit together.

Example: a pair of sneakers costs ₦22,000 from your supplier, ₦3,000 shipping per pair, and ₦1,000 packaging. Landed cost is ₦26,000. You sell at ₦38,000, which leaves ₦12,000 profit per pair. The deposit at 50 percent is ₦19,000.

Do not announce a price and work out the cost later. If the cost turns out higher than the price, you either lose money on every order or you go back to customers to ask for more, which destroys trust.

### State the refund terms upfront

Write down what happens to the deposit if the customer cancels, and what happens if you cannot deliver. A fair standard is: the deposit is refundable if you cancel the order or fail to deliver by the promised date plus a short grace period. The deposit is not refundable if the customer changes their mind after you have already ordered their item, unless you can resell it.

Publish these terms wherever you take orders, and send them to each customer before they pay. Clear terms prevent most arguments.

## Where to Take Pre-Orders

You do not need expensive software to run pre-orders. Most Nigerian vendors run them on tools they already use every day.

### WhatsApp Business

WhatsApp is the most common pre-order channel in Nigeria. Post the product in your Status and your catalogue, with the price, the deposit, the order deadline, and the delivery date. Customers message you to order. You reply with the payment details and record the order.

Use WhatsApp Business features to stay organised: labels for "Pre-order Paid," "Balance Due," and "Delivered." Use broadcast lists to send updates to everyone who ordered, but only to customers who have bought from you or asked for updates, so you do not get flagged for spam.

### Instagram

Post the product as a Reel or carousel with the pre-order details in the caption. Use the link in your bio to send customers to your WhatsApp. Save the pre-order post to a highlight called "Pre-Orders" so new visitors can find it. Reply to every comment asking about the price or the deadline, because those comments are also selling the product to everyone else reading them.

### Your website or online store

If you have a website, add a pre-order option at checkout. On WooCommerce or Shopify, you can set a product as pre-order, take a deposit through Paystack or Flutterwave, and collect the customer's size and delivery address in the order form. A store page also lets you publish the terms and the delivery date where every customer can read them before paying.

A simple Google Form also works well for a first pre-order. The form collects name, phone number, product, size, colour, and delivery address, and it gives you a spreadsheet of all orders in one place. Send the payment details after the customer submits the form, and match each payment to a form entry.

### Payment links

Use Paystack or Flutterwave payment links for deposits. Create one link per product, with the deposit amount fixed, so you can see exactly which product each payment belongs to. For balance payments, send a second link when the goods arrive. Payment links also give you a receipt and a record, which is far safer than trying to track deposits from bank transfer alerts alone.

If a customer pays by bank transfer, ask them to send a screenshot of the transfer and their name. Match it to your order list the same day. Do not let unmatched payments pile up.

## Running a Pre-Order Cycle Step by Step

A pre-order cycle has a clear rhythm. Here is one that works for a two-week turnaround, which suits most fashion and gadget orders.

### Day 1 to 2: Announce

Post the product with photos or a video, the price, the deposit, the order deadline, and the expected delivery date. If you have a sample, photograph it. If you do not, use the supplier's photos but say so honestly, and show the exact colour and design customers will receive.

### Day 3 to 7: Collect orders

Keep posting reminders. Answer questions quickly. Record every order in your tracker the moment the deposit is paid. If a customer says they will pay tomorrow, note it, but do not count the order until the money arrives.

### Day 8: Close orders and count

Close the order window on the date you promised. Count the paid orders, by size and colour. This is your buying list. Do not order extra stock on a guess. If you want a few spares for walk-in customers, decide the number in advance and pay for them from your own money, not from deposits.

### Day 9: Order from your supplier

Pay your supplier with the deposits collected, plus your top-up if needed. Get a written confirmation of the order, the price, and the delivery date. If the supplier is in China or Turkey, confirm the shipping method and the tracking number.

### Day 10 to 18: Wait and communicate

This is the period where customers get anxious. Send one update in the middle: "Your orders have been placed with our supplier and are on the way. Delivery is still on track for 28 November." A short update prevents a flood of "where is my order" messages.

### Day 19 to 21: Receive, check, and deliver

When the goods arrive, check every item against your order list: the right size, the right colour, no damage. Message each customer that their item is ready, and collect the balance before dispatch. Ship with a trackable courier and send the tracking number.

### Day 22: Follow up

Message each customer after delivery: "Your order has been delivered. Please let us know you received it in good condition." A happy customer who confirms delivery is a customer who will join your next pre-order, and a short review or a photo of them using the product is the best advert for the next cycle.

## Tracking Orders Without Losing Your Mind

Pre-orders fail when tracking fails. A vendor who loses track of who paid for which size will deliver the wrong items, miss orders, and spend hours sorting out the mess. Use a simple system from the start.

A spreadsheet is enough for most small vendors. Create one sheet per pre-order cycle with these columns: order number, customer name, phone number, product, size or colour, deposit paid, balance due, total paid, delivery address, status, and tracking number.

Give every order a number. Order 001, 002, 003, and so on. Use the number in every message to the customer: "Your order PO-014 is ready for delivery." Numbers remove confusion when two customers have the same name.

Update the sheet the same day money arrives. Do not keep deposits in your head or in a pile of screenshots. At the end of each day during the order window, count the deposits received and compare them with the sheet. If the bank balance and the sheet disagree, find the difference that night, not a week later.

Keep pre-order money separate from your other money. Open a separate bank account, or at least a separate wallet, for pre-order funds. The deposits are not your profit yet. They belong to the orders until the goods are delivered. Mixing them with your daily spending is how vendors end up unable to pay their supplier.

## Handling Delays, Cancellations, and Refunds

Even a good system hits problems. A supplier delays a shipment. A customer cancels. A size is out of stock. How you handle these moments decides whether customers trust you again.

### If the supplier is late

Tell your customers before they ask. A message that says "Our shipment has been delayed by four days because of a customs check. Your new delivery date is 2 December, and we will update you again on 30 November" keeps most customers calm. Silence makes them assume the worst. Offer a refund to anyone who cannot wait, and honour it quickly.

### If a customer cancels

Follow the terms you published. If you have not yet ordered their item, refund the deposit in full. If you have already ordered it, explain that the deposit covered their item, and offer to resell it and refund them when it sells, or offer a swap for another product. Be fair and be consistent. Making exceptions for one customer and refusing another will be noticed.

### If the wrong item arrives

If the supplier sends the wrong colour or size, tell the customer immediately and offer the options: wait for a replacement, take a different colour at a discount, or get a full refund. Never deliver the wrong item and hope the customer will not notice.

### If a customer refuses to pay the balance

Remind them politely, twice, a few days apart. If they still do not respond, tell them their item will be offered to the next person on the waiting list after a stated date, and their deposit will be handled according to your published terms. Keep the messages calm and factual. A waiting list is valuable here: a refused item is not dead stock, it is an item you can sell to the next person.

## Common Pre-Order Mistakes to Avoid

**Promising a date you cannot control.** Adding no buffer to your supplier's delivery time is the most common mistake. If your supplier says 10 days, promise 14. Customers forgive a delivery that arrives early. They do not forgive one that arrives late.

**Taking orders without a deadline.** An open-ended pre-order never closes, so you never know how many to order. Always set a closing date, and stick to it.

**Spending the deposits.** Deposits are customer money held for their orders. Spending them on rent, another product, or personal costs is how pre-order businesses collapse. Keep the money separate and untouched until the supplier is paid.

**Poor communication.** The most common complaint about pre-order vendors is silence. Customers pay and hear nothing for two weeks. One update in the middle of the wait and one message when the goods arrive prevents most complaints.

**No written terms.** Verbal agreements on WhatsApp voice notes are hard to prove and easy to misunderstand. Write the price, deposit, deadline, delivery date, and refund terms in text, and send them to every customer before they pay.

**Ordering extras on a guess.** A few spares are sensible. Doubling your order because you think more people might want it is how you end up back where you started, with unsold stock.

## Start With One Product This Week

You do not need a new website, a new app, or a big budget to start. Pick one product your customers already ask about. Set a price and a deposit. Choose a closing date seven days from now and a delivery date a week after that. Post it on your WhatsApp Status and your Instagram, with the terms written out clearly. Record every deposit in a spreadsheet the day it arrives.

Run that one cycle well, deliver on time, and message every customer after delivery. Then look at the numbers and decide what to run next. The vendors who succeed with pre-orders are not the ones with the biggest audience. They are the ones who keep their promises, communicate clearly, and never spend money that belongs to an order.

Start small, deliver on time, and let the system grow with your reputation.
