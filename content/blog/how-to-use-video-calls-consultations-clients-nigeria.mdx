# How to Use Video Calls for Client Consultations and Close More Deals

A prospective client in Abuja wants a new website for her restaurant. You are in Lagos. In the past, that distance meant days of WhatsApp messages, a missed phone call, and a deal that slowly went cold. Today, you can send her a link, meet her face to face on video in thirty seconds, show her examples on your screen, answer her questions live, and agree a price before the call ends.

That is the opportunity video calls offer Nigerian service businesses. Whether you are a web designer, consultant, coach, lawyer, accountant, tailor, interior designer, or real estate agent, a video consultation lets you do what a text message cannot: build trust, read the client's reactions, and close the deal in one conversation.

Yet many small businesses in Nigeria still treat video calls as an afterthought — a shaky connection, a noisy background, no agenda, and no follow-up. The result is a call that wastes everyone's time and a client who quietly chooses a competitor who looked more professional.

This guide shows you how to run video consultations properly: which tool to use, how to prepare, how to handle Nigeria's network and power realities, and how to turn a good call into a paying client.

## Why Video Calls Win Clients

Text and voice calls have their place, but video adds something neither can match: presence. When a client can see your face, your office, and your work, the distance between you shrinks. That matters in Nigeria, where trust is the biggest barrier to buying a service from someone you have never met.

First, video builds trust faster. A client who has seen you, heard your voice, and watched you explain their project feels they know you. That feeling is hard to create over WhatsApp alone. A thirty-minute video call can do more for trust than a week of messages.

Second, video lets you show, not just tell. A web designer can share a screen and walk a client through a demo. An interior designer can show mood boards and material samples. A real estate agent can show photos and videos of a property while the client asks questions. A consultant can sketch a plan on screen. Showing your work live is far more persuasive than sending a PDF and hoping the client opens it.

Third, video saves time and money for both sides. A client in Port Harcourt does not need to travel to Lagos for a first meeting. You do not need to spend hours in traffic for a consultation that may not lead to a sale. You can hold four consultations in a morning from your desk, each one focused and on time.

Fourth, video helps you qualify clients. In ten minutes on video, you can tell whether a client is serious, whether their budget is realistic, and whether you actually want the job. That is much harder to judge from a short WhatsApp message that says "How much is a website?"

Finally, video calls make you look professional. A business that offers a proper consultation link, starts on time, and follows up with a summary stands out from competitors who only chat on WhatsApp. In a crowded market, professionalism is a competitive advantage.

## Choosing the Right Tool: Google Meet vs Zoom vs WhatsApp

You do not need expensive software. Three free tools cover almost every consultation need in Nigeria. Pick the one your clients already use, and learn it well.

**Google Meet** is the simplest choice for most small businesses. It is free, it runs in a browser with no download, and most clients already have a Google account. You create a meeting link, send it on WhatsApp, and the client taps it to join. Meet works well on phones and laptops, lets you share your screen, and records the call if you need it. If you use Gmail and Google Calendar, Meet fits naturally into your workflow: create a calendar event, add the Meet link, and the client gets a reminder automatically.

**Zoom** is the best choice when you need more control. Its free plan allows meetings of up to 40 minutes for groups, and one-to-one meetings can run longer. Zoom offers breakout rooms, polls, a whiteboard, and stronger recording options. It is widely known in Nigeria, especially among corporate clients, and many clients already have the app installed. The downside is that a client who does not have Zoom must download it, which can be a barrier for a first-time caller on a slow connection.

**WhatsApp video calls** are the easiest option of all, because almost every Nigerian client already uses WhatsApp daily. For a quick first chat or a follow-up, WhatsApp video is perfect: no link, no app to install, no learning curve. The limitation is that WhatsApp is poor for screen sharing, for calls with several people, and for anything that needs to look formal. Use it for informal check-ins, not for a full consultation where you need to present work.

Choose one main tool, put its link in your booking process, and use WhatsApp video as a backup when the main tool fails or the client prefers it.

When choosing, ask three questions: Can my clients join without installing anything? Does it work well on a phone, since most Nigerian clients will join from a phone? And can I share my screen easily? Google Meet scores well on all three, which is why it is a good default for most service businesses.

## Setting Up Your Video Call Space

You do not need a studio. You need a quiet corner, good light, and a stable connection. Small details make a big difference to how professional you look.

**Find a quiet spot.** Choose a room or corner where you will not be interrupted. Tell family or colleagues you are on a call. Close the door. Turn off the television and put your phone on silent, except for the device you are calling on. Background noise — a generator, a blender, children playing — distracts the client and makes you look unprepared. If your home is noisy, consider a small co-working space in Lekki or Ikeja for important calls, or schedule calls for a quiet time of day.

**Get the light right.** Sit facing a window or a lamp so light falls on your face, not behind you. If the light is behind you, your face will be dark and the client will struggle to see you. A simple ring light, which costs from about ₦8,000 in Lagos markets, makes a visible difference. Test your lighting with a friend before an important call.

**Mind your background.** The client will see whatever is behind you. A plain wall, a tidy shelf, or a branded banner is fine. A messy bed, a pile of clothes, or a busy street through an open door is not. If you cannot tidy the background, use the blur feature in Google Meet or Zoom. It is one click and it instantly looks more professional.

**Check your camera and sound.** Use a laptop where possible, because the camera is steadier and screen sharing is easier. If you use a phone, prop it up at eye level — do not hold it in your hand, and do not place it below your chin. Test your microphone with a short call to a colleague. A basic earphone with a microphone, from about ₦5,000, improves sound far more than shouting into a laptop.

**Prepare for power and network failure.** This is Nigeria, and you must plan for it. Keep your phone charged and your laptop plugged in. Have data on both your phone and a second network if you can — for example, MTN as your main connection and Airtel as backup. If your Wi-Fi fails, switch to your phone hotspot. Tell the client at the start of the call: "If we disconnect, I will call you straight back on WhatsApp." That one sentence removes the awkwardness when a drop happens.

## How to Book and Confirm a Consultation

A consultation that is booked properly is already half successful. A vague "let us talk sometime" leads to missed calls and wasted time.

Offer a clear booking path. The simplest is a Google Calendar booking link, or a Calendly link if you use it, that lets the client pick a time. If that is too much for now, use WhatsApp: offer two or three specific slots, such as "Tuesday 10 am or Wednesday 2 pm," and let the client pick one. Specific times get answers; open-ended invitations do not.

Once a time is agreed, send a confirmation message with everything the client needs: the date and time, the meeting link, how long the call will last, and what they should prepare. For example: "Our consultation is Tuesday 10 am, 30 minutes, on Google Meet: [link]. Please have your current website address and your budget range ready if you can."

Send a reminder one hour before the call, and again ten minutes before if the client is new. Many missed calls happen simply because the client forgot. A polite reminder is not pestering — it is good service.

Ask the client to prepare one thing: their goal. A short question in your confirmation message — "What is the one thing you want this project to achieve?" — focuses the call and shows the client you are serious. It also gives you a head start on preparing.

## Preparing for the Call

Preparation is what separates a professional consultation from a casual chat. Spend fifteen minutes before each call on these steps.

First, review what you already know about the client. Read their WhatsApp messages, their website, or their Instagram page. Note their business, their location, and the problem they want solved. A client who hears you say "I saw your restaurant in Abuja has just launched a new menu" knows you did your homework.

Second, prepare what you will show. Have the tabs, files, or demo links open before the call starts, not during it. If you plan to share your screen, close unrelated tabs and silence notifications. Nothing kills a presentation faster than a personal message popping up on a shared screen.

Third, prepare your questions. Write down five questions you need answered: their goal, their budget range, their timeline, who makes the decision, and what success looks like. Keep the list beside you during the call so you do not forget a key question.

Fourth, prepare your price. Know your rates before the call, and know what is included. A client who asks "How much?" and hears a confident, clear answer trusts you more than one who hears "Let me check and get back to you." If the price depends on details, give a range: "A website like this usually costs between ₦350,000 and ₦550,000, depending on the number of pages and features."

## Running the Call: A Simple Structure That Works

A good consultation follows a simple structure. It keeps the call focused, it respects the client's time, and it leads naturally to a decision.

**Open warmly and confirm the time (2 minutes).** Greet the client by name, thank them for their time, and confirm how long you have: "We have thirty minutes — is that still good for you?" This sets expectations and shows respect for their schedule.

**Listen to their problem (10 minutes).** Ask the client to describe what they need, in their own words, and listen without interrupting. Ask follow-up questions: What is not working now? What have you tried? What would a good result look like? Most clients will tell you everything you need to know if you let them talk. Take notes, and repeat back the key points: "So you need a website that takes orders, because most of your customers order on WhatsApp now." That summary proves you were listening.

**Show your solution (10 minutes).** Now present your answer. Share your screen and show a relevant example, a demo, or a simple plan. Keep it focused on their problem, not on your full portfolio. Three relevant examples beat ten random ones. Explain what you would do for them, step by step, and how long it will take.

**Discuss price and next steps (5 minutes).** State the price clearly, explain what is included, and ask for the decision. Do not be afraid of this moment — the client expects it. A simple close is: "The total is ₦450,000, with 50 percent to start and the balance on delivery. If you are happy, I can send the invoice and we can start this week. Does that work for you?" Then stop talking and let them answer.

**Close with a summary (3 minutes).** Before you end, summarise what was agreed: the scope, the price, the timeline, and the next step. Tell them what you will send and when: "I will send the proposal and invoice on WhatsApp within two hours." End warmly, thank them, and end the call on time. A call that runs twenty minutes over because you kept chatting teaches the client your time is not valuable.

## Handling Bad Network, Power Cuts, and Other Nigerian Realities

No guide to video calls in Nigeria is honest without this section. Network drops, power cuts, and noisy generators are part of doing business here. The goal is not to pretend they do not happen, but to handle them so smoothly that the client barely notices.

If the video freezes, turn your camera off and continue with audio — a clear voice call beats a frozen video. If the audio breaks up, switch to WhatsApp voice as a backup and keep the conversation going. If the call drops completely, call back within a minute, apologise briefly, and continue. Do not spend five minutes apologising; clients respect a quick recovery more than a long excuse.

Keep your calls short. A focused thirty-minute call is less likely to be ruined by a network drop than a rambling ninety-minute one. Short calls also respect the client's data — a video call uses roughly 500 MB to 1 GB per hour, which matters to a client on a limited data plan. Tell them the expected length in advance so they can plan.

If you know your network is weak at a certain time of day, schedule calls for a better time. Many business owners in Lagos find mornings more stable than evenings. Test your connection before important calls with a speed test, and close other apps that use data.

## After the Call: The Follow-Up That Closes the Deal

Most deals are not closed on the call — they are closed in the follow-up. Send a message within two hours of the call, while the conversation is still fresh. Include a short summary of what was agreed, the price, and the next step. Attach the proposal or invoice if you promised it.

A good follow-up message looks like this: "Thank you for your time today. As discussed, we will build a five-page website with online ordering for ₦450,000, delivered in three weeks. I have attached the proposal and the invoice for the 50 percent deposit of ₦225,000. Let me know if you have any questions, and we can start as soon as the deposit is confirmed."

If the client does not reply, follow up once after two days and once after a week. Keep it short and helpful, not pushy: "Good afternoon, just checking if you had a chance to look at the proposal. Happy to answer any questions." After two follow-ups with no reply, leave the door open and move on. Chasing a client five times makes you look desperate.

Record the outcome of every call in a simple log: client name, date, what they wanted, price quoted, and result. After a month, you will see your conversion rate — how many consultations become paying clients — and you can work on improving it.

## Final Word

Video consultations let a small business in Lagos serve a client in Abuja, Enugu, or London without leaving the office. They build trust faster than messages, they let you show your work, and they turn a conversation into a closed deal in a single sitting.

Start simply. Pick Google Meet, set up a quiet corner, and offer a free thirty-minute consultation to your next three enquiries. Prepare your questions, follow the structure in this guide, and send the follow-up within two hours. You will be surprised how many of those calls become paying clients — and how quickly video becomes the most valuable sales tool in your business.
