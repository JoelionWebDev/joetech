You sold the product. The customer paid. And then the delivery went wrong. The rider got lost in Ajah, the food arrived cold, the customer called you five times, and by evening they had posted about it on their WhatsApp status. In Nigeria, delivery is not the last step of a sale — it is the step customers remember most.

For small businesses in Lagos, Abuja and Port Harcourt, dispatch riders are the backbone of daily operations. Almost every business that sells online depends on riders to get products into customers' hands. Yet most small businesses manage their riders with no system at all: a phone call here, a WhatsApp message there, cash handed over with no record, and chaos whenever a rider is sick or a bike breaks down.

This guide shows you how to manage delivery and dispatch riders properly — hiring your own riders or using a logistics company, recruiting and training riders, setting delivery fees and routes, tracking orders, and handling problems when they come up. Whether you do five deliveries a day or fifty, the systems here will save you money, time and customers.

## Your Own Riders or a Logistics Company?

The first decision is the biggest one. There are two main ways to handle delivery, and the right choice depends on your volume, your budget and how much control you need.

**Hiring your own riders** means you employ one or more riders directly, usually with your own bike or theirs. You control their schedule, their uniform and how they treat your customers. This works well if you do at least 10 to 15 deliveries a day in one city, because the cost per delivery drops as volume rises. A full-time rider in Lagos typically earns between ₦80,000 and ₦150,000 a month, depending on whether you provide the bike and fuel or they use their own. If you provide the bike, add fuel, maintenance and repairs to your calculation.

**Using a third-party logistics company** means you hand deliveries to firms like GIG Logistics, Kwik, Fez Delivery, or smaller local dispatch companies. You pay per delivery — typically ₦1,500 to ₦5,000 within Lagos, and ₦5,000 to ₦15,000 for inter-state deliveries, depending on distance, weight and speed. This is the better option when you are starting out, when your delivery volume changes from day to day, or when you deliver to many different states and cannot cover them all yourself.

Many growing businesses use both. They keep one or two in-house riders for urgent deliveries within their neighbourhood — Lekki, Ikeja, Surulere — and use a logistics company for everywhere else. That combination gives you speed where it matters and reach where you need it.

Before you decide, do a simple calculation. Add up what you spent on delivery in the last month and divide it by the number of deliveries. That is your real cost per delivery. If a full-time rider would cost less per delivery than what you pay now, and you have steady volume, hiring starts to make sense.

## How to Recruit a Good Dispatch Rider

A rider represents your business to your customer. They are the only person from your company the customer ever meets in person. A rude rider, a rider who arrives late, or a rider who handles a package carelessly can undo all the good work your product and your marketing did.

When hiring, look beyond whether the person can ride a bike. Check these things before you make an offer:

**A valid rider's permit and knowledge of the area.** The rider should know the major roads and neighbourhoods in your delivery area without depending entirely on Google Maps. Ask them to describe the route from your shop to two or three common destinations. A rider who knows Lekki, Ajah, Ikeja and Surulere by heart will deliver faster and get lost less.

**A working phone and a habit of answering it.** Communication is half the job. Your rider must pick up calls, reply to WhatsApp messages promptly, and call the customer before arriving. Test this during the interview — if they do not answer your call the first time, that is a warning sign.

**Honesty and a clean record.** Riders handle cash and valuable goods every day. Ask for a guarantor — a common practice in Nigeria — and take the person's full details: full name, home address, phone number, and a copy of their ID card or national ID number. Keep these on file. For riders who will carry high-value goods like phones or laptops, consider a simple written agreement that spells out responsibilities for loss or damage.

**A roadworthy bike.** If the rider uses their own bike, inspect it. Check the brakes, tyres, lights and engine condition. A bike that breaks down twice a week will cost you more in missed deliveries than a newer bike would have cost to arrange.

**A trial period.** Give every new rider a one- or two-week trial before confirming them. During the trial, watch three things: do they arrive on time, do they communicate clearly, and do customers complain? A rider who passes the trial on all three is worth keeping.

Where do you find riders? Ask other business owners for referrals — a rider recommended by someone you trust is usually a safer hire than a stranger from a job board. You can also post in local WhatsApp groups, on job sites, or through rider associations in your area. And if you use a logistics company regularly, ask whether any of their riders want full-time work with a single business.

## Setting Delivery Fees That Make Sense

Delivery pricing is where many small businesses quietly lose money. They charge a flat ₦1,000 for delivery everywhere, then discover that a trip from Lekki to Ikorodu costs them ₦4,000 in fuel and rider time. Or they charge too much, and customers abandon their orders at checkout.

Build your delivery fees on distance zones. For example, a Lagos-based business might use three zones:

- **Zone 1 — your immediate area:** Lekki Phase 1, Victoria Island, Ikoyi. Fee: ₦1,500 to ₦2,000. Delivery time: same day, within 2 to 4 hours.
- **Zone 2 — wider Lagos:** Ikeja, Surulere, Yaba, Ajah, Festac. Fee: ₦2,500 to ₦3,500. Delivery time: same day or next day.
- **Zone 3 — far Lagos and outskirts:** Ikorodu, Epe, Badagry, and inter-state. Fee: ₦5,000 and above, or handed to a logistics partner. Delivery time: 1 to 3 days.

Publish these fees where customers can see them — on your Instagram bio link, your WhatsApp catalogue, or your website — before they order. Nothing frustrates a customer more than agreeing to buy, then hearing a delivery fee they did not expect. Some businesses offer free delivery above a certain order value, for example free delivery within Lagos on orders above ₦50,000. That works well because it encourages bigger orders while covering your costs on the average order.

Review your fees every few months. Fuel prices change, and a delivery fee that made sense six months ago may be losing you money today. When you raise fees, tell your regular customers in advance and explain briefly why. Most will understand if the increase is reasonable.

## Planning Routes and Daily Schedules

A rider who criss-crosses Lagos all day wastes fuel, wastes time and delivers late. A rider who follows a planned route can do twice as many deliveries in the same hours. Route planning is the single easiest way to cut your delivery costs.

Here is a simple daily system that works for most small businesses:

**1. Collect all orders by a cut-off time.** For example, orders placed before 12 noon go out the same day; orders after 12 noon go out the next morning. Tell your customers the cut-off time so they know what to expect.

**2. Group deliveries by area.** Sort the day's deliveries into clusters — all the Lekki deliveries together, all the Ikeja deliveries together, all the Surulere deliveries together. A rider should finish one cluster before moving to the next.

**3. Give the rider a written or WhatsApp list.** For each delivery, the rider needs the customer's name, phone number, exact address with a landmark, the item, the amount to collect if it is pay-on-delivery, and the time window. Never send a rider out with only a phone number and "call the customer for the address". That wastes everyone's time.

**4. Set a realistic number of stops.** In Lagos traffic, one rider can realistically complete 8 to 15 deliveries a day, depending on distance. Do not overload them. A rider with 25 stops will rush, make mistakes, and arrive late to the last ten customers.

**5. Build in a buffer for Lagos traffic.** A trip that takes 20 minutes on a Sunday can take 90 minutes on a weekday afternoon. When you promise a delivery time to a customer, add a generous buffer. It is far better to promise 4pm and arrive at 2pm than to promise 1pm and arrive at 4pm.

Use Google Maps to check routes before the rider leaves, and ask the rider to share their live location on WhatsApp during the delivery run so you can answer customer questions accurately.

## Tracking Orders and Keeping Records

If you cannot see where an order is, you cannot fix a problem. Every delivery should be recorded, even if your system is just a notebook or a simple spreadsheet. For each order, record the date, the customer's name, the item, the delivery address, the rider's name, the delivery fee charged, the fee paid to the rider, and whether the delivery was completed, delayed or failed.

That record does three things. First, it settles disputes. Second, it shows you your true delivery costs, so you can price correctly. Third, it shows you patterns — which rider is consistently late, which area has the most failed deliveries, which days are busiest.

For businesses doing more than 15 deliveries a day, a simple spreadsheet quickly becomes too slow. At that point, look at affordable delivery management tools or a logistics company that provides tracking. Even a shared Google Sheet that your rider updates from their phone — marking each delivery as done with the time — is a big step up from memory and guesswork.

## Paying Riders and Handling Cash

Money is where most rider relationships break down. Be clear from the start about how and when the rider is paid, and put it in writing — even a simple one-page agreement signed by both sides.

There are three common payment models in Nigeria:

**Monthly salary.** The rider gets a fixed amount, for example ₦100,000 a month, plus fuel allowance. This works best when you have steady daily volume and you provide the bike. It gives the rider income security and gives you a rider who is available all day.

**Per-delivery payment.** The rider earns a fixed amount per completed delivery, for example ₦1,000 to ₦1,500 per drop within your zone. This works well when volume varies, and it rewards fast, reliable riders. Make sure the rate is fair — a rider who earns too little per delivery will cut corners or take side jobs during your hours.

**Salary plus commission.** A base salary plus a small amount per delivery. This is the model many growing businesses settle on, because it combines security for the rider with an incentive to complete more deliveries.

Whatever model you choose, pay on time. A rider who is paid late will look for other work, and you will be hiring again in a month. Pay weekly or monthly on a fixed day the rider can rely on, and use bank transfer so there is a record.

Cash on delivery needs its own rules. If customers pay the rider in cash, the rider must return the money the same day, with a record of each payment. Count it together and record it. Better still, encourage customers to pay by transfer or with a Paystack or Flutterwave payment link before delivery, so the rider carries no cash at all.

## Training Riders to Represent Your Business

A rider is not just a person on a bike. They are your brand on the road. Spend one day training every new rider. Cover these five things:

**Greeting the customer.** The rider should greet the customer politely, confirm their name, and confirm the order before handing it over. "Good afternoon, Mrs Ade. I have your package from [Business Name]." That is all it takes to make a customer feel respected.

**Handling the package.** Food should be carried upright and kept hot or cold as needed. Fragile items should never be thrown into a box with heavier items. Cakes, in particular, need a flat surface and a slow, careful ride — every cake seller in Lagos has a story about a cake that arrived sideways.

**Calling before arrival.** The rider should call or message the customer 15 to 30 minutes before arriving. Customers are not always at home, and a warning call means the customer can come to the gate or send someone to collect the package. It also prevents the rider from waiting at a gate for 40 minutes.

**Handling problems calmly.** If a customer is angry, the rider should stay polite, apologise, and call you — not argue. Give riders a clear rule: never argue with a customer. Their job is to call the boss and let the boss resolve it.

## Handling Failed Deliveries and Customer Complaints

Deliveries fail. The customer is not at home. The address is wrong. The phone is switched off. Rain floods the road. The bike breaks down. What matters is not that it happened, but how you handle it.

First, have a clear policy and tell customers about it before they order. For example: "If we cannot reach you after two attempts, your order will be returned to our shop and a redelivery fee of ₦1,500 applies." Customers accept rules they knew in advance. They resent rules they discover after a problem.

Second, when a delivery fails, contact the customer immediately. Do not wait for them to call you angry. "Hello, our rider came to your address at 2pm but could not reach you. When would be a good time to come again?" That message turns a complaint into a rescheduled delivery.

Third, keep a record of failed deliveries and look for patterns. If the same area fails again and again, the problem may be bad addresses or a rider who does not call ahead. If one rider has far more failed deliveries than the others, have a conversation with them.

Fourth, when the failure is your fault, apologise and offer something concrete: a free redelivery, a discount on the next order, or a replacement.

## When and How to Scale Your Delivery

You will know it is time to scale when one rider can no longer finish the day's deliveries, when customers start complaining about late deliveries, or when you are turning down orders because you cannot deliver them. Do not wait until the system breaks — scale just before it does.

Scaling can mean hiring a second rider, adding a second bike, or moving high-volume routes to a logistics company. Before you spend money, check your numbers: how many deliveries a day are you doing now, what is your cost per delivery, and which areas generate the most orders? A practical milestone for many Lagos businesses is around 20 to 30 deliveries a day. At that volume, a second rider and a simple shared spreadsheet usually pay for themselves within a month, because fewer late deliveries mean fewer lost customers and fewer refunds.

## A Daily Checklist for Managing Riders

- Confirm the day's orders and the cut-off time with your team each morning.
- Group deliveries by area and give each rider a written list with names, addresses, landmarks and phone numbers.
- Check that each bike is fuelled, the rider has airtime and data, and their phone is charged.
- Tell customers their delivery window and the rider's name before the rider leaves.
- Ask riders to confirm each delivery with a message or photo.
- Collect cash and reconcile it the same day.
- Review any failed or late deliveries before closing, and message affected customers.
- Check the bikes weekly and service them monthly.

Delivery is a system, not a favour you ask a rider to do. Build the system once, and it will carry your business for years. Your customers will remember whether their order arrived on time, in good condition, delivered by someone polite. Get that right, and they will order again — and tell their friends to order too.
