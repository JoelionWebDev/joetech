A client in Lagos asks you for a website, and you reply with a price and a promise to start tomorrow. A week later, they have hired someone else. It is rarely because your price was wrong. It is usually because your proposal did not help them decide.

A proposal is not a price list. It is a document that shows a client you understood their business, you know what to build, and you have a plan for building it. In Nigeria, where many clients have been disappointed by unfinished websites or developers who disappeared mid-project, that reassurance matters as much as the design itself. A clear, professional proposal is often the difference between a polite "we will get back to you" and a signed agreement.

This guide walks through the sections every website proposal should include, and how to write each one in plain language that a busy business owner can understand.

## Start with the client's problem, not your services

Most proposals fail in the first paragraph because they start with the writer, not the client. A long introduction about your agency, your tools, and your years of experience tells the client nothing about their own business. Start instead with what you heard in your first conversation.

Write one short section that restates the client's goal in their own terms. For example: "You run a catering business in Lekki and currently take orders mainly through WhatsApp and Instagram. You want a website where customers can see your menu, place orders, and pay, without you having to answer the same questions all day." When a client reads that and thinks "yes, that is exactly it", they keep reading.

This section also protects you. If you misunderstood the goal, the client will correct it now, before work begins, instead of after you have built the wrong thing. ## Describe exactly what you will build

Vague scope is where most website disputes begin. "A modern website" could mean five pages to you and twenty pages to your client. Your proposal must list what is included, page by page and feature by feature.

A clear scope section might look like this:

- Homepage with a clear headline, services, and contact options
- About page, services pages, and a contact page with a working form
- A WhatsApp button and a Google Map showing your location
- A blog section you can update yourself
- Basic search engine setup so Google can find each page

List what is not included too, or at least state that anything outside the list will be quoted separately. This is not being difficult. It is being honest, and it prevents the painful moment when a client asks for an online store halfway through a project that was priced as a simple brochure site. For more on gathering these details before you quote, read our guide on [how to brief a web designer in Nigeria](/blog/how-to-brief-web-designer-nigeria), which covers the questions that shape a good scope.

## Show the process and timeline in simple steps

Clients worry about two things: how long will this take, and what will you need from me? A simple step-by-step timeline answers both. Avoid promising a delivery date you cannot control, especially when you depend on the client for content such as photos, prices, and text.

A realistic process for a small business website often looks like this:

1. Content gathering: the client sends text, photos, prices, and logo
2. Design: you present the homepage design for feedback
3. Build: you build the remaining pages and features
4. Review: the client tests the site and lists corrections
5. Launch: the site goes live and you hand over the basics

Give a time range for each stage rather than a single fixed day, and say clearly which stage depends on the client. For example, "Design begins within three working days of receiving your content." That one sentence prevents weeks of waiting while each side thinks the other is holding things up.

## Present the price with structure, not just a total

A single total figure invites the client to compare you with the cheapest option they can find. A structured price helps them understand what they are paying for. Break the price into its parts: design, development, content setup, and any first-year costs such as hosting or a domain name, where those apply.

Explain the payment schedule plainly. Many Nigerian web professionals ask for a deposit before work begins and the balance before launch. Whatever your own terms are, write them down: how much is due at each stage, how payment is made, and what happens if the project pauses. Clients respect clear terms far more than they respect a low price followed by surprise charges.

Avoid discounting on the spot in the proposal itself. If you want to offer options, present them as packages instead: a basic package, a standard package, and a fuller package, each with a different scope. That way the client chooses based on what they need, rather than bargaining you down on a single price for an undefined job.

## Add proof, terms, and a clear next step

A proposal should end with confidence, not desperation. A short section on how you work is enough: how you communicate, how many rounds of corrections are included, and what support you offer after launch. You do not need to list every client you have ever worked with. A few sentences about the kinds of businesses you have built for, and what changed for them in practical terms, carry more weight than a long list of logos.

Then include short, fair terms. Cover who owns the website content, who provides the text and photos, what happens if the client delays content, and how long the proposal price remains valid. Keep the language simple. A client who cannot understand your terms will either ignore them or walk away.

Finally, end with one clear next step and a deadline. "To proceed, please confirm this proposal by reply and pay the deposit. This proposal remains valid for fourteen days." A proposal without a next step sits in an inbox. A proposal with a next step becomes a project.

## A proposal checklist before you send

Before you send any proposal, check it against this list:

- Does the first section describe the client's goal in their own words?
- Is every page and feature listed, with exclusions stated?
- Does the timeline say what you need from the client, and when?
- Is the price broken down, with payment stages explained?
- Are corrections, ownership, and support covered in plain terms?
- Is there one clear next step, with a validity period?

If any answer is no, fix that section before sending. A proposal sent too quickly is worse than a proposal sent a day later and done properly, because the first document a client sees from you sets the standard for the whole relationship.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How long should a website proposal be?**

Long enough to remove doubt, and no longer. For most small business websites, two to four pages is plenty. One page is usually too thin to cover scope, timeline, and terms properly, while ten pages suggest a project far more complex than the client asked for.

**Should I include design samples in a proposal?**

A small number of relevant examples helps, especially if they are similar businesses. Avoid sending a long portfolio dump. Choose two or three examples that show the kind of site the client is asking for, and explain briefly what each one does well.

**What if the client says my price is too high?**

Do not panic and do not cut your price immediately. Ask what they were expecting and what they would remove from the scope to reach it. Often the issue is not the price itself but a misunderstanding about what is included. A structured proposal makes that conversation easy, because you can point to the exact items.

**Should a proposal be free?**

In most cases, yes, the proposal itself is free. It is your sales document. What you should not give away for free is the detailed design work, the full content plan, or hours of consultancy before any agreement. Keep the proposal focused on scope and plan, and save the deep work for after the deposit.

## Write proposals that start projects

A winning proposal does a simple job: it proves you listened, it defines exactly what will be built, it explains the price and timeline honestly, and it makes the next step easy. Clients in Nigeria have plenty of people offering to build them a website. What they are really looking for is someone who will finish the job, communicate clearly, and treat their business with care. Your proposal is your first chance to show that.
