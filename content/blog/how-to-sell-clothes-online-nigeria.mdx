Selling clothes online is one of the easiest businesses to start in Nigeria. You do not need a shop, you do not need a lot of capital, and you do not need a degree in fashion. What you need is a phone with a good camera, a WhatsApp or Instagram account, a reliable source of clothes, and the discipline to treat it like a real business. Thousands of Nigerians — students, mothers, young graduates, and side hustlers — already earn a living selling clothes online, from Ankara dresses and abayas to sneakers, vintage shirts, and children's wear.

But many of them also struggle, because selling clothes online looks easy from the outside and is harder than it seems. The photos have to be good, the prices have to be right, the delivery has to work, and the customers have to trust you enough to send money to a stranger on the internet. This guide covers the whole process, step by step: choosing what to sell, finding stock, pricing your clothes, taking photos that sell, choosing where to sell, handling payment and delivery, and turning first-time buyers into customers who come back every month.

## Choose What to Sell — and Who You Are Selling To

The biggest mistake new clothing sellers make is trying to sell everything to everyone. Men's wear, women's wear, children's wear, shoes, bags, and accessories, all at once, with no clear customer in mind. The result is a page that looks confused, and customers who cannot tell what you actually specialise in.

Start with one clear niche and one clear customer. For example: affordable women's dresses for working ladies in Lagos, aged 22 to 35. Or quality men's shirts and trousers for office workers in Abuja. Or trendy streetwear for university students. Or modest abayas and hijabs for Muslim women. Or children's clothes for mothers in Lekki. A narrow niche makes everything easier — your sourcing, your photos, your captions, and your advertising all speak to one person.

Think about what you know and what you can source reliably. If you live near Balogun Market and you know fabrics well, Ankara and ready-to-wear dresses may be your strength. If you have a connection who imports sneakers, focus on footwear. If you are good at styling, thrift clothes — okrika — can be very profitable, because you buy a bale, select the best pieces, wash and iron them, photograph them well, and sell each piece for several times what it cost you.

Also consider the price range your customers can afford. A student in Yaba will not buy a ₦45,000 dress, and a banker in Victoria Island will not trust a ₦3,000 "designer" gown. Match your products to your customers' pockets, and be honest about quality. Selling a ₦6,000 dress as if it were a ₦25,000 designer piece will get you one sale and a bad reputation. Selling it honestly as a good-quality affordable dress will get you a repeat customer.

## Where to Source Clothes in Nigeria

Your profit is decided at the point of buying, not at the point of selling. If you buy well, you can price fairly and still make a good margin. If you buy badly, no amount of marketing will save you.

Balogun Market on Lagos Island is the biggest source for fabrics, ready-to-wear clothes, and fashion accessories in Nigeria. You can buy Ankara, lace, and ready-made dresses in bulk, and the prices reward those who compare across several shops before buying. Tejuosho Market in Yaba is another strong option, often less crowded, with good prices for clothes and fabrics.

For thrift clothes, the main okrika markets are in Yaba, Katangua in Abule Egba, and parts of Aba in Abia State. You buy a bale — a large compressed bundle of second-hand clothes — for anything from ₦80,000 to ₦300,000 depending on the type and grade. First-grade bales cost more but contain better pieces. You open the bale, sort the clothes by quality, wash and iron the good ones, and sell them individually. A single bale can contain 100 to 200 pieces, and the best pieces can sell for ₦5,000 to ₦15,000 each. The risk is that a bale can also contain damaged or unsellable items, so always inspect a bale's grade before you pay, and buy your first bale from a dealer someone you trust recommends.

For new imported clothes, Trade Fair Complex in Ojo and Alaba Market have importers who sell in bulk. You can also order from Turkey or China through agents, but for a small seller, buying from a Lagos importer is usually cheaper and faster than importing a small quantity yourself.

Instagram and WhatsApp wholesalers are another source. Many Lagos wholesalers post their stock on Instagram with prices. This is convenient, but verify any new supplier carefully — visit their shop if you can, start with a small order, and never pay in full before you have seen the goods. If you need a refresher on how to check a supplier, the rules are the same as for any wholesale purchase: real shop, real name, real bank account, small test order first.

When you source, always check the quality before you buy in bulk. Check the stitching, the zips, the fabric, and the sizes. Sizes vary wildly — a "large" from one supplier may be a "medium" from another — so measure the clothes yourself and record the actual measurements. Your customers will thank you for it, and you will get fewer returns.

## How to Price Your Clothes

Pricing is where many online clothing sellers go wrong. They copy a competitor's price, or they price by feeling, and they end up either too expensive to sell or too cheap to make a profit.

Start with your costs. Add up the cost of the item, the cost of transport to get it to you, the cost of washing and ironing if it is thrift, the cost of packaging, and the delivery cost if you offer free delivery. That total is your landed cost. Your selling price must cover that cost and leave you a profit.

A common guide for clothing in Nigeria is to aim for a profit margin of 40 to 60 percent on the landed cost. For example, if a dress costs you ₦5,000 in total, selling it for ₦8,000 to ₦10,000 gives you a healthy margin. For fast-moving items like T-shirts, a lower margin of 30 to 40 percent can work because you sell many of them. For slow-moving, high-value items like a quality abaya, a higher margin of 50 to 70 percent is normal.

Round your prices to figures that feel natural to Nigerian buyers: ₦7,500, ₦10,000, ₦12,500, ₦15,000. Prices like ₦9,847 look strange and make customers suspicious. Always state whether delivery is included or separate, and state it early — customers who discover a delivery fee at the end of an order often cancel.

Do not join the race to the bottom. If you keep cutting your price to beat a competitor, you will eventually be selling at a loss. Compete on quality, on photos, on service, and on trust, not only on price. A customer who buys from you because you are the cheapest will leave you the moment someone cheaper appears. A customer who buys from you because your clothes fit well and your delivery is reliable will stay.

## Taking Photos That Sell Your Clothes

In online fashion, your photos are your shop window. A customer cannot touch the fabric, try the dress on, or see how it falls — they can only see your photo. Good photos sell clothes. Bad photos kill sales, even for beautiful clothes.

You do not need a professional camera. A phone with a good camera, in daylight, is enough. Take your photos near a window or outside in soft daylight — never under a yellow bulb at night, which changes the colour of the fabric and makes the clothes look dull. Use a plain background: a clean white wall, a plain bedsheet, or a simple backdrop. The background should not compete with the clothes.

There are three ways to photograph clothes, and each has its place. Flat lay — laying the item flat on a clean surface and photographing it from above — is fast, cheap, and works well for T-shirts, children's clothes, and accessories. A hanger shot — hanging the item on a plain hanger against a white wall — shows the shape of the dress or shirt clearly. A model shot — a person wearing the clothes — sells best of all, because customers can see how the item looks on a real body. You do not need a professional model. You, a friend, or a family member can model, as long as the photos are clear and the fit is visible.

For every item, take at least three photos: the front, the back, and a close-up of the fabric or an important detail like the zip, the embroidery, or the label. Include a photo that shows the true colour, and say in the caption if the colour may look slightly different on different phone screens.

Be honest in your photos. Do not use heavy filters that change the colour, do not photograph a different item from the one you will send, and do not hide damage. A customer who receives a dress that looks nothing like the photo will demand a refund, leave a bad review, and never buy from you again. A customer who receives exactly what they saw in the photo becomes a repeat buyer.

## Where to Sell: Instagram, WhatsApp, and Marketplaces

Instagram is the biggest fashion marketplace in Nigeria. It is visual, it is where your customers already spend their time, and it is free to start. Set up a business account with a clear name, a bio that says what you sell and where you deliver, and a link to your WhatsApp. Post regularly — at least once a day — and use a mix of product photos, styling videos, customer reviews, and behind-the-scenes content. Use relevant hashtags like #LagosFashion, #AnkaraStyles, or #OkrikaLagos, and reply to comments and direct messages quickly.

WhatsApp Business is where most Nigerian clothing sales actually close. Customers see your clothes on Instagram, then message you on WhatsApp to ask about sizes, negotiate, and pay. Set up a WhatsApp Business catalogue with your products, prices, and sizes, so customers can browse without asking you the same questions fifty times a day. Use the broadcast list and status features to show new arrivals to your existing customers.

Jumia and Konga are also options, especially if you want to reach customers outside your social media following. They handle payment and give you access to a large audience, but they take a commission on each sale and they have rules about product quality and delivery times. They work best once your business is already running and you have stock to spare.

Facebook Marketplace and Facebook groups for fashion are free and can bring local buyers, especially for larger items and for customers who are not on Instagram. TikTok is growing fast for fashion in Nigeria — a short video of you styling an outfit or opening a new bale can reach thousands of people for free.

Do not try to be on every platform at once. Pick one main platform where your customers are, do it well, and add a second one when the first is running smoothly. For most clothing sellers in Nigeria, that means Instagram for discovery and WhatsApp Business for sales.

## Payment, Delivery, and Returns

Payment is the point where trust matters most. Most Nigerian clothing sellers use bank transfer, and customers pay before delivery. This works only if the customer trusts you, and trust is built by consistency — real photos, real reviews, a real phone number, and a history of delivered orders.

Offer Paystack or Flutterwave payment links if you can, because they let customers pay by card or transfer through a secure page, and they make your business look professional. For customers who are nervous about paying in advance, payment on delivery is an option, but use it carefully — agree the terms clearly, use a rider you trust, and be aware that some customers will refuse the item on arrival and you will still pay the rider.

Delivery works the same way as for any online business in Lagos. State the delivery fee early, with the product price. Use reliable dispatch riders, agree the fee before pickup, and package the clothes neatly — a clean nylon bag inside a branded bag or box, with a thank-you note if you can. Good packaging costs little and makes the customer feel they bought from a real brand.

Have a clear returns policy and state it before the sale. For example: "You can exchange an item within 48 hours if the size is wrong, as long as the tag is still on and the item is unworn. No refunds on thrift items." A clear policy protects you from endless arguments and gives honest customers confidence to buy. When a genuine mistake happens — you sent the wrong size or a damaged item — fix it quickly and graciously. A fast, honest exchange turns an unhappy customer into a loyal one.

## Growing from a Side Hustle to a Real Fashion Business

Most online clothing sellers start small — a few dresses, a phone, and a WhatsApp account. Growing means doing the same things, but more systematically.

Keep records from day one. Write down what you bought, what you paid, what you sold, and what you earned. A notebook is enough at first, and a simple spreadsheet on your phone is better. Without records, you cannot tell which products make money and which ones lose money, and you will keep ordering the wrong things.

Reinvest your profit. When you make ₦50,000 profit in a month, do not spend all of it. Put part of it back into stock, part into better packaging, and part into a small advertisement on Instagram or Facebook. Paid ads, even ₦5,000 a week targeted at Lagos or Abuja, can bring in new customers steadily if your page already looks good and your prices are clear.

Build a customer list. Save the name, phone number, and preferences of every customer who buys from you. When new stock arrives, message your past customers first — they are far more likely to buy than strangers, and it costs you nothing. A simple broadcast message with photos of new arrivals, sent once or twice a week, can generate steady sales.

Ask happy customers for photos and reviews. A photo of a real customer wearing your dress is worth more than any advertisement, because it proves that your clothes look good on a real person and that you actually deliver. Post these with the customer's permission, and tag them if they are happy for you to do so.

Finally, think about your brand. Choose a name, use it consistently on Instagram, WhatsApp, and your packaging. A small logo, a consistent colour, and a consistent style of photos make your business look professional and memorable. Customers forget a seller called "Fashion Hub 123" with random photos. They remember a brand with a clear name, a clear style, and clothes that arrive as promised.

Selling clothes online in Nigeria is a real business, and it rewards the sellers who treat it like one. Choose your niche, source carefully, price with your real costs in mind, photograph honestly, sell where your customers are, deliver reliably, and keep records. Do that consistently for six months, and you will have something most sellers never build: a clothing business with repeat customers, a reputation, and a profit you can count on.
