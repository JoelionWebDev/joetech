Your WhatsApp buzzes. A customer wants 200 souvenir bags for a wedding. Another wants 500 lunch packs for a company training. A school wants 300 branded T-shirts. Your heart jumps — this is the biggest order you have ever received — and then your stomach tightens. Can you deliver it on time? Do you have enough money to buy the materials? What if something goes wrong?

Bulk orders are exciting because they bring big money in one sale. They are also risky, because a mistake on a bulk order is a big mistake: a large refund, a damaged reputation, or a loss that wipes out weeks of profit. Many small businesses in Nigeria have lost money on bulk orders not because they lacked skill, but because they had no system.

This guide gives you that system. It covers how to decide whether to accept a bulk order, how to price it, how to collect payment safely, how to plan production, and how to deliver on time — calmly, step by step.

## Step 1: Decide Whether to Accept the Order

Not every bulk order is worth taking. Before you say yes, ask yourself four questions.

First, can you deliver it on time with the people and equipment you have? If a customer wants 500 cakes in three days and you bake alone in a home kitchen, the honest answer is probably no. Saying yes and failing is worse than saying no politely.

Second, do you have, or can you get, the materials you need? Check with your supplier before you promise. If the fabric, the bottles, or the ingredients are not available in the quantity you need, do not promise a delivery date you cannot keep.

Third, will it make a profit? A bulk order with a tiny margin can still lose money if there is waste, breakage, or extra transport costs. Do the maths first — the pricing section below shows you how.

Fourth, is the customer serious? A customer who asks for a quote, a sample, and a clear delivery date is serious. A customer who cannot tell you the exact quantity, the date, or the delivery address may waste your time. Ask for those details in writing before you start work.

If the answer to any of these questions is no, you have options: you can ask for more time, reduce the quantity, suggest a simpler version of the product, or work with another small business to share the order. Saying "I can do 200 pieces by Friday, and the remaining 100 by Monday" is professional. Promising 300 by Friday and delivering 150 late is not.

## Step 2: Get Every Detail in Writing

Bulk orders fail most often because of unclear details. The customer expected red bags, you made maroon. The customer wanted lunch packs by 11am, you delivered at 1pm. The customer thought delivery was included, you charged extra.

Prevent this with a simple written agreement, even on WhatsApp. Before you start, confirm in writing: the exact product, size, colour, and quantity; the price per unit and the total price; the delivery date, time, and address; who pays for delivery; the payment terms; and what happens if the customer changes the order.

For example, a clear message could read: "Order: 200 branded T-shirts, size L, white, with logo on the front. Price: 4,500 naira each, total 900,000 naira. Delivery: Friday 20 November, 10am, to the event hall in Ikeja. Delivery cost: 15,000 naira, paid by the customer. Payment: 60 percent deposit to start, balance on delivery."

Ask the customer to reply "Confirmed" to that message. That short reply protects both of you. If there is a disagreement later, you can show the message. Keep screenshots of the agreement and the payment receipts in one place.

Also ask for a sample approval where possible. If you are making branded items, send a photo or a physical sample of one finished piece and get the customer's approval before you make the other 199. One approved sample can prevent a whole batch of wrong products.

## Step 3: Price Bulk Orders Correctly

Bulk pricing is different from retail pricing. The customer expects a discount because they are buying a lot, but you still need to make a profit. The trick is to give a discount that is fair to the customer and safe for you.

Start with your true cost per unit: materials, labour, packaging, and a share of transport and other costs. Then add your profit margin. For a bulk order, your margin per unit may be smaller than for a single sale, but your total profit is larger because you sell many units at once.

For example, if a single souvenir bag sells for 5,000 naira and costs you 3,000 naira to make, your profit is 2,000 naira per bag. For an order of 200 bags, you might charge 4,500 naira each. Your profit per bag falls to 1,500 naira, but your total profit is 300,000 naira, and you save time because you make the same product 200 times instead of handling 200 separate customers.

Be careful with discounts. A discount of 10 to 20 percent is common for bulk orders in Nigeria. A discount of 40 or 50 percent will usually wipe out your profit, especially if there is waste or breakage. If a customer asks for a very large discount, show them what the price covers — quality materials, careful work, and on-time delivery — and offer a smaller discount or a free extra instead, like ten free pieces on an order of 200.

Watch out for hidden costs in bulk orders. These include extra packaging, extra transport because the goods are heavy or bulky, overtime pay if your staff work late, and the cost of replacing damaged items. Add a small buffer — five to ten percent — to your quote for these surprises.

Never agree a price on the phone and start work the same day. Send the quote in writing, with the total, the deposit, and the delivery date, and wait for the customer to confirm it. A rushed price is almost always a wrong price.

## Step 4: Always Collect a Deposit

This is the most important rule in this guide: never start a bulk order without a deposit.

A deposit protects you in two ways. First, it gives you money to buy materials, so you do not have to use your own savings or borrow money. Second, it shows the customer is serious. A customer who has paid 60 percent is far less likely to cancel or disappear than one who has paid nothing.

A common deposit in Nigeria is 50 to 70 percent of the total price, paid before you start work. For large orders, you can agree to payment in stages: for example, 60 percent to start, 30 percent when production is halfway done and you send photos, and 10 percent on delivery. For new customers, ask for a higher deposit. For trusted repeat customers, you can be more flexible, but still collect something upfront.

Make the deposit terms clear in writing: "Deposit of 540,000 naira (60 percent) to start production. Balance of 360,000 naira on delivery." Also state whether the deposit is refundable if the customer cancels. A fair rule many sellers use is that the deposit is non-refundable once materials have been bought, because those materials cannot be returned.

If a customer refuses to pay any deposit, be very careful. Explain politely why you need it: "We buy materials specifically for your order, so we need a deposit to start." If they still refuse, consider reducing the order to a size you can afford to risk, or politely decline. A customer who will not pay a deposit for a large order is a warning sign.

Keep deposit money separate from your personal money, and use it only for that order until the materials are bought. Do not spend a deposit on other things and then struggle to buy materials.

## Step 5: Plan Production Backwards from the Delivery Date

A bulk order is a project, not just a big sale. Plan it like one, starting from the delivery date and working backwards.

Write down the delivery date and time. Then work backwards: when must the goods be packed, when must production finish, when must materials arrive, and when must you order them? Add a buffer of one or two days for delays, because in Nigeria, a supplier can be late, a machine can break down, or Lagos traffic can delay a delivery.

Break the order into daily targets. If you need 300 items in six days, your target is 50 items a day. Write the daily target on a wall or in your notebook, and check it every evening. If you are behind on day two, you still have time to catch up. If you only discover you are behind on day five, it is too late.

Prepare your materials early. Buy all the materials at once if you can, from the same batch, so colours and quality match. If you buy fabric or ingredients in two different batches, the colour or taste may differ, and the customer will notice.

Check your equipment before you start. If your sewing machine, oven, printer, or generator needs servicing, do it before production starts, not in the middle of it. Keep spare parts or a backup plan ready — for example, a second supplier, or a friend with the same machine who can help if yours breaks down.

If you need extra hands, arrange them early. Hire casual workers, ask a trusted colleague to help, or partner with another small business. Brief them clearly on quality: show them a finished sample and explain what "good" looks like. Check their work regularly, especially at the start, so mistakes are caught early when they are cheap to fix.

## Step 6: Control Quality on Every Batch

When you make 200 of something, a small mistake repeated 200 times becomes a disaster. Quality control must happen during production, not only at the end.

Check the first five items carefully against the approved sample. Are the colours right, the sizes right, the spelling on a branded item correct? If the first five are wrong, stop and fix the problem before you make the next 195.

Then check in batches. Every 20 or 50 items, pick a few at random and inspect them. Look for stains, broken zips, uneven printing, or wrong portions. Keep a small box for rejects — items that do not meet your standard — and do not send them to the customer. It is better to remake ten items than to deliver ten faulty ones and lose a customer.

Keep a record of waste. If you bought materials for 200 items and only 185 are good, you need to know why, so you can order extra materials next time. Many experienced sellers order five to ten percent extra materials for bulk orders, precisely because some waste is normal.

Take photos as you go. Photos of production, packing, and the finished goods are useful for the customer, for your social media, and as proof if there is a dispute later. Send the customer progress photos at agreed points — for example, when half the order is ready — so they feel confident and do not worry.

## Step 7: Pack, Label, and Deliver Carefully

Packing a bulk order well is as important as making it well. Goods that arrive damaged, mixed up, or in the wrong quantities create problems even when the product itself is good.

Count everything twice. Count as you pack, and count again before the goods leave. Write the count on each box or bag: "Box 1 of 4: 50 pieces." That makes it easy for the customer to check on delivery, and easy for you to find a missing box.

Label clearly. Each box should show the customer's name, the contents, and the quantity. If the order has different sizes or colours, pack them separately and label each pack. Do not mix sizes in one bag and expect the customer to sort them out.

Protect the goods in transit. Use strong boxes, tape them well, and cover them if it may rain. For food, use insulated bags and keep hot food separate from cold food. For fragile items, add extra padding. A small amount spent on good packaging saves a large refund later.

Plan delivery in advance. Confirm the delivery address, the contact person, and the phone number the day before. Agree on the delivery time, and allow extra time for Lagos or Abuja traffic. If you use a dispatch rider or a van, book it early, especially for large orders that need a vehicle.

On delivery, ask the customer or their representative to count and check the goods with you, and to sign or confirm on WhatsApp that they received the correct quantity in good condition. That confirmation protects you if someone later claims items were missing.

## Step 8: Follow Up and Learn from Every Bulk Order

After the order is delivered and the balance is paid, the work is not over. A short follow-up can turn a one-time bulk customer into a regular client.

Send a thank-you message the same day or the next day: "Thank you for your order. We hope the event went well. Please let us know if everything was satisfactory." If they are happy, ask politely for a testimonial or a photo of the products in use at the event. A photo of your 200 souvenir bags at a wedding is powerful marketing for your next bulk customer.

Ask what could be better. "Is there anything we should improve for next time?" Customers who order in bulk often have useful suggestions about packaging, timing, or communication, and they will tell you if you ask.

Then review the order for yourself. Write down what you earned, what you spent, how long it took, what went wrong, and what you would do differently. Did you price it right? Did you allow enough time? Did your supplier deliver on time? That one-page review will make your next bulk order easier, faster, and more profitable.

Keep a list of bulk customers, with their names, what they ordered, and when. Contact them before the next season — before Christmas, before wedding season, before school resumption — with a short reminder: "We supplied your souvenirs last year. Would you like to order again this year?" Repeat bulk customers are the most valuable customers a small business can have.

## A Real Example: A Baker and 500 Lunch Packs

A home baker in Ikeja was asked to supply 500 lunch packs for a company training, at 4,000 naira each — a total of 2,000,000 naira. She had never done an order that large.

She asked for a 60 percent deposit, which gave her 1,200,000 naira to buy ingredients and packaging. She ordered all ingredients from one supplier, hired two helpers for three days, and set a daily target of 170 packs. She cooked a sample pack first and sent photos to the client for approval.

On delivery day, she packed the food in labelled boxes of 50, used a van she had booked two days earlier, and arrived 30 minutes early. The client counted the packs with her and paid the balance on the spot. Her profit after all costs was about 450,000 naira, and the company has ordered from her three times since then.

The order worked because she collected a deposit, planned backwards, checked quality in batches, and delivered early. None of those steps required extra money. They required a system.

## Your Bulk Order Checklist

Before you say yes, check that you can deliver on time, that materials are available, and that the order will make a profit. Get every detail in writing and ask the customer to confirm it. Price carefully, with a buffer for surprises. Collect a deposit of 50 to 70 percent before you start. Plan production backwards from the delivery date, with daily targets. Check quality on the first five items and then in batches. Count, label, and pack carefully, and confirm delivery with the customer in writing. Follow up, ask for feedback, and record what you learned.

Bulk orders do not need to be stressful. With a deposit, a plan, and quality checks, a big order becomes what it should be: a big opportunity. Say yes to the right orders, prepare properly, deliver on time, and your small business will grow one large, happy customer at a time.
