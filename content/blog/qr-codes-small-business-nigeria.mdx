A customer walks past your shop in Ikeja, sees a poster for your food business, and keeps walking. Nothing on that poster can answer their questions, take their order, or save your contact. Now imagine the same poster with a small square code in the corner. The customer points their phone at it, and within seconds they are chatting with you on WhatsApp, looking at your menu, or viewing your latest products. That is what a QR code does: it turns a physical moment into a digital one, with no typing and no searching.

QR codes have become ordinary in Nigeria. Customers already scan them to pay, to join a group, or to open a menu. The technology is free to create and costs nothing to print, which makes it one of the cheapest marketing tools a small business can use. The businesses that benefit most are the ones that place codes where customers already stand, and give them a clear reason to scan.

## What a QR code can do for a small business

A QR code is simply a link that a phone camera can read. When a customer scans it, their phone opens whatever you connected to it: a WhatsApp chat, your website, a menu, a Google Map, a payment page, or a form. The customer does not need to type your web address, search for your business name, or save your number first. One scan removes all of that friction.

For a small business, the most useful destinations are the ones that lead to a conversation or an order. A code that opens a WhatsApp chat with a pre-written message, such as "Hello, I would like to order," is more valuable than a code that opens a general homepage, because the customer arrives ready to talk. A code that opens your product catalogue lets a customer browse while they wait. A code that opens your location on Google Maps helps a first-time customer find your shop without calling you for directions.

## Where to place your QR codes

A QR code only works if people can see it and reach it with their phone. Placement matters more than design.

Start with the places customers already look. Your shop counter is one of the best spots, because customers stand there with their phones in their hands while they wait. A small standing card on the counter with a clear instruction, such as "Scan to chat with us on WhatsApp," will get more scans than a code hidden on a back wall.

Your packaging is the second best place. A sticker or a printed code on a shopping bag, a food pack, or a product box travels home with the customer. When they open the product later, the code is still there, inviting them to reorder, review, or follow you. This costs almost nothing to add, and it works long after the customer has left your shop.

Flyers, posters, and banners are the third place. A code on a flyer turns a piece of paper into a link, which matters because most flyers are thrown away within a day. If the customer scans while the flyer is still in their hand, you have kept the connection. Put the code large enough to scan from a normal standing distance, and never place it near the fold or the edge where it can be damaged.

## What to link your QR code to

The destination matters more than the code itself. A customer who scans and lands somewhere useless will not scan your next code. Choose destinations that give them something immediately.

A WhatsApp chat link is the best destination for most Nigerian small businesses, because WhatsApp is where your customers already talk, ask questions, and place orders. Set up the link so it opens a chat with your business number and a short starter message. That way the customer does not have to think about what to type first.

A menu or catalogue is the best destination for food, retail, and service businesses. Link to a page where customers can see your products, prices, and options without needing to ask. If your menu changes often, link to a page you can update, such as your website or your WhatsApp catalogue, rather than a fixed image that becomes outdated.

A Google Maps link is the best destination for businesses that depend on walk-in customers, such as salons, pharmacies, and restaurants. A first-time customer who can tap once and get directions is far more likely to visit than one who has to call and ask for landmarks.

Avoid linking to your general social media profile as the main destination, unless that profile is where you actually take orders. A customer who scans expecting to order and lands on a page of old posts will feel misled, and you will have wasted a good moment.

## How to create a QR code properly

Creating a QR code is free and takes minutes, but a few details decide whether it works well.

First, use a generator that lets you change the destination later. These are called dynamic QR codes. A static code locks in one link forever, so if your WhatsApp number changes or your menu moves, you have to reprint everything. A dynamic code lets you update the link behind it without reprinting a single flyer or sticker. Several free and low-cost generators offer this, and for a small business the ability to change the link is worth far more than any fancy design feature.

Second, always add a short instruction beside the code. A bare code with no explanation gets ignored, because people do not know what they will get. Write one clear line: "Scan to order on WhatsApp," "Scan to see today's menu," or "Scan to pay." Tell them the benefit, not just the action.

Third, test the code before you print it in bulk. Print one copy, scan it with two different phones, and check that it opens the right place quickly. Test it in the light where it will actually be used, including the dim light inside a shop and the bright sunlight outside. A code that works on your desk but fails on your shop window is useless.

Fourth, make the code big enough. A code printed the size of a postage stamp will frustrate everyone who tries to scan it. As a rule, the code should be at least three centimetres wide for a counter card, and much larger on a poster or banner that people read from a distance. Also leave a clear white border around the code, because the scanner needs that quiet space to read it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do customers need a special app to scan a QR code?**

No. Almost every modern phone can scan a QR code with its normal camera. The customer points the camera at the code and taps the link that appears. There is no app to download and no account to create.

**Are QR codes safe for my customers?**

A QR code itself is safe, because it is only a link. The risk is where the link goes, so only print codes you created yourself, and check the destination regularly. Never print a code someone else gave you without checking where it leads.

**How much does it cost to use QR codes?**

Creating a basic QR code is free, and printing it costs no more than the flyer, sticker, or card it goes on. The only cost worth paying for is a dynamic code service that lets you change the destination later, which is usually a small monthly or yearly fee.

**Can I track how many people scan my code?**

Yes, if you use a dynamic QR code service, it will usually show you how many scans each code received and when. Use different codes for different places, so you can compare which placements work best.

## Turn every surface into a doorway

Your shop counter, your packaging, your flyers, and your window are all places where customers already stand and look. A QR code turns each of those places into a doorway to your business online. Place your codes where customers can reach them, link them to something useful like a WhatsApp chat or a menu, and give people a clear reason to scan. Done well, a small printed square can quietly bring you orders, reviews, and repeat customers every single day, without any extra advertising spend.
