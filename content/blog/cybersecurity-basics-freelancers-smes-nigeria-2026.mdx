Tolu is a freelance web designer in Yaba. One laptop, one phone, nine clients. On that laptop sit the admin passwords for four client websites, the brand files for a fashion label in Lekki, the half-finished store of a supermarket chain in Abuja, and three months of unpaid invoices. On Tuesday, she set the laptop down at a cafe in Ikeja GRA to take a call outside, came back ninety seconds later, and the table was empty. The laptop was gone. So was her phone charger, her mouse, and — for a sickening moment — her whole business.

She got the laptop back two days later, through a friend of a friend and a negotiation she would rather forget. But the two days in between taught her something no checklist ever had: a freelancer is not a small business with an IT department. A freelancer is one person carrying other people's businesses on a machine that fits in a backpack.

This guide is written for that person. Not for a company with staff and servers, but for you, with your laptop, your phone, and clients who trust you with the keys to their businesses.

## Your Laptop Is Your Clients' Office

The first shift is mental. The laptop is not just yours. Every client who hands you their Instagram login, their hosting password, or their customer list is trusting you the way they would trust a bank with their money. If your laptop is compromised, it is not only your problem. It is four clients' problem, and they will not all forgive you.

So the first job is to separate your life from your clients' lives on the same machine. If you can, create a separate user account on your laptop just for work. Windows and Mac both let you do this in a few minutes, and it means your personal downloads, your games, your random browsing and your client files never sit in the same space. Inside the work account, keep a folder for each client, named clearly, and keep their passwords out of those folders entirely.

Passwords belong in a password manager, never in a WhatsApp chat, never in an email thread, never in a notes app, and never in a file called "passwords.docx" on your desktop. That file is the first thing anyone looks for on a stolen laptop. Use Bitwarden — it is free — and create a folder for each client inside it. When a client sends you their login details on WhatsApp, move them into the manager and delete the message. When the project ends, tell the client to change their password, and delete it from your vault. A password you no longer hold cannot leak from you.

One designer I know in Surulere kept every client password in a Google Doc titled "Logins". The doc was shared with "anyone with the link" because he once needed to open it on a client's computer. A year later he could not remember who had that link. He changed every password he could think of and spent a weekend apologising to clients. Do not be him.

## Working From Cafes and Co-Working Spaces in Lagos

Freelancers work where the light and the WiFi are good. The Workstation in Victoria Island, a cafe in Yaba, the food court at Ikeja City Mall, a co-working hub in Lekki. All of them share one problem: the WiFi is shared with strangers, and some of those strangers are not there to work.

Public WiFi is risky in a very specific way. On an open network, someone with basic tools can watch traffic that is not encrypted, and can set up a fake network with a believable name — "Cafe_Free_WiFi" or "Workstation_Guest" — that your phone joins automatically and routes everything through them. This is not a film plot. It happens in Lagos cafes the way pickpocketing happens in Oshodi.

The practical rules are simple. First, use your own phone hotspot whenever you are doing anything that involves money or a client's account. Your data is your own network, and it is far safer than a cafe router you know nothing about. Yes, data costs money. A lost client costs more.

Second, when you must use public WiFi, use a VPN. Proton VPN has a free tier that is genuinely usable, and it encrypts your traffic so the person at the next table sees noise instead of your work. Turn it on before you open anything, not after.

Third, never log into your bank app, your PayPal, your Paystack dashboard, or a client's admin panel on public WiFi without a VPN. If you need to send an invoice or check a payment, tether to your phone. It takes ten seconds to switch on the hotspot.

Fourth, turn off the setting that lets your phone and laptop join networks automatically. Your phone remembers every network it has ever joined, and it will happily connect to a fake one with the same name in a different location. Go into your WiFi settings once and tell your devices to forget the networks of cafes you no longer visit.

And a smaller thing that matters: lock your screen every time you stand up. Not in five minutes, now. A laptop left open on a cafe table while you queue for coffee is an invitation. In a co-working space, a cable lock costs about ₦8,000 and stops the casual grab-and-walk that took Tolu's laptop. Set a short screen lock — one minute — and use your fingerprint or face to unlock. The two seconds it costs you are nothing next to two days without your business.

## Your Phone Is the Key to Everything

Your phone is more dangerous to lose than your laptop, because your phone is the second factor for almost everything. Your email, your bank, your WhatsApp, your client's Instagram — all of them send their reset codes to that phone. Whoever holds your phone holds the keys to your recovery.

So treat it that way. A strong lock screen, fingerprint or face, no exceptions. Turn on Find My Device — Find My iPhone or Google's Find Hub — and test it once, from your laptop, so you know it works before you need it. Write down, on paper kept at home, the recovery codes for your email and your password manager. If your phone is gone, those codes are the only way back in.

Keep your SIM safe too. SIM swap fraud is a real problem in Nigeria: someone convinces a network agent they are you, gets a replacement SIM, and starts receiving your bank codes. Go to your network provider and put a SIM replacement restriction on your line if they offer one, and never share the OTP that arrives when you did not request it. A message that says "your verification code is 483920" followed by a stranger calling to ask for that code is a theft in progress.

Back up your phone every night. Photos of your work, client chats, voice notes with instructions — all of it should sync to the cloud automatically. A freelancer in Abuja lost a phone in a taxi and with it the only copy of a client's brand photoshoot, three weeks of work, because she had turned off auto-backup to save data. She redid the shoot at her own cost, around ₦150,000. Cloud backup on home WiFi at night costs nothing.

## When a Device Is Stolen

It will happen to someone you know, and it may happen to you. The difference between a bad day and a ruined month is what you do in the first hour.

First, borrow a phone and lock or erase the stolen device. From any browser, open your Google account or iCloud, find the device, and lock it, then erase it. Do this before anything else. A locked phone is a brick. An unlocked phone is your whole life.

Second, change your email password immediately, from the borrowed phone or a friend's laptop. Your email is the master key: password resets for everything else go there. Once your email is safe, go through your banking apps and your client accounts and revoke sessions — every bank app and most platforms have a "sign out of all devices" option. Use it.

Third, call your network provider and block the SIM. This stops the thief receiving your codes. Then go to the police station nearest to where it happened and report it, with the phone's IMEI number, which you should have written down at home. A report rarely gets the phone back, but it protects you if the phone is later used in a crime, and some insurers require it.

Fourth, tell your clients. This is the hard one, and the one people skip. If the stolen laptop held a client's website password, tell them the same day: "My laptop was stolen, I have changed all your passwords, here are the new ones." Clients forgive honesty. They do not forgive finding out a month later that their Instagram was posting scam links and you knew your laptop was gone.

Tolu's story ended better than most because her laptop had a lock screen, her files were in the cloud, and her client passwords were in a password manager protected by a master password the thief never got. She lost two days and a mouse. She did not lose a single client. The laptop was the thing that was stolen. The business was not in the laptop, because she had put it somewhere safer.

## The Weekly Habit That Holds It All Together

None of this works as a one-time project. It works as a habit, and freelancers are good at habits when they are short.

Every Sunday evening, give it twenty minutes. Check that your backups ran. Look at the devices logged into your email and your WhatsApp and sign out anything strange. Update your phone and laptop. Glance at your password manager and add any new client login from the week. Check that your VPN still works.

That is it. Twenty minutes, once a week, and the freelancer carrying ten clients on one laptop stops being the easiest target in the room. Your clients trust you with their businesses. Make sure that trust is built on something stronger than luck and a laptop bag.
